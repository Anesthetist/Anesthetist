# Obsessive Care Planning at Rush: How Systematic Clinical Thinking Was Forged

## The Practice

During SRNA training at Rush University, Randy developed a maniacal obsession with anesthesia care plans. For at least the first three cases of every day, he wrote care plans of approximately three pages each — not the perfunctory one-paragraph summaries most students produced, but exhaustive deliberations covering every dimension of the case:

- **The type of surgery** — what exactly is happening, what are the surgical stages, what are the critical moments for the anesthesia provider
- **The type of pain** — somatic vs. visceral vs. neuropathic, acute surgical pain vs. anticipated post-operative pain trajectory
- **The options for pain control** — regional vs. systemic, multimodal analgesia strategies, nerve block candidates, opioid-sparing approaches
- **The options for intubation** — airway assessment, anticipated difficulty, backup plans (video laryngoscope, fiberoptic, LMA, surgical airway), patient-specific anatomy considerations
- **The options for medications** — induction agents, maintenance choices, hemodynamic management, reversal agents, antiemetics, adjuncts
- **The options for fluid management** — estimated blood loss, crystalloid vs. colloid, transfusion triggers, goal-directed fluid therapy

For each dimension, Randy didn't just list the options — he **thought through the implications** of that particular surgery for that particular patient. Every comorbidity, every medication interaction, every anatomical variation, every contingency.

## What This Built

### 1. Systematic Clinical Architecture

After doing hundreds of three-page care plans, the systematic approach became internalized. The care plan wasn't on paper anymore — it was running as a background process in every clinical encounter. Randy stopped needing to write it down because the framework had become embodied cognition.

This is the same phenomenon that [[awareness-before-technique]] describes: the intensive deliberate practice period builds a foundation that eventually operates automatically. The care planning obsession was the awareness phase of clinical mastery — forced, slow, deliberate attention to every dimension before the speed of real-time clinical practice could be trusted.

### 2. Decision Tree Fluency

Each three-page care plan was effectively a decision tree with branches for every contingency. After building hundreds of these trees, Randy developed the ability to rapidly navigate decision spaces under pressure — not because he memorized protocols, but because he had *built the branching structures* through repetitive deliberate practice.

This connects directly to [[error-archetypes-qrc]]: the errors Randy later observed on the Quality Review Committee were often failures of decision tree navigation — clinicians who hadn't built the branching structures and therefore couldn't find the right path under time pressure.

### 3. Patient-Specific Thinking

The discipline of writing case-specific care plans — not generic templates but individualized analyses — built the habit of treating every patient as a unique clinical problem. This is the antidote to [[normalization of deviance]]: when you've trained yourself to think specifically about this patient, you're less likely to default to "we always do it this way."

## Connection to Gap Moment Training

The care planning practice was, in retrospect, a pre-case gap moment drill. The time spent writing the plan was a transition window between "I'm a student preparing" and "I'm a clinician responsible for this patient's life." The care plan wasn't just clinical preparation — it was **cognitive and autonomic preparation** for the demands ahead.

The structure maps to the Gap Moment Training framework:

| Care Plan Phase | GMT Equivalent | What It Trains |
|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| Patient review and problem list | The Stability Snap (grounding) | Situational awareness and presence |
| Options analysis for each domain | Cognitive rehearsal | Decision tree pre-loading |
| Contingency planning ("what if...") | The Error Reversal Loop | Error recovery pre-visualization |
| Final mental walkthrough | The Coherent Return (re-entry) | Procedural confidence and readiness |

## Why This Matters for Somnistics

This origin story demonstrates that Randy's clinical methodology was always about **preparation in the transition window** — the time before the case, not just performance during it. Gap Moment Training is the generalization of what the care planning obsession taught: the quality of the transition determines the quality of the performance.

The care plan obsession also explains why the NeuroMinute structure includes a **cognitive-intentional phase** alongside the somatic phase. Pure autonomic regulation isn't enough. The clinician also needs to pre-load the decision architecture for the specific challenge ahead. Iyengar would call this sankalpa — setting an intention. The Blue Angels would call it a pre-flight brief. Randy called it a three-page care plan.
