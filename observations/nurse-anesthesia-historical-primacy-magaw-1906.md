## The Finding

Nurse anesthesia predates physician anesthesiology as a formal specialty in the United States by roughly two generations. The "physician-led care team" frame that dominates modern scope-of-practice rhetoric is a late-arriving apex predator's territorial marking — not a description of historical reality.

## Timeline

- **1863** — Nurses administer anesthesia during the American Civil War. Contemporary accounts describe surgeons preferring nurse anesthetists for their attention to the whole patient and their willingness to devote undivided focus to ether drip.
- **1899–1906** — Alice Magaw publishes a series of papers on open-drop ether at the Mayo Clinic, reporting over **14,000 anesthetics without a reported fatality**. Magaw is widely considered the "Mother of Anesthesia."
- **1920s** — Nurse anesthetists are the dominant anesthesia workforce in the United States.
- **1938** — The American Board of Anesthesiology is established, formally credentialing physician anesthesiology as a medical specialty. This is **two full generations after nurses were already running the workflow.**
- **2026** — The ASA still frames nurse anesthetists as "extenders" in its core messaging. The frame is inverted relative to the historical record.

## Why This Matters

The scope-of-practice fight is often conducted on rhetorical terrain that assumes physician primacy as the historical default and nurse anesthesia as the upstart encroachment. The evidence runs the other way. Practicing CRNAs who know the dates rarely frame the scope fight as evidence of *that inversion* — as a late-arriving specialty's territorial dynamics playing out against an older, more geographically distributed profession.

Reframing the scope fight this way changes the psychology of the argument. It also changes what "deference" means: the historically dominant species does not defer to a newer entrant; it accommodates it.
