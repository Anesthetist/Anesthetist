# Blue Angels Pre-Flight Visualization: Embodied Mental Rehearsal for Clinical Practice

## The Practice

Randy adopted a visualization practice modeled on the Blue Angels pre-flight ritual. In the Blue Angels squadron, before every flight, pilots sit together and physically move through the motions of the routine — turning their heads, moving their hands, tracking each maneuver in their bodies — while still on the ground. They are not thinking about the flight. They are **rehearsing the flight in their bodies.**

Randy applied this same principle to anesthesia practice, beginning in SRNA training at Rush and continuing through 28 years of clinical work:

### Pre-Case Visualization

Before each case, Randy would mentally walk through the entire anesthetic:

- **Drawing up medications** — visualizing the specific syringes, the specific doses for this patient, the sequence of preparation
- **The induction sequence** — step by step, hands on the mask, the feel of ventilation, the view through the laryngoscope, the tube passing through the cords
- **Positioning, monitoring, maintenance** — the full arc of the case from first touch to emergence
- **Emergence and extubation** — the criteria, the sequence, the feel of the patient waking up

This wasn't abstract cognitive review. It was **embodied visualization** — feeling the weight of the laryngoscope, the resistance of the syringe plunger, the rhythm of ventilation. Motor imagery that engages the same neural circuits as actual performance.

### Deliberate Failure Visualization

The deeper practice — and the one that distinguishes this from standard case preparation — was **deliberately visualizing things going wrong.** Using *Anesthesia Emergencies* (Ruskin & Rosenbaum, Oxford) as his source material, Randy would:

1. **Visualize the case proceeding normally** — then introduce a failure
2. **What would it look like?** — the specific visual, auditory, and tactile cues that something is wrong (the monitor alarm pattern, the change in ventilation compliance, the color shift)
3. **What would I notice first?** — training the perceptual recognition system, not just the cognitive response
4. **What would be going wrong physiologically?** — mapping the observable signs to the underlying pathophysiology
5. **What are the options?** — branching the decision tree in real time
6. **What would I do?** — selecting and committing to the intervention
7. **Visualize every step of the response** — including drawing up emergency medications, the specific doses, the physical actions

He would run through these failure scenarios **over and over again**, particularly during downtime — between cases, during slow moments, when bored. The boredom was the training window. The gap moment before the term existed.

## Why This Works: The Neuroscience

### Motor Imagery and Procedural Memory

Motor imagery (mentally rehearsing physical actions) activates the premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and cerebellum — the same circuits used during actual motor execution. This means:

- Visualization builds procedural memory without physical practice
- Emergency responses become partially automated through repeated mental rehearsal
- The "freeze" response under acute stress is less likely because the motor sequences are pre-loaded

### Perceptual Priming

Visualizing what failure *looks like* — the specific monitor patterns, the tactile changes, the auditory cues — trains the perceptual recognition system. This is [[neurogating]] in its proto-form: the nervous system learns to detect threat cues faster because they've been pre-encoded through visualization.

### Emotional Inoculation

Repeatedly visualizing emergencies in a calm, controlled context (sitting in a chair, between cases) reduces the emotional shock when they occur in reality. This is exposure-based desensitization applied to clinical practice — and it's the exact mechanism behind [[ptsd-prehabilitation-gap]]: building resilience before the stressor arrives.

## Connection to Gap Moment Training

The visualization practice was a gap moment drill before the concept had a name:

| Visualization Component | GMT Equivalent |
|------------------------|----------------|
| Pre-case mental walkthrough | The Stability Snap (grounding + preparation) |
| Failure mode visualization | The Error Reversal Loop (recover executive function) |
| Downtime/boredom as training window | Gap moment recognition — dead time repurposed as training reps |
| Embodied motor rehearsal | Somatic engagement — body-based, not just cognitive |
| Running scenarios "over and over" | NeuroMinute stacking — brief, repeated, cumulative |

The Blue Angels metaphor is already embedded in the GMT concept note, which describes gap moments as "pre-flight checks for your nervous system." This observation reveals the autobiographical origin of that metaphor — it wasn't invented for marketing. It was lived.

## The Boredom Insight

The most significant detail: Randy did this practice **when he was bored.** Not during dedicated practice time. Not in a meditation session. During the interstitial dead time of clinical work — the same windows that Gap Moment Training now targets.

This means the training surface for emergency preparedness was always there, embedded in the workflow, waiting to be recognized. Just as CRNAs intuitively hum on the commute (see [[crna-humming-commute-regulation]]), Randy intuitively used boredom as a visualization training window. Both are gap moments recognized through embodied clinical wisdom before they were formalized.
