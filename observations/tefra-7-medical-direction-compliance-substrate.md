## The Finding

The shape of everyday anesthesia practice in medical-direction models is determined by seven CMS compliance rules (TEFRA-7). To bill modifiers QY or QK (Medicare medical direction of CRNAs by an anesthesiologist), the supervising MDA must meet **ALL seven rules for every case**. Missing any element drops the case to medical supervision (modifier AD), paid at 3 base units + 1 time unit only — a material revenue cut.

## The Seven Rules

1. Performs a pre-anesthetic examination and evaluation
2. Prescribes the anesthesia plan
3. **Personally participates in the most demanding procedures of the anesthesia plan, including (if applicable) induction and emergence**
4. Ensures that any procedures in the anesthesia plan that they do not perform are performed by a qualified individual
5. Monitors the course of anesthesia administration at frequent intervals
6. Remains physically present and available for immediate diagnosis and treatment of emergencies
7. **Provides indicated post-anesthesia care** (personal post-anesthesia note)

Plus the concurrency cap: no more than **four concurrent procedures** in a medical-direction ratio.

## Why It Shapes Everyday Practice

A single missed induction (rule 3) can blow up a whole day's medical-direction billing for the entire group. This is why compliance-driven ACT (Anesthesia Care Team) models are so rigid about MDA presence, room-start synchronization, and room-count ceilings. The TEFRA-7 substrate *produces* the rigidities that CRNAs experience as political friction but that are actually mechanical compliance constraints.

## Why It Matters for CRNAs

- In QZ (non-medical-direction) practice, TEFRA-7 does not apply. The entire billing substrate is different, and so is the practice texture.
- In QX/QY/QK practice, every case is subject to the seven-rule test, whether or not anyone is checking on any given day.
- The modifier on the case determines who signs your contract, who has leverage over your schedule, and what the practice actually feels like.
- Most CRNAs can quote their salary but cannot diagram their own billing geometry. Understanding the geometry is an under-used source of negotiating leverage and career-path clarity.
