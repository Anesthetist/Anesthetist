## Post Option A: The 16-Minute Illusion
**Target audience:** clinicians / healthcare leaders / researchers
**Domain:** Clinician Durability

AI scribes just completed their largest real-world test of the "give clinicians time back" hypothesis.

16 minutes a day.

And burnout dropped 21%.

Those two numbers don't reconcile if you believe burnout is a clock injury. You can't get 21% burnout relief from 16 minutes. But there's a different explanation.

Clinicians started looking at their patients instead of their screens.

That moment of eye contact isn't a break from clinical work. It's what clinical work is — the nervous system doing what it trained for, without interruption. When the scribe absorbs documentation, attention returns to the room. To the patient's breath, color, posture. To the signals the body spent a career learning to read.

The Mass General Brigham data quietly killed the time-savings theory of burnout.

Burnout isn't accumulating hours. It's the chronic cost of overriding what you're sensing in order to document what you saw.

Scribes didn't give clinicians time back. They gave them their attention back.

Those are not the same thing.

## Post Option B: The Speed Trap
**Target audience:** developers / tech / engineering leaders
**Domain:** Developer/Tech Burnout

Research finding, buried in the AI productivity coverage:

Experienced developers using AI coding tools took 19% longer to complete tasks. They believed they were 20% faster.

That's a 39-point gap between perceived and actual performance. And it only showed up in experienced developers — the ones with enough pattern recognition to move quickly, and enough confidence to miss when they're not.

Here's what's happening in the body: the sensation of speed and the reality of speed decouple. The nervous system reads fluency — text generating quickly, less friction at the keyboard — as competence. Your shoulders drop. Your breathing shallows out in the comfortable way, not the stressed way. You feel like you're in flow.

You're not tracking your actual error rate. The body doesn't have a metric for that.

This is what interoceptive suppression looks like at work. The internal signal that says "slow down, something's off" gets overridden by the signal that says "this feels right."

Working at machine speed doesn't make you faster. It makes you feel faster while the gap widens.

What's your actual signal for when AI is helping versus when it's making you confident and wrong?

## Post Option C: The Map Problem
**Target audience:** general / wellness professionals / executives
**Domain:** Wellness Industry Critique

Calm downloads are down 61% from peak. Headspace is down 74%. Eighty percent of wellness app users churn within 30 days.

The industry's response: better content. AI personalization. More features.

This is not a content problem.

You cannot build the capacity to sense your own body by listening to audio. Interoception — the ability to detect and use signals from inside you — is a trainable sensory skill. It sharpens with practice the way balance sharpens with movement. Not with information.

A guided meditation can introduce you to the concept of bodily awareness. It cannot build the neural architecture that makes that awareness available under load, when you actually need it — when the meeting goes sideways, when the diagnosis lands, when the email arrives.

The churn isn't consumer fickleness. It's people discovering, however dimly, that something isn't transferring. That they listened to 200 sessions and still can't find their breath in a difficult moment.

Wellness apps didn't fail because the content was bad. They failed because they were selling a map of territory you can only learn by walking.

What actually changed something for you — not what you enjoyed, but what moved?
