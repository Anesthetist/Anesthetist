## Overview

A single-page awareness exercise that investigates the participant's relationship to boundary-setting through two complementary lenses: inherited beliefs about boundaries (cognitive layer) and embodied experiences of boundary-setting (somatic/emotional layer).

## Exercise Structure

### Part 1: Beliefs About Boundaries
- What beliefs were you raised with regarding boundaries?
- What does it mean to set a boundary?
- Does setting a boundary feel loving to you? Why or why not?

### Part 2: The Energy of Setting Boundaries
Three contrasting experiences are solicited:
1. A boundary that felt **selfish** — taking care of yourself at the expense of others
2. A failure to set a boundary that felt **self-sacrificial** — letting others take advantage
3. A boundary that felt like it **created value** — served the highest good of yourself AND the overall system

### Core Framework

The exercise reveals a three-position spectrum of boundary energy: over-protective (selfish), under-protective (self-sacrificial), and integrated (value-creating for the whole system). The final question reframes boundaries not as walls but as system-level value generators — a boundary properly set serves the individual, the relationship, AND the system simultaneously.

## Somnistic Relevance

### Boundary-Setting as Autonomic Regulation Proxy

The exercise's three-position spectrum maps directly onto autonomic states: over-protective boundaries (sympathetic dominance / fight), absent boundaries (dorsal vagal / freeze/fawn), and integrated boundaries (ventral vagal / social engagement). The participant's habitual boundary pattern reveals their default autonomic strategy. This makes the exercise a non-instrumented interoceptive assessment — the participant's relationship to boundaries IS their autonomic signature in relational contexts.

### Co-Regulation Architecture

The final question — boundaries that create value for the whole system — describes co-regulation at the interpersonal level. A clinician who can set boundaries that simultaneously serve their own autonomic needs AND the clinical system's needs is practicing what SRL calls embodied clinical intelligence. This exercise provides a training pathway for CRNAs to recognize when their boundary patterns (either over-rigid or permeable) are degrading clinical co-regulation.

### Diagnostic-Therapeutic Unity

The exercise simultaneously surfaces (a) inherited beliefs about boundaries (cognitive schema), (b) the felt experience of boundary-setting (interoceptive data), and (c) the gap between the participant's current boundary practice and the integrated ideal. The assessment reveals the training target — where the stopping point is, what needs work.
