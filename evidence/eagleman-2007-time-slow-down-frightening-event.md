## Citation

Stetson, C., Fiesta, M.P., & Eagleman, D.M. (2007). Does time really slow down during a frightening event? *PLoS ONE*, 2(12), e1295. [DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0001295](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0001295)

## Key Findings

- Participants in a free-fall experiment (SCAD diving, 31m drop) overestimated their own fall duration by 36%
- However, they showed no increase in temporal resolution during the fall (measured by ability to read rapidly alternating digits on a wrist-mounted display)
- Conclusion: the subjective sense that "time slowed down" during a frightening event is a retrospective memory artifact, not a real-time perceptual enhancement
- Frightening events receive richer memory encoding (more detail, more sensory channels). When recalling the event, the brain interprets this richer memory as having lasted longer
- The perceptual system did not actually speed up. The memory system recorded more densely

## The Controversy

Newer research partially contests Eagleman's conclusion. Studies on action preparation during high-stress events suggest that motor planning does increase information processing rate in real time, even if passive perception does not speed up. The truth may be that both mechanisms operate: real-time motor enhancement (Wittmann's climbing activation speeding up under arousal) AND retrospective memory density effects (Eagleman's mechanism).

## Relevance to SRL

This is important counter-evidence that prevents overclaiming. SRL should not say "breathing slows time down." The more accurate claim: breathing modulates the neural accumulator rate (Wittmann mechanism), widening the temporal integration window in real time, AND changes the quality of memory encoding during the interval.

For clinical practice, the distinction matters. When a CRNA reports "everything happened so fast" after a code, two things happened: their temporal processing compressed in real time (sympathetic acceleration of the insular accumulator) AND their memory of the event is sparse (fewer landmarks encoded under cognitive overload). The 4-breath reset addresses the first mechanism. Structured debriefing addresses the second.

Eagleman's work also highlights why temporal training should be practiced before the emergency, not during it. You cannot voluntarily slow your clock in the middle of a free fall. But you can enter the emergency with a slower baseline clock if you've been training autonomic regulation.

## Methodology

Free-fall experiment with wrist-mounted chronometer displaying rapidly alternating digits. If temporal resolution increased during fear, participants should have been able to read the digits. They could not. Combined with duration estimation tasks pre/post fall.
