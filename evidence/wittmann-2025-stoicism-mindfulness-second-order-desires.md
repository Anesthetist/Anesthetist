## Citation

Wittmann, M., Montemayor, C., & Dorato, M. (2025). Stoicism, mindfulness, and the brain: The empirical foundations of second-order desires. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 16, 1569237. [DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1569237](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1569237)

## Key Findings

- Challenges the mainstream neuroscience dismissal of free will based on Libet-type experiments
- Argues that contemporary findings actually support the **Stoic view of free will** — diachronic self-regulation through second-order willed actions over time
- We possess the capacity to regulate actions proactively by cultivating **deliberate, voluntary intentions** — a meta-cognitive ability that can override first-order desires or impulsive habits
- Freedom arises from a **hierarchical, second-order will** that causally influences first-order impulses
- The self becomes a self-determined free agent through the capacity to reflect upon and respond to influences, not through freedom from them

## Second-Order Desires Framework

- **First-order desire:** Impulse to react (fight/flight, habitual response, autopilot)
- **Second-order desire:** The willed intention to regulate one's own first-order response
- Stoic proairesis = the capacity for deliberate choice about one's own dispositions
- Mindfulness training develops this meta-cognitive capacity empirically

## Relevance to SRL

**Gap moments ARE second-order desires in action:**

- When a CRNA recognizes a transition point and chooses to pause rather than react — that IS the exercise of second-order will over first-order autonomic impulse
- **GMT trains diachronic agency:** Not single-moment willpower but the sustained capacity to regulate across time — Wittmann's "diachronic self-regulation"
- The Stoic framework validates SRL's philosophical positioning: somnistics isn't relaxation (passive), it's **self-regulation** (active, willed, trained)
- **Consciousness engineering** in SRL's framework = developing second-order cognitive capacities that govern first-order autonomic responses
- This paper provides philosophical grounding for why brief practices (60 seconds) can have career-spanning effects — they train the meta-cognitive architecture, not just the momentary response
