# A Dynamic Model of Stress and Sustained Attention

## Summary

Hancock and Warm (1989) proposed an **extended-U model** (also called the dynamic adaptability model) of stress and sustained attention, integrating the classic Yerkes-Dodson inverted-U with resource theory and ecological demands. The model describes how performance degrades under both understimulation (hypostress/boredom) and overstimulation (hyperstress/overload), with a comfort zone of adaptability in between — and critically, how these boundaries shift dynamically with time on task.

## Key Contributions

- **Extended-U model**: Performance is bounded by zones of maximal adaptability; beyond these zones (in either direction), performance collapses — but the zones are dynamic, not fixed
- **Vigilance decrement**: Sustained attention tasks show reliable performance decline over time, with decrements accelerating in cognitively demanding conditions — watching for rare events is genuinely effortful
- **Stress as input demand**: Stress is not a simple arousal phenomenon; it reflects the mismatch between environmental demands and the operator's capacity for adaptive response
- **Time-on-task effects**: The boundaries of the adaptability zone narrow with time — what was manageable at the start of a shift becomes overwhelming later, even at the same objective demand level
- **Resource depletion model**: Attentional resources are finite and non-renewable within a session — performance degradation reflects genuine resource exhaustion, not mere boredom

## Methodological Notes

- Theoretical model integrating laboratory vigilance research with field data from military, aviation, and industrial settings
- Vigilance decrement is one of the most robust findings in human factors — replicated across hundreds of studies
- Model later extended by Hancock (2017) to include physiological stress markers
- Critiqued for not fully specifying the resource depletion mechanism — later work (Thomson et al., 2015) proposed mind-wandering as the proximal cause

## SRL Relevance

This is the foundational framework for understanding why CRNAs — whose job literally requires vigilance — experience performance degradation over long cases and across shift hours. The vigilance decrement is not a character flaw; it is a neurocognitive inevitability. SRL's approach reframes the problem: rather than demanding unsustainable vigilance, build **regulatory micro-practices** ([[neurominute]], [[five-breath-re-embodiment]]) that restore attentional resources during the natural gaps in clinical workflow.

The dynamic adaptability model also validates [[attention-as-gain-control]]: the CRNA's task is not to maintain maximum attention at all times (impossible) but to dynamically modulate attentional gain — high during critical moments, conserved during maintenance phases. This is the sophisticated attention management that [[gap-moment-training]] develops.
