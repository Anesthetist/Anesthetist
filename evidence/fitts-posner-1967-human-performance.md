# Human Performance

## Summary

Fitts and Posner (1967) established the canonical **three-stage model of motor learning** that remains the dominant framework in motor control and skill acquisition science. The model describes how motor skills progress from effortful, attention-demanding performance to fluid, automatic execution through three stages: cognitive, associative, and autonomous.

## Key Contributions

- **Three-stage model of motor learning**:
  - **Cognitive stage**: Learner must think about every component of the movement; performance is slow, inconsistent, and attention-demanding; relies on explicit verbal-cognitive strategies
  - **Associative stage**: Basic movement patterns are established; learner refines and links sub-movements; errors decrease; less verbal mediation needed; performance becomes more consistent
  - **Autonomous stage**: Movement is automatic, requires minimal attention, highly consistent, resistant to interference from concurrent tasks; performer can attend to higher-order strategic concerns
- Demonstrated that skill acquisition follows a **power law of practice** — rapid early gains that decelerate over time
- Showed that **attentional demands** decrease systematically with practice, freeing cognitive resources for other tasks
- Established that **automaticity** is not all-or-nothing but a graded phenomenon

## Methodological Notes

- Synthesized laboratory research on motor skill acquisition
- Power law of practice later debated (Heathcote et al., 2000) — may be an exponential law
- Three-stage model simplified — Gentile (1972) later added a two-stage model emphasizing the distinction between getting the idea of the movement and fixation/diversification
- Remains the most widely taught framework in physical therapy, sports science, and clinical skill training

## SRL Relevance

The Fitts-Posner model explains why experienced CRNAs can perform intubations while simultaneously monitoring hemodynamics and communicating with the surgeon — their procedural motor skills have reached the autonomous stage, freeing attentional resources for higher-order clinical judgment. This is the physiological basis of [[attention-as-gain-control]]: automaticity in lower-level skills creates bandwidth for the perceptual sensitivity that defines expert performance.

The model also explains the design logic of [[neurominute]] protocols: each NeuroMinute targets a specific regulatory skill (breathing pattern, body scan, state transition) and aims to move it from cognitive to autonomous stage through structured repetition — so that the skill becomes available under load without consuming attentional resources.
