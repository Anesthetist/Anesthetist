# Choke

## Summary

Sian Beilock (2010) synthesized two decades of research on **choking under pressure** — the paradox of performing worse precisely when performance matters most. She identified two primary mechanisms: **attention-based choking** (too much self-focused attention disrupts automated skills) and **working memory depletion** (worry and anxiety consume the cognitive resources needed for complex reasoning). The book bridges laboratory research and real-world performance failure.

## Key Contributions

- **Two mechanisms of choking**:
  - **Explicit monitoring / self-focus**: Pressure causes experts to attend to the step-by-step components of automated skills, disrupting their fluid execution — "paralysis by analysis"
  - **Distraction / working memory depletion**: Anxiety and worry consume working memory resources, leaving insufficient capacity for tasks requiring complex thinking, reasoning, or multi-step problem solving
- **Expertise paradox**: The same automaticity that makes experts excellent under normal conditions makes them vulnerable to self-focus choking — attending to automated processes breaks them
- **Interventions**: Practiced under pressure, dual-task training, expressive writing before performance, and left-hand squeezing (right hemisphere activation) all reduce choking
- **Stereotype threat as choking**: Connected choking research to stereotype threat — performance decrements in stigmatized groups work through the same working memory depletion mechanism
- **Practice under pressure**: Training under conditions that simulate performance anxiety builds inoculation — the stress of practice must match the stress of performance

## Methodological Notes

- Synthesizes laboratory experiments (putting studies, math under pressure, test-taking) and field research
- Population-level book; underlying research published in peer-reviewed journals (Beilock & Carr, 2001; Beilock et al., 2004)
- Some debate about generalizability from sports/academic tasks to clinical settings
- Connects to Yerkes-Dodson, attentional control theory (Eysenck), and processing efficiency theory

## SRL Relevance

Beilock's work explains exactly the failure mode that SRL's training prevents. Clinical emergencies create the conditions for both forms of choking: self-focus (over-attending to procedural steps that should be automatic) and working memory depletion (anxiety consuming cognitive resources needed for clinical reasoning). [[learning-under-load]] is explicitly designed as inoculation training — building regulatory skills under simulated pressure so they remain available during actual crises.

The explicit monitoring mechanism explains why novice CRNAs can perform well in simulation but fail under real pressure — and why experienced CRNAs who develop anxiety about a procedure they have performed thousands of times can suddenly fumble. [[gap-moment-training]] provides the meta-cognitive tool to catch the self-focus spiral before it disrupts automaticity.
