# The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception

## Summary

James Gibson (1979) proposed a radical alternative to cognitive/computational theories of perception, arguing that perception is **direct** — organisms pick up information from the environment without the need for internal representations, mental models, or computational processing. The environment offers **affordances** (action possibilities) that are directly perceived by an attuned organism. Expertise, in this view, is about developing perceptual attunement to increasingly subtle affordances, not building richer internal models.

## Key Contributions

- **Affordances**: The action possibilities that an environment offers to an organism — a chair affords sitting, a handle affords grasping, an OR affords specific clinical actions. Affordances are properties of the animal-environment system, not of the environment alone
- **Direct perception**: Information sufficient for action is available in the ambient energy arrays (optic flow, acoustic structure) — no internal computation or representation required
- **Perceptual learning as attunement**: Expertise develops through the differentiation of increasingly fine-grained information in the environment, not through the construction of richer internal models
- **Ecological validity**: Perception research must study organisms in their natural environments, not in impoverished laboratory settings
- **Information pickup**: The organism actively explores the environment, creating information through movement and action — perception is active, not passive reception

## Methodological Notes

- Theoretical/philosophical framework with experimental foundations (Gibson's earlier work on visual cliff, texture gradients)
- Deeply influential in ecological psychology, human factors, sports science, and HCI
- Controversial within cognitive science — the "perception wars" between computational and ecological approaches continue
- Extended by Turvey, Reed, and Warren into dynamical systems approaches

## Challenge to SRL

Ecological psychology argues that expertise is about **direct perception of affordances in the environment**, not internal modeling. SRL's heavy emphasis on interoception (internal signals) may **underweight the expert's relationship with the clinical environment**. A CRNA's expertise may be more about perceiving what the OR affords — reading the monitors, the surgeon's hands, the anesthesia machine's behavior, the patient's color — than about monitoring internal bodily states. Gibson would argue that training people to attend inward when they should be attending outward is a category error. The expert does not introspect their way to good clinical decisions; they perceive the situation directly and act.

## SRL Response

The stairway meta-awareness protocol explicitly integrates **external** signals (soundscape, team dynamics, environmental affordances) alongside **internal** signals (body state, emotional texture). SRL does not claim interoception replaces environmental perception — it claims that the clinician's interoceptive state modulates their capacity to perceive environmental affordances accurately. A dysregulated CRNA perceives fewer affordances (attentional narrowing under stress). The internal and external are coupled, not competing. However, Gibson's challenge remains a useful corrective: if any protocol trains sustained inward attention during clinical performance, it is pulling attention from the environment where the action is.

## Design Constraint

**If a protocol directs attention exclusively inward during clinical performance, it degrades environmental perception. Interoceptive check-ins must be momentary, not sustained, during active clinical work.**

## SRL Relevance

Gibson provides the theoretical counterbalance to SRL's interoceptive emphasis. The concept of affordances reframes what clinical expertise actually is — not internal modeling but direct perception of what the clinical situation allows and demands. This connects to Hutchins' distributed cognition: the intelligence is in the coupling between clinician and environment, not in the clinician's head alone. SRL must ensure its protocols enhance this coupling rather than disrupt it.
