# ALI Leadership Skill: Positive Boundaries

## Framework Summary

This is the applied Leadership Skill version of Positive Boundaries (complementing the Awareness Exercise version that explores beliefs about boundaries). Where the Awareness Exercise diagnoses the internal landscape around boundary-setting, this document provides the 5-part protocol for actually setting and maintaining value-creating boundaries in relationships.

### 5-Part Protocol

**Part 1 — Presenting Problem**
Identify a specific relationship where internal conflict exists: one part feels the obligation to give more while another part feels angry, overwhelmed, or resentful. Name the specific situation and the feelings that arise.

**Part 2 — Shared Purpose**
Frame the boundary as serving the whole system — not just self-protection but mutual benefit. The boundary is positioned as something that strengthens the relationship rather than creates distance.

**Part 3 — Needs Assessment**
Identify the specific needs the boundary would meet: emotional safety, sustainability, energy preservation. The example template shows how a boundary that prevents escalation simultaneously meets both parties' needs.

**Part 4 — Positive Communication**
A structured communication template that:
1. Opens with shared purpose and positive value desired
2. Acknowledges the current difficulty honestly
3. Proposes a specific boundary with clear behavioral terms
4. Explains the rationale (why this serves both parties)
5. Invites collaboration: "That's the best idea I can think of. Do you have a better one?"

The collaborative close is critical — it positions boundary-setting as a shared creative act rather than a unilateral demand.

**Part 5 — Boundary Management Strategies**
Two-tier contingency planning:
- **Centered strategy**: When regulated, address boundary violations directly without blame or judgment — name the behavior, state the request
- **Fallback strategy**: When not centered (escalating emotionally), declare a pause and commit to re-engaging within a specified timeframe (e.g., 24 hours) once both parties are re-regulated

The two-tier design acknowledges that the capacity to hold boundaries is state-dependent — different strategies are needed depending on one's current autonomic state.

## Somnistic Relevance

This protocol operationalizes several core Somnistic principles in the relational domain:

- **State-dependent capacity**: The two-tier strategy (centered vs. not centered) is an explicit acknowledgment that relational skill is autonomic-state-dependent. This IS the polyvagal principle applied to boundary communication — ventral vagal engagement enables clean communication, while sympathetic activation requires a different protocol (strategic withdrawal and re-regulation).
- **Co-regulation through boundary**: The framework reframes boundaries not as barriers to connection but as containers FOR connection — the boundary creates the safety that enables deeper co-regulation. This inverts the common assumption that boundaries reduce intimacy.
- **Autonomic home base as prerequisite**: The protocol's emphasis on centeredness as the platform for effective communication maps directly to the concept of autonomic home base — one must be able to access and maintain a regulated baseline state to deploy these skills.
- **Clinician application — patient boundaries**: For CRNAs and clinical teams, this framework translates directly to setting professional boundaries with patients, families, surgeons, and administrators — using positive framing, shared purpose, and state-aware contingency planning rather than reactive or passive-aggressive boundary management.
- **Diagnostic-therapeutic unity**: The presenting problem identification (Part 1) simultaneously reveals the boundary deficit AND begins the repair process. Naming the conflict IS the first step of resolution.
