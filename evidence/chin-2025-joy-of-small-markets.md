# The Joy of Small Markets (Chin 2025)

## Summary

Cedric Chin challenges the venture-capital orthodoxy that only billion-dollar markets merit entrepreneurial pursuit. His core thesis: **"It can be easier to get generationally rich in a small market compared to a large one."**

The argument builds on Jonathan Knee's (Columbia Business School, former investment banker) framework of **relative scale economics**, originally elucidated by Bruce Greenwald in *Competition Demystified*, transmitted to Chin via investor George Ho (who taught a semester of Columbia's legendary Securities Analysis class).

## The Three-Player Market Model

Chin constructs a detailed hypothetical:

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Total market size | ~$100M |
| Number of major competitors | 3 |
| Per-competitor revenue | ~$33M/year |
| Extractable annual dividends (owner earnings) | ~$5M/year |
| Year-over-year share fluctuation | 1-3% |
| Market share stability | Mostly stable; gains/losses reverse year to year |

The key insight is **relative scale** — what matters is not the absolute size of the company, but its size relative to competitors within a contained market.

### Protection from Below (Sub-Scale Competitors)

Established players amortize fixed costs across a larger customer base than smaller entrants can match. Relative size creates a structural unit-economics advantage within the contained market, even if the leader would be "small" in absolute terms.

### Protection from Above (Large Corporations)

Large companies see a market that is too small and too expensive to pursue:
- **Investment required:** ~$25M/year for 5 years = $125M total
- **Prize:** One-quarter of a $100M market = ~$25M/year revenue, shared with three other major competitors
- **Verdict:** "This cost is not typically worth the prize — unless capital is exceedingly cheap, or there is a non-economic reason present … or the large business has run out of other lucrative opportunities to pursue."

### The Result: Dual Moat

The small-market leader sits in a **structurally protected position** — too large for small competitors to dislodge, too small for large competitors to justify attacking.

## Capital Cycles and Cyclical Markets

Chin extends the framework to cyclical markets where demand outstrips supply, causing capital to flood in:

> "You might imagine a pond, expanding during a particularly wet season and becoming host to a greater number of organisms. … But when the capital cycle inevitably ends, the company that survives is often the one with relative scale, as every sub-scale player loses capital support."

The cycle *benefits* the incumbent with relative scale: "the pond shrinks, leaving the big fish happy and well fed … with the corpses of its many smaller neighbours." Skilled operators avoid entering during times of plenty; only unsophisticated players do.

## Hidden Champions

The framework explains the phenomenon coined by Hermann Simon (1990): medium-sized, relatively unknown companies with revenues under $4-5B that achieve global leadership in highly specialized niches. Originally focused on German family-owned firms contributing to export success, but the pattern exists globally.

## The Francois Barrel-Making Case Study (via Anthony Deden)

A multi-generational French cooperage (barrel-making) family business:
- Controlled the Bordeaux and Burgundy barrel market
- The son saw opportunity in consolidation driven by competitor incompetence, particularly in Scottish cooperages
- Expanded to Australia, California, Scotland, South Africa, Chile
- Became the largest company of its kind globally at only ~$50M revenue
- Deden's assessment: "They will be making the same barrels 50 years from now, and they will be the very best at it, and they will be three, four, five times the size they are today"

## Weak Competitors in Small Markets

Chin's direct experience: "small, unsexy markets attract subpar operators." Over a decade or two, competitors become bored, distracted, or make unforced errors due to bad succession or greed. Result: "your 33% market share becomes 50%, or 66% as they bow out. And if you get lucky, if you stick around long enough, you may eventually capture the whole damned thing."

His old company: "we executed a coherent strategy, and in the process put our competitors out of business. It wasn't that they weren't competent operators. Many of them were competent enough. But they just weren't strategic."

## Owner Earnings Model

Drawing on Warren Buffett's concept:
- Pay yourself a decent middle-class salary ($10K-$20K/month) for expenses
- Issue yourself a dividend: single-digit millions in cash, once a year, every year, for at least a decade
- 100% ownership means no dilution, no board approval, no exit dependency

Buffett: "I don't look to jump over 7-foot bars: I look around for 1-foot bars that I can step over."

## Knee's Market Identification Framework (*The Platform Delusion*, Chapter 2)

Two critical diagnostic questions:

1. **Maximum number of profitable competitors:** If 5% market share achieves commercial viability → 20 competitors can thrive. If 35% break-even share is required → only monopoly or duopoly is sustainable.

2. **Time to breakeven for new entrants:** If a market requires 15% share to break even and shares never move more than 3% annually, a new entrant cannot hope to achieve profitability in under five years. Normalized share shift under 5% over 2-3 years signals high barriers.

Key Knee insight: "truly global markets are often significantly less lucrative for operators than the purely local ones that had previously prevailed — with the dramatic increase in total market opportunity typically comes a decrease in the newly relevant global market share at which viability is achieved."

## Caveats and Counterarguments

Chin is explicit about limitations:
- "Not all small markets are good." The vast majority of SME operators "chew glass for a living."
- The essay's purpose is to show that a) the dynamic exists in some markets, b) smart operators have exploited it for generational wealth, and c) you should be able to identify the dynamics needed.
- Knee warns that relative scale only applies to markets with **supply-side scale advantages** that are **isolated from competition in larger adjacent markets**. If an overseas competitor with equal relative scale can attack (Toyota in the 70s, BYD today), the moat breaks.
- Large, global markets "can support many viable competitors" — the venture-capital assumption that market size = opportunity is often wrong because large markets have low concentration.

## Personal Experience

Chin's company was acquired by Ant Group. He describes the small-market wealth model as "the predominant model in the world that I come from" and the way "most of the successful SME entrepreneurs I know made their wealth."

## Key Claims Relevant to SRL

- **CRNAs as textbook small market:** 60,000 US CRNAs is exactly the kind of concentrated, identity-driven, high-income professional market Chin describes. Small enough that large wellness companies (Calm, Headspace, Lyra) can't justify clinical specialization to enter. Concentrated enough (AANA as single professional body) that a focused player can dominate.

- **Protection from below validates:** Generic breathing apps (Breathwrk, Prana Labs) can't match SRL's clinical credibility, CRNA-designed protocols, or domain-specific workflow integration. They are sub-scale in the domain expertise dimension.

- **Protection from above validates:** Enterprise mental health companies (Lyra at $910M raised, Spring Health at $3.3B valuation) won't build CRNA-specific products for 60,000 users. Calm and Headspace won't add biometric-integrated clinical protocols for a market that small.

- **Weak competitors confirm:** The wellness app market aimed at clinicians is filled with generic, non-clinical competitors. No one is running Randy's playbook — 28 years of CRNA practice + polyvagal science + biometric integration + workflow-embedded delivery. Chin's observation that "they just weren't strategic" maps to SRL's competitive landscape.

- **Knee's diagnostics applied to CRNAs:** If CRNA-specific clinical wellness requires 30%+ market share to break even (high fixed costs of clinical credibility, protocol development, CE accreditation), the market supports at most 2-3 viable competitors. Current normalized share shift is near zero — no one holds meaningful share yet. First mover with relative scale wins.

- **The Don Katz answer:** When Don asks "why is the target market so specific (and small)?" — this is the strategic answer. The market isn't small as a limitation; it's small as a competitive advantage. Chin: "If one's goal is independence and wealth, then there are probably easier ways to get to independence and wealth than the pursuit of hyper-competitive global markets."

- **Relative scale in credibility, not just revenue:** Chin's framework applies to SRL's credibility moat. Randy's CRNA practice, AANA membership (#071955), and clinical publication record create a relative credibility scale advantage that no VC-backed wellness startup can replicate. The credibility is the fixed cost that can't be amortized by a new entrant.

- **Capital cycle protection:** If a VC-backed competitor (like Climatic, $10M, Mount Sinai) enters the clinical breathing space, SRL is the relative-scale incumbent in the CRNA niche. When VC funding cycles turn, sub-scale clinical wellness startups will fail. SRL, with low burn and high credibility, survives and consolidates.
