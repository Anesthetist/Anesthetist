## Overview

Stephan et al. propose "allostatic self-efficacy" as a metacognitive construct — the brain's belief in its own capacity to regulate bodily homeostasis. Within a computational (Bayesian) framework, fatigue and depression are recast as consequences of metacognitive inference that allostatic control is failing. Interoceptive prediction errors accumulate, signaling dyshomeostasis, and the resulting loss of confidence in self-regulation drives the phenomenology of exhaustion and anhedonia.

## Key Findings

1. Introduces "allostatic self-efficacy" — the metacognitive belief in one's ability to regulate internal bodily states and maintain homeostasis
2. Fatigue and depression arise from metacognitive inference that allostatic control is failing, not merely from peripheral signals
3. Interoceptive prediction errors serve as the key signal of dyshomeostasis, driving updates to metacognitive models of self-regulation capacity
4. Treatment should target metacognitive beliefs about body regulation, not just symptoms or peripheral physiology
5. Bridges predictive processing theory to clinical intervention, providing a computational account of why mind-body practices work

## Connection to Somnistics

Allostatic self-efficacy is essentially what [[interoceptive-literacy]] builds — both the capacity and the belief that you can regulate your own body. This paper provides the computational bridge between SRL's physiological interventions ([[resonant-breathing-frequency]], [[autonomic-regulation]]) and their psychological outcomes ([[clinician-durability]], resilience).
