# Notes from the Lab: The Missing Metric — The Awe Index and the Dimension Wellness Programs Don't Measure

*Clinical wellness measurement has a missing dimension: current instruments reliably detect what the nervous system defends against, but not what it opens toward — and awe, with its distinct measurable autonomic and inflammatory signature, is the proposed biomarker for that second domain.*

---

## The Observation

Every metric in the standard CRNA wellness stack points in the same direction: away from pathology.

RMSSD tells you how fast the heart adapts between beats — a proxy for how much autonomic reserve the system is holding. MAIA-2 tells you how accurately a clinician notices, trusts, and doesn't distract from their body signals — a measure of interoceptive competence. The Maslach Burnout Inventory's depersonalization subscale tells you whether a clinician has begun to emotionally detach from the work — a red-flag marker for terminal depletion. Even HRV coherence, which sounds positive, measures the efficiency of a recovery system under stress.

All of these measure what the nervous system costs, holds, or defends against.

None of them measure what it opens toward.

This is not a minor gap. Bannon, Evans, Freedman, and colleagues (2022) surveyed 2,411 healthcare workers at a major academic health system during the COVID-19 pandemic and found that positive affect and meaning/purpose were each significantly, independently associated with reduced burnout — positive affect (coef = -0.19, p < 0.001) and meaning/purpose (coef = -0.12, p < 0.001) — beyond the protective effect of lower depression and anxiety scores. [PMID: 36925776, DOI: 10.3389/frhs.2022.994474] In other words, adding a positive dimension to the measurement predicted outcomes that the deficit-only model couldn't explain.

The clinical wellness infrastructure is measuring only one of two dimensions.

---

## The Mechanism

The Dispositional Positive Emotion Scales (DPES), developed by Shiota, Keltner, and John (2006), operationalized seven distinct positive emotional dispositions: joy, contentment, pride, love, compassion, amusement, and awe. Güsewell and Ruch (2012), analyzing the DPES in 574 German-speaking adults, confirmed that awe loads on its own separate factor — distinct from joy, contentment, and all other DPES emotions — suggesting it represents a qualitatively different mode of positive experience, not merely a stronger version of happiness. [PMID: 26286979, DOI: 10.1111/j.1758-0854.2012.01070.x]

What makes awe physiologically distinct is not simply that it feels different, but that it produces a different pattern of biological signals.

Stellar, John-Henderson, Anderson, Gordon, McNeil, and Keltner (2015) measured blood levels of IL-6 — the proinflammatory cytokine most consistently elevated in stress and burnout — against dispositional positive affect across ten emotion categories in two healthy samples. Awe was the strongest predictor of lower IL-6 levels across all positive emotions tested, including joy, gratitude, love, and contentment. [PMID: 25603133, DOI: 10.1037/emo0000033] Not the most pleasant, not the most frequently experienced — the one that demands the most cognitive work: the accommodation of something vast that existing mental schemas cannot hold.

The ANS signature of awe appears to differ from generic parasympathetic activation (the standard target of HRV biofeedback). Zickfeld, Arriaga, Santos, Schubert, and Seibt (2020) measured continuous cardiovascular, respiratory, and electrodermal activity in 144 participants exposed to emotion-inducing videos targeting kama muta (being moved), sadness, and awe. [PMID: 32786039, DOI: 10.1111/psyp.13662] The physiological profile of kama muta — elevated phasic skin conductance and temperature with reduced heart rate and respiration — was successfully distinguished from both sadness and awe. Awe produced a different pattern, with less piloerection and a distinct respiratory signature. This is not a clean verification of a single awe biomarker, but it is direct empirical evidence that awe is physiologically distinguishable from closely related emotional states.

Here is where the Awe Index becomes interesting: if awe has a distinct physiological profile, then a passive biometric reading that tracks only the defensive dimension (HRV elevation, RMSSD, respiratory coherence) will systematically miss the moments when the nervous system is in the awe state. A clinician generating a physiological signature of awe is not necessarily a clinician generating high RMSSD. These may co-occur, but they are measuring different things.

The critical distinction, per the concept note authored by Randy Graybeal: "Most Pausality metrics measure resilience *against* stress. The Awe Index measures resilience *toward* something." A clinician who has optimized their defensive metrics — recovering fast, holding coherence under load, suppressing sympathetic activation reliably — can still be experiencing the progressive hollowing that characterizes depersonalization. They can be technically excellent and phenomenologically detached. The defensive metrics don't capture that distinction. The Awe Index is proposed as the metric that does.

---

## The Framework

The Awe Index is not a single scale. It is a three-layer detection architecture, each capturing a different access point to the same underlying phenomenon: the nervous system orienting toward something larger than ego-centric processing.

**Layer 1: Passive biometric.** The proposed physiological signature of awe — parasympathetic activation without the deflation of low arousal, combined with the skin conductance and respiratory patterns that distinguish it from generic relaxation — can be approximated through wearable sensors. Heart rate elevation with simultaneously low tonic skin conductance (suggesting engagement without defense) is one candidate pattern. Respiratory rate that slows alongside elevated HRV (the coherence signature) without accompanying sympathetic indicators (the traditional defense pattern) is another. These are research hypotheses, not validated biomarkers. But the biometric architecture exists. The signal needs isolation.

**Layer 2: Self-tagged moments (Autonomic Diary).** The [[autonomic-diary]] is where the passive biometric becomes clinically meaningful. Rather than relying on algorithmic detection alone, the clinician tags moments in their dictation: "expansive," "different," "something shifted." These self-report tags provide ground truth labels for the biometric training — a way to teach the system what the awe state looks like *for this clinician*, not for a population average. Individual variability in awe's physiological expression is real and documented. Golec de Zavala, Keenan, Ziegler, and colleagues (2023) conducted an RCT (N = 219) in which mindfulness training measurably increased self-transcendent emotions including awe, with individual changes in awe positively associated with wellbeing changes. [PMID: 37432062, DOI: 10.1111/aphw.12468] The training effect was significant, but the individual-level association between awe change and wellbeing change was what the study confirmed. Self-report access to awe is trainable and meaningful.

**Layer 3: Contextual correlation.** Over longitudinal tracking, the Awe Index generates a personalized map: *what reliably produces the opening response for this clinician?* A patient waking safely after a difficult airway. The stillness at the top of a breath hold. An unexpected moment of team synchrony during a complex case. These patterns, identified through combined biometric and diary data, become an individualized awe profile — the clinician's personal map of where flourishing lives, not where stress abates.

---

## The Failure Mode

The central failure mode of the Awe Index is not technical. It is phenomenological: awe cannot be generated on command, and the act of trying to generate it collapses it into its opposite.

When a clinician approaches a gap moment with the goal of "experiencing awe" — because the metric is tracking it, because the program asks for it — they have immediately re-entered ego-centric processing. The goal-directed performance of awe is the precise attentional structure that prevents it. This is the performance trap that destroys every prescriptive wellness protocol that has been institutionalized: the moment a genuine state becomes a managed metric, it ceases to be the genuine state.

The second failure mode is confounding by case complexity. High-complexity cases produce physiological arousal that may co-occur with something like awe — the sense that this physiology is extraordinary, this work is at the edge of what the team can do — but the arousal signature may be nearly indistinguishable from defensive sympathetic activation on passive biometrics alone. The Autonomic Diary is the disambiguating layer: the self-tag creates the distinction the biometric cannot make alone.

The third: the Awe Index loses meaning if it becomes a performance target rather than a reflective signal. An administrative system that asks CRNAs to report their weekly awe score has already misunderstood the instrument.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 12 CRNAs. 8 weeks. Three weekly Autonomic Diary sessions (voice-first, 60-second post-case dictations, self-tagging expansive vs. contractive moments). Continuous wearable HRV and EDA monitoring during shifts via Apple Watch or equivalent.

**Primary measure:** Frequency of self-tagged expansive/awe moments per week, correlated with simultaneously recorded biometric pattern (HRV, EDA, respiratory). Goal: identify whether a consistent biometric signature differentiates clinician-labeled awe moments from clinician-labeled neutral moments.

**Secondary measure:** MBI depersonalization subscale at baseline, 4 weeks, 8 weeks.

**Tertiary:** MAIA-2 "Trusting" subscale — the relationship between interoceptive trust and awe frequency. The hypothesis is that clinicians with higher interoceptive trust report more awe moments, because they are more available to the signals that initiate accommodation.

**What this doesn't test:** Whether the awe state *causes* reduced depersonalization, or whether clinicians who are less depersonalized are simply more available to awe — the causal arrow is unknown. This study establishes the biometric correlate and the self-report-biometric alignment, not the direction of causality. A longitudinal design with a pre-post intervention (introducing the Autonomic Diary and awe-attentional training) is the next step.

## The Connection

The Awe Index is the eudaimonic layer of the Somnistics measurement stack. [[gap-moment-training]] creates the container. [[vagal-tone]] and [[attentional-sovereignty]] create the capacity. The [[autonomic-diary]] creates the longitudinal narrative. But without an Awe Index, the stack can optimize a clinician for recovery without ever measuring whether they are flourishing.

The distinction matters because the failure mode of clinical training is not low RMSSD — it is progressive detachment from the meaning of the work. Depersonalization, in the burnout literature, is the most treatment-resistant dimension of burnout precisely because it is not a deficit of recovery. It is a deficit of opening. And the current measurement infrastructure has no metric for opening.

What Güsewell and Ruch confirmed in 2012 — that awe is a distinct, measurable positive emotional disposition separable from all other positive emotions — and what Bannon and colleagues demonstrated in 2022 — that positive affect and meaning each independently predict burnout reduction — together establish the empirical ground for what the Awe Index proposes: that the generative dimension of autonomic regulation is measurable, clinically meaningful, and currently unmeasured.

---

*At the end of a long night, the body that has been managing crises finally encounters one uncomplicated fact: the patient is breathing on their own. That is not a metric. That is an opening. The difference between a clinician who notices it and one who does not is the difference between a career that compounds and one that hollows — and we have, until now, had no instrument for that difference.*
