# Notes from the Lab: The Structure of the Game — Non-Ergodic Risk and the Physiology of the Absorbing State

*In anesthesia practice — a non-ergodic system where a single cannot-intubate/cannot-ventilate event is terminal regardless of a provider's career success rate — RMSSD is not a wellness metric but the physiological buffer that determines whether a provider's nervous system has the reserve to prevent entry into an absorbing catastrophic state, making vagal tone maintenance patient safety infrastructure rather than provider wellness preference.*

## The Observation

Pre-case. You've done this 10,000 times. The number settles over you like armor. Four hundred milligrams of propofol calculated. Succinylcholine drawn. Blade selected, videolaryngoscope backed up on the cart. Ten thousand times.

But this patient — BMI 42, Mallampati IV, short neck, prior neck surgery with radiation — has anatomy that doesn't care about your prior 10,000. The question is not about probability. It never was. The question is about the structure of the game you're playing.

And in this game, the relevant mathematics is not statistics. It's ergodicity.

## The Mechanism

The mathematician and risk philosopher Luca Dellanna described it plainly: in a non-ergodic system, the outcome of an activity performed once by many people diverges from the outcome of one person performing it many times. Put differently — what happens across a population does not predict what happens to you over time when ruin is possible. Ensemble averages and time averages decouple. The population's "average outcome" becomes a statistic you cannot live inside of.

Anesthesia is a canonical non-ergodic system. The evidence supports this framing with unusual precision.

Sigurdsson and McAteer (1996) established that failure to ventilate the patient remains the most common cause of anesthesia-related death and serious injury — independent of improvements in monitoring and pharmacology [PMID: 8908219, DOI: 10.1111/j.1399-6576.1996.tb05623.x]. They noted a structural paradox that maps exactly onto non-ergodic logic: because anesthesia-related adverse outcomes are rare, "strict safety procedures must be applied to thousands of patients in order to prevent one catastrophe." The reassuring denominator in a population study does not protect the individual patient on today's table.

Niven and Doerschug (2013) quantified the difficult airway encounter rate during emergent intubation at 10% [PMID: 23242213, DOI: 10.1097/MCC.0b013e32835c6014]. Darby, Halenda, Chou, Quinlan, Alarcon, and Simmons (2016) examined what happens when that 10% escalates to a cannot-intubate scenario requiring emergency surgical airway: 68% of events involved complications, overall hospital mortality was 59%, and 38% of deaths occurred at the time of the airway event itself [PMID: 27899469, DOI: 10.1177/0885066616680594].

This is the absorbing state. Not a complication that trails another course. A state from which a meaningful fraction of patients do not return. A provider's prior 10,000 successful intubations provide no mathematical insurance against this specific case.

That is what non-ergodicity means in clinical practice. The relevant question stops being "what is the average outcome?" and becomes "does this path include a state from which there is no recovery?" The CRNA who has never had a failed airway carries the same absorbing-state risk as any other qualified provider facing the same patient anatomy. Averages lie when ruin is on the table.

This reframes everything about what HRV measures.

Liberman, Slagle, Whitney, and colleagues (2020) prospectively documented nonroutine events across 556 cases at three academic hospitals and found that anesthesia provider contribution was an identifiable factor in 59% of cases with nonroutine events — the single most common modifiable variable in the failure chain [PMID: 32404773, DOI: 10.1097/ALN.0000000000003336]. The provider is not a bystander in clinical failure. They are the intervention point with the highest leverage.

What does autonomic state do to that leverage point?

Hattori, Asamoto, Otsuji, and colleagues (2019) measured HRV in anesthesiology residents continuously throughout their work day. SDANN — the standard deviation of 5-minute average RR intervals, a measure of overall autonomic reserve — was significantly lower in residents than in same-aged healthy controls: 69.3 ± 27.9 ms versus 137.0 ± 43.0 ms [PMID: 30937595, DOI: 10.1007/s10877-019-00305-z]. The suppression was measurable before psychological distress reached threshold. Residents reported being within normal psychological mood ranges while their autonomic nervous systems were already running at roughly half the reserve of their age-matched peers. The physiological signal preceded the subjective one.

Chand, Li, Jamalabadi, and colleagues (2020) demonstrated that RMSSD correlates dynamically with functional connectivity between the default mode network and the central executive network — a relationship that disappears after acute psychosocial stress exposure [PMID: 32714132, DOI: 10.3389/fnins.2020.00645]. When the vagal brake is compromised, the brain loses the dynamic coupling between its reflective and executive systems. This is precisely the coupling required for rapid, adaptive decision-making when an unexpected difficult airway demands sequential judgment under time pressure.

Ramakumar and Sama (2024), reviewing the perioperative evidence for HRV biofeedback, identified utility in "predicting catastrophic complications" — the application of real-time autonomic monitoring not as a wellness indicator but as a risk stratification tool in the perioperative environment [PMID: 39287174, DOI: 10.4274/TJAR.2024.241658].

The mechanism now has a shape. RMSSD is not a readout of how calm a provider feels. It is a real-time index of the physiological buffer available when the system is forced toward its worst-case branch. When RMSSD is suppressed, the vagal brake is withdrawn, prefrontal-executive coupling degrades, and the cognitive flexibility required for adaptive airway management is compromised — all before the provider's subjective assessment registers any change. The absorbing state moves closer not because the anatomy changed, but because the provider's system has less reserve to prevent the path toward it.

Three variables changed simultaneously in the residents Hattori studied: autonomic tone, stress reactivity at induction, and psychological self-report. Only one of the three detected the problem. That is the confound Somnistics is designed to address.

## The Framework

Non-ergodic risk management is not the same as risk reduction. You cannot make a cannot-intubate/cannot-ventilate event impossible. You can, however, build and maintain the physiological reserve that widens the window between unexpected difficulty and catastrophic failure.

This is why Gap Moment Training is correctly understood as patient safety infrastructure rather than provider wellness programming. The Gap Moment — the 60 to 180 seconds between cases, during a medication reconciliation pause, before walking into a room — is the interval where autonomic reserve is either maintained or eroded. Each skipped gap is not a missed wellness opportunity. In the mathematics of a non-ergodic system, it is a marginal increase in the probability of reaching an absorbing state in a later case.

The CO2 tolerance threshold, operationalized through the BOLT score, functions as a practical proxy for this reserve. A score below 25 seconds is associated with a pattern of chronic over-breathing, sympathetic activation, and reduced physiological buffering capacity. It is the body's own report card for absorbing-state risk — not a diagnosis, but a direction.

The key variable in a non-ergodic system is not your average. It is whether your path reaches the terminal branch. Training for that variable looks different than training for performance improvement. It looks like building a system that doesn't go where you can't come back from.

## The Failure Mode

The most dangerous thought in anesthesia is: "I've done this 10,000 times." It is dangerous precisely because it is true. It describes the average correctly while saying nothing about this specific path.

The statistical worldview — "I have a low error rate; therefore my risk is low" — imports ergodic assumptions into a non-ergodic environment. It treats career outcomes as a portfolio that can be diversified when, for any individual patient on any individual table, they cannot be. The CRNA who skips autonomic maintenance because their track record is strong is making the same logical error as an investor who stays fully exposed to ruin-risk because their prior returns have been excellent.

The failure mode also lives at the system level. Clinical cultures that measure provider health through productivity metrics — cases per shift, hours per week — without measuring autonomic reserve metrics are optimizing the wrong variable. They are managing the average when the game is decided by the tail.

The confounds are significant: a provider who reports "feeling fine" may have suppressed autonomic reserve as Hattori demonstrated. A provider who runs on four hours of sleep may show normal psychological mood screening while their vagal brake has been depleted. Self-report is not a sufficient proxy for the physiological variable that determines absorbing-state resistance. This is one isolatable finding, stated explicitly: self-report of readiness and physiological readiness are not the same measurement.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 15 CRNAs tracked continuously for 28 days with HRV monitoring. BOLT score at the start of each shift. Nonroutine event frequency drawn from existing OR incident reporting systems.

Primary hypothesis: providers with RMSSD below one standard deviation of their own 28-day moving average on a given shift will show higher nonroutine event frequency on that same shift.

Secondary hypothesis: BOLT score below 25 seconds at shift-start correlates with suppressed RMSSD throughout the shift.

This is testable with current wearable infrastructure and existing incident reporting systems. No clinical trial. No novel instrumentation. Honest collection from infrastructure already in place.

## The Connection

Non-ergodic risk connects every concept in SRL's framework at the foundational level. [[gap-moment-training]] is the mechanism for maintaining physiological buffer between cases — not a productivity cost but a ruin-avoidance interval. [[clinician-durability]] is the career-scale expression of absorbing-state avoidance: reaching end of career without catastrophic adverse events, rather than maximizing output. [[neural-transition-failure]] maps the specific error archetypes that appear when autonomic reserve is insufficient for a state transition. [[vagal-tone]] (RMSSD) is the biomarker that indexes where on the risk curve a provider stands at any given moment.

The non-ergodic frame does not replace SRL's other concepts. It explains why they matter. This is not the science of performing better on average. It is the science of not going where you cannot come back from.

---

*The question before every case is not "how many have I done?" It is: "does my system have enough reserve to handle the one that doesn't go as planned?" Those are different questions. One looks backward at the population. The other looks forward at the path.*
