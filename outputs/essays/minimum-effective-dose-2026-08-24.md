# Notes from the Lab: Minimum Effective Dose — The Anesthesia Principle That Runs the Whole Company

*The minimum effective dose of autonomic regulation training is not measured in minutes but in repetitions — the pharmacological logic that makes five brief daily exposures produce more durable neurophysiological change than a single extended session is the same titration principle governing every drug dose a CRNA has ever administered.*

---

## The Observation

In anesthesia, there is a phrase that cannot be replaced: *titrate to effect.* It is not a reminder to be careful. It is the operating principle behind every induction. No two bodies respond identically to propofol. Some patients are asleep at 1.5 mg/kg; others require 2.5. The CRNA who adds drug incrementally — watching pupil response, jaw tension, the imperceptible settling of the masseter — is doing something categorically different from the one who enters a weight-based number and pushes. The first is reading. The second is calculating.

The same gap exists in autonomic training. Most wellness programs hand clinicians a 20-minute meditation and a suggestion to practice daily. Most clinicians do not. The field has mostly blamed motivation. The literature suggests the problem is dose architecture — not willpower, but pharmacology.

---

## The Mechanism

The minimum effective dose (MED) originates in pharmacology: the smallest quantity of a drug that produces the desired clinical effect without unnecessary side effects. Below MED, there is no therapeutic response. Above it, there is tolerance, side effect burden, or toxicity. The clinical art is finding the threshold and holding it there — adjusting, not bolusing.

Applied to behavioral intervention, MED reframes the design question. The question is no longer "how long should a session be?" It becomes: "what is the smallest unit of exposure that produces measurable change in the target system?"

Balban, Neri, Kogon, and colleagues at Stanford answered this directly in 2023. Their remote randomized controlled trial compared three 5-minute daily breathing exercises against 5 minutes of mindfulness meditation over one month, with physiological endpoints including mood, anxiety, respiratory rate, and heart rate variability. The result was unambiguous: breathwork outperformed mindfulness meditation for mood improvement and respiratory rate reduction. Cyclic sighing — double inhale through the nose, extended exhale — showed the strongest effect. Five minutes. Daily. Physiologically measurable. Balban et al. (2023) [PMID: 36630953] established that brief structured daily exposure to breathwork produces autonomous autonomic change without requiring extended sessions or clinical setting.

The mechanism beneath the measurement is the baroreflex. Lehrer, Vaschillo, and Vaschillo (2000) [PMID: 10999236] identified the cardiorespiratory resonance loop as the operative pathway. Slow breathing at an individual's resonant frequency — approximately 0.1 Hz, or five to six breaths per minute — produces maximal heart rate oscillations through baroreflex stimulation. Each breath at resonant frequency is a repetition. Each repetition trains baroreflex sensitivity. Across multiple sessions, baroreflex gain increases and resting HRV rises. The dose is small. The mechanism is precise. This is not relaxation — it is exercise of a specific neural feedback circuit.

The carry-over question follows naturally: does a single session produce any effect that persists after the session ends? Prinsloo, Derman, Lambert, and Rauch (2013) [PMID: 23435801] addressed this in a pilot RCT with 18 healthy males exposed to work-related stress. A single session of short-duration HRV biofeedback — not a course, not a protocol, one session — produced measurable carry-over into a subsequent cognitive stress task: the biofeedback group maintained higher RMSSD and HF power during the following Stroop test. The protective effect was not large. But it was real, and it appeared from a single exposure. One MED administration does something.

This sets up the frequency question: if one session creates temporary protection, how many sessions per day, distributed how widely, produce durable structural change? The surgical training literature offers a precise answer.

Spruit, Band, van der Heijden, and Hamming (2016) [PMID: 27988169] randomized novice laparoscopic trainees across four conditions: massed training on a single day, massed with breaks, massed with nap intervals, and spaced across three consecutive days. Total training time was identical across groups. Three months post-training, the spaced group retained significantly more skill, with effect sizes ranging from 0.20 to 0.37 over the massed conditions. Same dose. Different distribution. The mechanism is memory consolidation during off-task intervals — the periods between sessions are not rest, they are processing time. Frequency-plus-spacing, not duration, determines retention.

This generalizes to behavioral regulation. Chen (2026) [PMID: 41583131] conducted a three-arm RCT of online emotion regulation training comparing three daily prompts versus one daily prompt versus a psychoeducation control. Single daily prompts were sufficient for initial skill acquisition — what Chen explicitly described as the "minimal effective dose for engagement." Higher frequency (three daily prompts) produced superior long-term maintenance at three-month follow-up. The data point toward a tiered dose architecture: begin at MED to reduce burden and establish the habit; increase frequency as the system demonstrates capacity to absorb more. This is titration — behavioral drug delivery applied to emotion regulation.

The acute physiology of a minimum dose was mapped directly by Im, Lee, and Woo (2026) [PMID: 41697528] in chronically stressed caregiving mothers. During a single five-minute slow-paced breathing condition at 0.1 Hz, SDNN, TP, and LF power increased significantly — a resonance-driven reorganization of autonomic activity through baroreflex-vagal coupling. Critically, vagally mediated measures (RMSSD and HF power) remained stable rather than amplifying, indicating the acute mechanism is global autonomic coherence reorganization, not simple vagal augmentation. Anxiety and tension decreased in both the caregiver group and age-matched controls. Five minutes. No prior training. Measurable autonomic reorganization.

Several variables changed simultaneously in this study — breathing rate, pacing instruction, group assignment, and prior chronic stress exposure. Im and colleagues held duration constant across the three conditions (resting baseline, cognitive stress, slow-paced breathing) and treated group as a moderator. The consistency of the baroreflex-mediated response across both chronically stressed and non-stressed participants suggests the mechanism is general rather than population-specific.

## The Protocol

The MED protocol for somnistics is operationalized as five sessions per day, distributed across natural workflow transition points — not clustered. Each session is one NeuroMinute™: 60 seconds of coherent breathing paced at individual resonant frequency. Intensity progression follows the principle of simplest effective protocol first: resonant breathing before box breathing, box breathing before advanced multi-phase interoceptive coupling.

Measurement: RMSSD delta post-session via consumer wearable; subjective state shift (0–10) logged immediately before and after each session.

Success criterion: measurable RMSSD elevation post-session on at least four of five daily sessions, sustained across a two-week window.

---

## The Failure Mode

MED breaks in three specific conditions.

First, it fails when "brief" is used to justify incomplete presence. A 60-second resonant breathing session requires full attentional engagement — not split attention across a monitor and a breath cue. The session that runs while the clinician reviews a chart is not a session. The needle entered tissue, not the vein.

Second, the framework fails when frequency drops below the consolidation threshold or when sessions cluster rather than distribute. The spacing advantage documented by Spruit et al. requires inter-session intervals for memory consolidation. Five sessions in one morning followed by two days of none produces a massed-training pattern and loses the retention benefit.

Third, MED fails when practitioners interpret "minimum" as an endpoint rather than a floor. MED is where the dose-response curve begins producing measurable effect — not where it plateaus. Under acute stress loading, deliberate capacity-building phases, or neuroplastic targets that require deeper exposure, longer or more intensive sessions are appropriate. The CRNA titrates propofol up under the right clinical conditions. The same logic applies here.

---

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 20 CRNAs, 14 days, consumer HRV wearables. Protocol: five 60-second sessions per day, distributed across clinical workflow transition points. App-logged completion rate and session timing. Maslach Burnout Inventory at days 0 and 14.

Primary hypothesis: mean RMSSD elevation per session will exceed zero across all participants by day 3, confirming physiological dose response to a single MED exposure.

Secondary hypothesis: participants completing five distributed sessions per day will show greater 14-day RMSSD growth than participants completing equivalent total practice time in one or two longer sessions (frequency-spacing effect).

This test is feasible at small scale with existing consumer technology. The four-variable confound to monitor: baseline HRV (high-HRV participants may show smaller acute delta), session context (between cases vs. pre-case may differ), session completion rate (non-adherence is not treatment failure, it is dose data), and wearable recording reliability (cross-validate a random 10% of sessions with ECG).
