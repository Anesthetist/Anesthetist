# Notes from the Lab: Why High Performers Go Numb

*Clinical training doesn't merely fail to teach interoception — it actively disables it through a hidden curriculum of body-signal suppression, and what we call burnout is largely the predictable autonomic failure of a nervous system whose primary feedback channel has been methodically extinguished.*

---

## The Observation

Somewhere in a CRNA's third year of clinical training, the belly stops moving.

Not because anything breaks. Because the system learns that belly-signals are irrelevant.

Consider the picture: a CRNA five years post-graduation, forty-hour weeks, monitoring her patient's arterial line with hawk-like precision — MAP trending toward 62, phenylephrine already in hand. But ask her to feel her own diaphragm — to report whether she is breathing into her chest or her abdomen — and she goes blank. The body she trained three years to surveil externally has become invisible internally.

This isn't a personality deficit or a failure of character. It is curriculum.

The hidden curriculum of clinical education transmits a coherent set of body-signal instructions, passed down through nursing programs and residencies and anesthesia schools like unspoken law: don't eat on shift; don't leave the bedside to use the restroom; don't show fatigue; don't show fear; don't stop. The explicit curriculum teaches physiology. The hidden curriculum teaches physiology-suppression — applied to the self. Over years of clinical training, this suppression becomes invisible because it works. The clinician who powers through hunger, bladder urgency, and fear without losing composure is rewarded, promoted, trusted. The body learns to be quiet.

The trouble with training a body to be quiet is that it goes quiet for everything.

---

## The Mechanism

Stephan and colleagues (2016) [PMID: 27895566] proposed a hierarchical Bayesian framework that reframes interoception not as passive body-awareness but as active prediction: the brain continuously generates a generative model of visceral states, and interoception is the continuous testing of that model against incoming signals. When predictions match — when the brain's model of the body's state is accurate — the system runs efficiently. When predictions fail chronically, the brain registers what Stephan calls "dyshomeostasis": a sustained discrepancy between expected and actual internal state. From there, the framework draws a direct mechanistic line to fatigue and depression.

Here is what chronic suppression does inside this framework. When a clinician is trained to ignore hunger, they are not merely ignoring a sensation. They are training the brain to generate predictions that exclude that class of signal from relevance. Over years, the prior probability assigned to "my body's signals matter" decreases. The interoceptive predictive model becomes coarser — less sensitive to deviation, less responsive to early-warning signals before they become disruptive.

Stephan's critical clinical proposition is that the brain monitors its own interoceptive performance through a metacognitive layer that updates beliefs about "allostatic self-efficacy": the brain's assessed capacity to regulate its own bodily states. When chronic suppression degrades this capacity — when the body keeps sending signals the model has been trained to dismiss — allostatic self-efficacy decreases. Fatigue emerges first (an adaptive withdrawal response). If the chronic dyshomeostasis continues, the result is a generalized belief of low self-efficacy and loss of control. Stephan identifies this as the computational signature of depression. The clinical literature calls the same state "burnout." The mechanism is identical.

This is not stress causing burnout through workload. It is systematic disabling of the feedback loop through which the nervous system monitors and corrects its own state.

Three evidence lines support the components of this mechanism:

**Line 1: Interoceptive accuracy buffers autonomic stress reactivity.**
Rapp and colleagues (2026) [PMID: 41672296] subjected 83 participants to a modified Trier Social Stress Test and measured interoceptive accuracy (IAcc, via heartbeat counting task) alongside heart rate, skin conductance, and state anxiety. Higher IAcc was associated with a *smaller* heart rate increase during social stress — an autonomic counter-regulatory process. The body that is more precisely self-perceiving mounts a more calibrated stress response. Note the confound: this was a non-clinical sample under acute laboratory stress, and cognitive reappraisal capacity varied simultaneously. The specific claim that clinical training reduces interoceptive accuracy is the suppression hypothesis's original contribution, not a finding from this paper. But the directionality is consistent with ISH's predictions: less interoceptive accuracy → less autonomic counter-regulation → more vulnerability to state drift under load.

**Line 2: Interoceptive capacity is trainable, and suppressed populations gain most.**
Bornemann, Herbert, Mehling, and Singer (2015) [PMID: 25610410] enrolled 148 participants in a 3-month contemplative training program using Body Scan and Breath Meditation, measuring all eight MAIA dimensions pre and post. Five of eight dimensions improved significantly. The strongest gains appeared in the regulatory aspects of interoceptive awareness — how the body is used for self-regulation in daily life. Critically: participants with the lowest interoceptive awareness at baseline showed the largest gains. If the suppression hypothesis is correct — that clinical training systematically produces low MAIA scorers — this finding implies that clinicians represent the population with the most to recover, not the population with the least to gain.

Suppressed capacity is not erased capacity. It can be recovered. The asymmetry matters for the intervention argument.

**Line 3: Interoceptive awareness and burnout co-move under intervention.**
Matiz and colleagues (2020) [PMID: 32899739] measured interoceptive awareness (MAIA), emotional exhaustion, anxiety, and depression in 66 Italian female teachers before and after an 8-week mindfulness course. Both interoceptive awareness and emotional exhaustion (burnout's core subscale) improved significantly in the same intervention. The low-resilience subgroup — participants with the most depleted baseline — showed the greatest improvement. The co-movement doesn't establish causality, but it is consistent with a shared underlying mechanism: interoceptive capacity and burnout resistance are coupled through the same autonomic infrastructure.

The institutional layer makes this structural, not incidental. Runyan (2017) [PMID: 29283621], writing in *Families, Systems & Health*, directly identified what medical training withholds: formal curricula contain no resilience skills, no mindfulness, no emotional-regulation tools, no instruction in how to maintain personal wellbeing under chronic occupational stress. This is stated as structural critique, not observation. The knowledge exists. Training does not transmit it — and, through the hidden curriculum, trains against it.

The consequence is measurable in anesthesia populations. Trainor and colleagues (2025) [PMID: 39945152] studied 50 anesthesia providers — CRNAs, physician anesthesiologists, and anesthesiologist assistants — and found a statistically significant inverse correlation between emotional intelligence and burnout (p = .022). The more internally aware of emotional states a provider was, the less likely they were to meet burnout criteria. Day and colleagues (2022) [PMID: 36413190] found that among student nurse anesthetists (n = 530), more clinical training hours correlated with higher disengagement scores and total burnout scores. Greater clinical exposure produced more burnout, not less. That is the suppression curriculum's signature: not declining burnout as competence grows, but increasing burnout as interoceptive training accelerates.

## The Protocol

If the mechanism is suppression of interoceptive capacity, the intervention is its restoration — not meditation as stress management, not resilience training as cognitive reframing, but the targeted reopening of the specific physiological channel that clinical education closed.

Structured interoceptive training for clinical populations follows this sequence:

**1. Baseline assessment (Week 1).** MAIA-2 administered in standardized conditions — all eight subscales scored. Five-minute resting HRV (RMSSD). Diaphragmatic engagement test: can the clinician feel whether they are breathing into the chest or belly without tactile feedback?

**2. Signal restoration (Weeks 1–3).** Body-scan practices targeting the specific deficit identified by MAIA-2. For most clinicians, this means Noticing (awareness of body signals) and Body Listening (actively attending to internal cues). Simple breath awareness with haptic feedback — one hand sternum, one hand abdomen. Duration: 6 minutes daily, not 45. Minimum effective dose applied to the instrument itself.

**3. Regulation bridging (Weeks 4–8).** Connecting interoceptive signal to volitional regulation. [[gap-moment-training|Gap Moment Training™]] micro-interventions inserted at natural clinical workflow transitions — case handoffs, room turnovers, pre-induction pauses. The clinician doesn't leave the clinical environment. They practice state-monitoring within it.

**4. Remeasurement.** MAIA-2 and HRV at 8 weeks. Primary predicted improvement: Noticing and Body Listening subscales. Secondary: RMSSD increase. Burnout measures (MBI or Oldenburg Burnout Inventory) measured in parallel.

Success is defined as MAIA-2 Noticing subscale increasing ≥ 0.5 SD from baseline. Failure is defined as no change in Noticing subscale after 8 weeks of consistent practice — which would suggest either the practice dose was insufficient or the training-injury hypothesis is wrong in its predictions.

## The Failure Mode

The suppression hypothesis misspecifies in one predictable direction: it overstates homogeneity.

Not all clinicians suppress equally. There is a likely subpopulation of high-sensation-seekers whose interoceptive channel remains partially open under training pressure — clinicians who use body signals to guide clinical judgment, who develop fine-grained self-awareness despite the hidden curriculum. This subpopulation burns out for different reasons: moral injury, organizational dysfunction, workload without agency. The suppression hypothesis explains one burnout pathway, not the whole landscape.

The hypothesis also risks a clinical application error: assuming that restoring interoception will resolve burnout automatically. It will not, if the workplace conditions that produced depletion remain unchanged. A clinician with restored interoceptive awareness may simply detect their exhaustion more accurately without having greater capacity to act on it. The restoration of a feedback channel does not generate the resources that feedback channel reports as depleted.

Interoceptive training without recovery conditions is a tool for knowing how far underwater you are. Not the same as surfacing.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 12 CRNAs at a single clinical site, randomized to interoceptive training (6 minutes daily MAIA-guided body-scan practice + weekly 10-minute GMT session) versus waitlist control, over 8 weeks.

Measures:
- MAIA-2 (all eight subscales), pre and post
- Five-minute resting HRV (RMSSD), pre and post
- Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OBI), pre and post
- Diaphragmatic engagement accuracy test, pre and post

Primary hypothesis: Noticing and Body Listening subscales increase ≥ 0.5 SD in the intervention group. Secondary: RMSSD increases ≥ 5ms. The prediction is testable with a wrist wearable, a validated self-report instrument, and twelve people willing to spend six minutes a day attending to what their bodies are already saying.
