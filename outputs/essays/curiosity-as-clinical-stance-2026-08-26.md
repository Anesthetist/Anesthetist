# Notes from the Lab: Curiosity as Clinical Stance

*Curiosity — deployed as a deliberate attitudinal stance toward internal arousal — converts sympathetic threat signals into processable data, recruits prefrontal broadening, and directly counters the rumination-defeat cycle that predicts PTSD in trauma-exposed clinicians.*

## The Observation

It is 2:00 AM. A trauma case has arrived — motor vehicle accident, catastrophic injuries, alcohol involved. The ARNP who leads innovation for 1,500 clinicians across an entire health system is about to walk in. Before going through the doors, he opens an app and takes sixty seconds.

He is not trying to calm down. He is not trying to feel less. He is, in a very precise sense, getting curious.

The case will demand everything: rapid assessment, autonomous clinical judgment, the management of multiple collapsing physiological systems under conditions that did not exist when his circadian rhythm said sleep. His body is already doing what bodies do — heart rate climbing, cortisol mobilizing, the full sympathetic cascade that evolution designed for exactly this kind of moment.

The question is not whether the arousal happens. It always does. The question is what he calls it.

"Interesting" is a different call than "I'm losing control."

That distinction — between threat and data — is not metaphor. It is mechanism. And it is trainable.

## The Mechanism

Barbara Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory, first published in 2001, identified curiosity as one of the prototype positive emotions — along with joy, love, and contentment — that broaden momentary thought-action repertoires and build enduring personal resources. Where fear narrows attention to the specific threat and anxiety tunnels cognition to worst-case scenarios, curiosity opens the cognitive aperture. "What is this?" replaces "What's wrong with me?" Fredrickson (2001) argued this broadening is not merely pleasant — it is the mechanism by which positive emotions build the psychological infrastructure that negative emotions spend.

The experimental evidence is direct. In a landmark study, Fredrickson and Branigan (2005) induced either anxiety or positive emotion in participants and then assessed the scope of their attention and thought-action repertoires. Anxiety narrowed both. Positive emotional states — including the curious, interested state — broadened them significantly. This is not a subtle finding: the same arousal interpreted as threat versus data produces measurably different cognitive fields. The person walking into the trauma bay under anxious threat-framing literally cannot perceive as much as the person walking in with a curious data-gathering frame.

There is a cardiovascular corollary. Fredrickson, Mancuso, Branigan, and Tugade (2000) documented what they called the undoing effect: positive emotions — even brief ones, even contentment and amusement — produce significantly faster cardiovascular recovery from anxiety-induced reactivity than neutral or negative states. The body is doing the arithmetic. When the arousal is labeled as interesting rather than dangerous, the parasympathetic system re-engages sooner. Heart rate recovers. The baroreflex comes back online. The CRNA is, physiologically, ready for the next decision faster.

The mechanism linking curiosity to this recovery runs through three steps. First, the interoceptive reframe: curiosity converts internal arousal signals from threat ("I'm failing") to data ("My sympathetic system is doing what it's supposed to do right now"). This is not suppression — the arousal is fully present — but the meaning assigned to it changes the trajectory. Second, prefrontal recruitment: the curious stance engages dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the same regions responsible for cognitive flexibility and executive control. The clinician who is curious about their crisis state has more prefrontal resources available than the one who is fighting it. Third, what Garland, Farb, Goldin, and Fredrickson (2015) called the upward spiral: curiosity leads to attentional broadening, which leads to more flexible appraisal, which leads to greater capacity for meaning-making and regulated action. Each element generates the next. The spiral rises rather than tightens.

Garland and Fredrickson (2019) extended this framework to show that the upward spiral eventually reaches what they call self-transcendence — a state where the subject-object boundary softens, absorptive experience deepens, and the person is, paradoxically, most effective precisely because self-monitoring has quieted. This is the state Randy has mapped as [[nondual-clinical-awareness]]: the CRNA who observes the clinical field without the "am I doing this right?" loop stealing bandwidth. Curiosity is the doorway. The gap moment is where you choose to walk through it.

The failure path is equally well-documented, and equally important to name. Kleim, Ehlers, and Glucksman (2007), in a prospective longitudinal study of assault survivors, identified the cognitive predictors of chronic PTSD. Rumination — the repetitive, passive cycling of distress without resolution — was among the strongest. Mental defeat — a cognitive state of complete submission to the stressor, a sense that the self has been annihilated by what happened — was the strongest single predictor of all. These are the states that curiosity directly counters. Rumination asks "why did this happen to me?" in a loop with no exit. Curiosity asks "what is happening in my body right now?" with an exit built in: the answer. Mental defeat says "I am what this crisis is doing to me." Curiosity says "I am the person observing what this crisis is doing to me." The gap between those two positions is the gap between PTSD vulnerability and resilience.

## The Protocol

Curiosity as clinical stance is deployed in the gap moment — the 60 seconds before entering a room, between cases, at the handoff. The practice has three moves:

1. **Notice the arousal without naming it as a problem.** Heart rate elevated. Breath shallow. Tension in the jaw. These are data points. The practice is to feel them without immediately trying to change them. "What's happening in my body right now?" asked with genuine interest, not dread.

2. **Assign a curious interpretation rather than a threat interpretation.** "Interesting — this is what my sympathetic system does before a high-stakes case. Same system that has carried me through every case before this one." This is not positive self-talk. It is an accurate re-labeling. The system is functioning; the interpretation is the variable.

3. **Use the information the body is providing.** What does the elevated arousal tell you? It may signal that you need one more breath before going in. It may tell you that the prior case is still metabolizing. It may confirm that you are appropriately calibrated for a high-acuity patient. Curiosity completes the interoceptive loop: receive, interpret, respond.

What constitutes success: the clinician exits the gap moment with a broader attentional field, not a quieter one. The arousal may be identical. The cognitive capacity will be measurably larger. What constitutes failure: the gap moment is skipped because "there is no time" — which is the moment when the failure mode is most likely.

## The Failure Mode

Curiosity fails when the signal is genuinely dangerous and the stance becomes dissociation. A clinician who is curious about chest pain without moving to evaluation has mistaken a clinical error for a regulatory strategy. The practice requires a prior commitment: curiosity is the stance for internal signals, not a substitute for clinical response to external ones.

Curiosity also fails under extreme cognitive load or sleep debt. The prefrontal resources that enable the curious reframe are finite and depleted by fatigue. This is the irony: the moments when the stance is most needed — night shifts, multi-case days, after adverse events — are the moments when it is hardest to access. This is why the practice must be trained at low load so that it becomes automated enough to deploy at high load. The 14-day HRV discipline that SRL's research model tracks is precisely this: building the resting baseline from which the stance becomes accessible under pressure.

The third failure mode is intellectualization — thinking *about* arousal rather than *through* it. A clinician who can give a lecture on broaden-and-build theory while walking into a case still frozen in threat-mode has learned the vocabulary without installing the capacity. Curiosity is embodied or it is nothing. The move is somatic before it is cognitive: feel the chest, then ask what it is doing, then allow a different interpretation. The body leads.

## The Test

A minimum viable study: 20 CRNAs randomized to 14 days of gap moment practice, half with standard HRV-paced breathing and half with an explicit curiosity-stance instruction added ("Before entering each gap moment, ask: what is happening in my body right now? Treat every signal as data, not danger"). Measure MAIA-2 Noticing and Emotional Awareness subscales pre/post. Measure RMSSD recovery speed after a standardized stress induction (cold pressor or surgical simulation). Measure rumination score (Ruminative Thinking Style scale) pre/post. If curiosity-stance produces faster RMSSD recovery and lower rumination scores than breath practice alone, the mechanism is confirmed. The test is 14 days. The outcome is measurable. The cost is one line added to the NeuroMinute instruction.

## The Connection

Curiosity-as-clinical-stance sits at the intersection of every SRL training layer. It is the attitudinal foundation for [[anterocept]] progression: you cannot move through the Anterocept domains without genuine curiosity about what each domain reveals. It is what transforms [[interoceptive-literacy]] from a skill into a practice — literacy without curiosity is inventory, not intelligence. It is what makes [[gap-moment-training]] something other than a stress management break: the gap moment is a container; curiosity is what you do inside it. And it is the closest cognitive analogue to [[awe-as-regulation]] — awe handles identity-level regulation (the self becomes small, the world becomes vast), while curiosity handles arousal-level regulation (the threat becomes data, the body becomes instrument). Together they constitute what it means to be genuinely present in a high-stakes clinical moment.

The body already knows how to be curious. It is built for exploration. The problem is that clinical training systematically replaces curiosity with certainty-seeking — the protocol, the algorithm, the decision tree. SRL's intervention is not adding something foreign. It is restoring what was there before the training covered it up.
