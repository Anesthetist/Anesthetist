# Notes from the Lab: Titrate to Effect — Why Every Nervous System Has Its Own Dose

*The principle of titrate-to-effect — observe the physiological response, then adjust the intervention — is not a metaphor borrowed from anesthesia pharmacology; it is the only epistemological structure capable of matching the nervous system's individual response variation, and any autonomic training protocol that substitutes population averages for observed response produces either sub-therapeutic habituation or overstimulation without adaptation.*

---

## The Observation

In the operating room, there is no such thing as "the standard propofol dose." There is a starting estimate — 1.5–2.5 mg/kg for induction — and then there is the patient in front of you. The CRNA watches. Heart rate drops fifteen beats: back off. Blood pressure holds: continue. The monitor is not a diagnosis; it is real-time feedback, and the drug delivery adjusts in response.

This is not rigid protocol. This is physiology being respected.

Schwilden and Schüttler (2008) formalized this as target-controlled infusion (TCI): a system in which pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK-PD) models estimate effect-site drug concentration and adjust infusion rates continuously [PMID: 18175103]. The key distinction they drew was between open-loop dosing — where past output does not influence future input — and adaptive closed-loop delivery, where the observed response feeds back into the next dose calculation. TCI was a step toward closed-loop. The direction of travel was unambiguous: watch the response, change the dose.

In somnistics training, every breathing protocol begins with a population estimate: start near 5.5 breaths per minute. But resonant frequency — the breathing rate at which HRV amplitude is maximized — varies across individuals from 4.5 to 6.5 BPM. Body composition, age, cardiac output, and baroreflex sensitivity all shift where that peak lands. A 58-year-old CRNA and a 31-year-old emergency department nurse will find their resonant peaks in different places. The starting frequency is a dose estimate, not a prescription.

## The Mechanism

The pharmacological case for titration rests on interindividual variation. Zhu and colleagues (2026) demonstrated this precisely in a study of remimazolam sedation across pediatric age groups: even after establishing age-stratified ED95 values — the dose effective in 95% of the population — they concluded that "individualized dose titration remains essential, particularly given the 17.5% incidence of bradycardia observed in toddlers" [PMID: 42292147]. The ED95 is not a prescription; it is a calibrated starting point. The patient's response is the actual datum.

The same principle operates in autonomic training. Leganes-Fonteneau and colleagues (2021) demonstrated that individual differences in baroreflex sensitivity determine how much cardiovascular resonance a given breathing protocol produces [PMID: 34534600]. In a two-session study of 67 participants, those who entered the resonance-breathing protocol with different baseline high-frequency HRV and baroreflex sensitivity showed meaningfully different magnitudes of change in interoceptive accuracy. The protocol was identical across subjects; the responses were not. Critically, the authors found a *negative* relationship between baseline HF-HRV and interoceptive accuracy — higher resting HRV correlated with lower interoceptive sensitivity at baseline. This contradicts the naive assumption that more HRV is always better. Where a person starts determines where the protocol takes them.

This is the mechanism: every nervous system has its own dose-response curve. The curve varies by individual, by time of day, by physiological state, and by accumulated training history. A protocol that does not observe the response and adjust — that delivers the same 5 minutes at 6 BPM every day regardless of effect — is pharmacologically analogous to continuous fixed-rate anesthetic infusion: it will land correctly for some, underdose others, and overshoot a third group without the clinician ever knowing which category a given patient belongs to.

Three variables determine where the effective dose falls:

1. **Resonant frequency** — the breathing rate at which HRV amplitude peaks. Found empirically through biofeedback, not read from population tables.
2. **Baroreflex sensitivity** — how strongly the cardiovascular system responds to oscillatory stimulation. Varies with conditioning, age, and autonomic baseline state.
3. **State at time of dosing** — a protocol effective at rest produces a different response under cognitive load. The clinically relevant dose is not the dose that works in a quiet laboratory; it is the dose that works when the CRNA needs it.

**Established science:** PK-PD modeling demonstrates that fixed-dose protocols produce variable effect-site concentrations across individuals (Schwilden & Schüttler, 2008). Resonance breathing produces variable cardiovascular and interoceptive responses depending on individual baroreflex function (Leganes-Fonteneau et al., 2021).

**The SRL claim that goes beyond the established literature:** The titrate-to-effect loop — baseline measure, apply stimulus, observe response, adjust — is the correct epistemological structure for autonomic training delivery. Current "5 minutes at 5.5 BPM" recommendations are structurally equivalent to running every surgical patient on the same propofol pump rate. The response is the data.

## The Protocol

The titration loop for somnistics:

1. **Establish baseline.** Measure resting RMSSD for 5 minutes before beginning. This is the pre-dose state.
2. **Apply starting stimulus.** Begin at 5.5 BPM — the population-level resonance estimate. Run for 3 minutes.
3. **Observe response.** Post-breathing RMSSD should rise from baseline by at least 15–20%. If not, the dose is not landing.
4. **Adjust.** If RMSSD change is absent: shift frequency ±0.5 BPM, extend session by 2 minutes, or add a body anchor (cardiac-anchored breathing layer). Repeat observation.
5. **Log the effective range.** Over 5–7 sessions, an individual's effective resonant frequency range becomes apparent. This becomes their calibrated dose.
6. **Titrate state-specifically.** Run a separate protocol during a high-load task simulation. The effective dose under load differs from the resting effective dose. Track both.

**Success:** RMSSD increases ≥15% from pre-session baseline AND the individual can maintain the response without a pacer — the internalized protocol. **Failure:** RMSSD change ≤5%, or consistent drops at certain frequency ranges.

Gross and colleagues (2016) documented that resonant frequency training can be internalized: practitioners learned to generate the resonance response *without* a breath pacer [PMID: 26782084]. HRV increased significantly across SDNN, RMSSD, and low-frequency power after five practitioner-led sessions. This finding matters beyond technique acquisition: when a CRNA can self-generate resonance without a pacer in a clinical environment, they have internalized not just the dose but the dosing loop itself. The practitioner becomes the feedback system.

## The Failure Mode

**The individualization trap.** Titrate-to-effect requires a starting estimate. The failure mode is treating "everyone is different" as a reason to avoid establishing any protocol — infinite personalization that never commits. Population averages are the starting dose, not the endpoint. Without a protocol to titrate against, there is nothing to adjust.

**Subjective tolerance mistaken for therapeutic effect.** In pharmacology, this is the opioid tolerance problem: the patient reports comfort at a dose that is no longer pharmacologically active. In autonomic training, this manifests as "the breathing feels easy now" without any HRV improvement. Comfort is not the biomarker; HRV response is. A protocol to which the nervous system has habituated loses its adaptive signal while appearing to be functioning normally.

**Ignoring the confound of state variation.** Baroreflex sensitivity and HRV are not stable across a clinical day. They shift across circadian phases, across shift length, and across sleep quality. A resonant frequency calibrated during a morning baseline session may underperform during an afternoon post-12-hour-shift context. Titration is not a one-time calibration; it is a practice of ongoing observation. A dose established in September may need revision by December.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 24 CRNAs, 3 weeks.

- **Group A (n=12):** Fixed protocol. All practitioners breathe at 5.5 BPM, 5 minutes, once daily.
- **Group B (n=12):** Titrated protocol. Session 1 establishes individual resonant frequency via 10-minute frequency-sweep biofeedback. Subsequent sessions use the individually calibrated rate, with weekly re-assessment.

**Measures:** RMSSD pre/post each session; RMSSD baseline trend across 21 days; self-reported protocol compliance; day-14 in-scenario HRV response during a simulated clinical stressor.

**Hypothesis:** Group B shows greater RMSSD improvement and higher protocol compliance by day 14. Secondary: Group B maintains greater HRV during the simulated stressor.

The distributed practice literature supports the dosing schedule. Kate and colleagues (2023) found that distributed practice produced comparable or superior neuroplasticity outcomes — measured by BDNF levels — compared to massed practice, with the authors noting that "appropriate timing along with optimal dosage became an active ingredient in functional recovery" [PMID: 38746529]. Five minutes daily outperforms thirty minutes weekly not despite smaller individual doses but because the frequency of the stimulus matters as much as its magnitude. This is dose-response logic applied to time.

This study is testable at SRL scale — 24 participants, consumer HRV monitors, 3 weeks, no clinical oversight required for a wellness protocol comparison.
