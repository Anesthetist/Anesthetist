# Notes from the Lab: The Filter You Cannot See — Relevance Realization and the Architecture of Clinical Intuition

*Relevance realization — the pre-conscious process by which the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex select what matters in a given moment — is the unrecognized substrate of clinical intuition, and its failure under sympathetic overload explains why experienced CRNAs miss signals their body registered before their attention arrived.*

---

## The Observation

The induction goes sideways. You are running the algorithm — bag-mask ventilation, suction, repositioning — and three minutes later, when the room stabilizes, your attending asks: "Did you notice her saturation was drifting for about ninety seconds before the desaturation event?"

You didn't. The number was on the monitor. Your eyes passed over it. Your brain, running under full sympathetic load, did not assign it salience. It was not filtered out by lack of knowledge. The problem was something earlier and less visible: the machinery that decides what gets noticed in the first place had already narrowed.

This is not an attentional failure in the classic sense. It is a relevance realization failure. And it is the invisible architecture beneath every near-miss, every premature closure, every experienced clinician who "missed something obvious" in a complex case.

---

## The Mechanism

John Vervaeke, philosopher and cognitive scientist at the University of Toronto, developed the framework of relevance realization to describe what he calls the "hard problem of relevance" — the question of how, from an effectively infinite field of possible stimuli, a finite brain decides what matters right now. This is not a trivial calculation. It is not conscious. And according to the available neuroscience, it is done primarily by two structures that clinicians rarely think about: the anterior insula and the anterior cingulate cortex.

Menon and Uddin, in a 2010 theoretical review in *Brain Structure & Function*, proposed what remains the most parsimonious account of how the brain implements relevance realization neurally. Menon and Uddin described a "salience network" anchored by the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex, whose core function is "to segregate the most relevant among internal and extrapersonal stimuli in order to guide behavior." The network operates through four linked mechanisms: bottom-up detection of salient events; switching between large-scale networks to direct attentional and working memory resources toward whatever is most relevant; modulation of autonomic reactivity to detected signals; and rapid coupling to motor systems when response is required. Menon and Uddin's model was not primarily a model of attention — it was a model of relevance selection. The salience network is the brain's implementation of what Vervaeke calls the relevance-selection problem [PMID: 20512370].

The anatomy of this selection is precise. Cauda and colleagues, in a 2012 meta-analysis in *NeuroImage* that aggregated 1,305 imaging studies involving 22,872 subjects, found that the anterior insula is organized into an attentional-cognitive hub anteriorly and an interoceptive-emotional hub posteriorly. Cauda et al. established that the anterior cluster is "characterized by an attentional pattern of connectivity with frontal, cingulate, parietal, cerebellar and anterior insular highly connected areas" and is "mostly activated by cognition," while the posterior insula is "mostly activated by interoception, perception and emotion." The relevance network is not purely cognitive or purely somatic — it is the interface between them. The anterior insula is where interoceptive signal meets top-down allocation [PMID: 22521480].

This interface is not static. Yao and colleagues (2017), in a pharmaco-fMRI study published in *Neuropsychopharmacology*, demonstrated that the right anterior insula actively regulates the trade-off between internal (interoceptive) signal processing and external social salience. When external salient stimuli were present, Yao et al. found that the right anterior insula increased its functional connectivity with the posterior insula in a manner that was negatively correlated with interoceptive accuracy — that is, as the network increased its attentional coupling toward external events, it simultaneously suppressed internal body signals. The mechanism is competitive, not additive. External salience decreases interoceptive signal access at the neural level [PMID: 28836577].

This competitive architecture is the hardware-level explanation for a clinical observation that CRNAs know from inside: when the case gets complex, you lose yourself. The saturation drift that you "should have noticed" was not processed at the level of awareness because the anterior insula was already routing maximum resources to the external salience field — the surgeon's body language, the hemodynamic instability, the equipment alarm. The internal signal was registered by the body and suppressed by the network before it reached attention.

**The novel claim here is not about attention.** Attention is downstream. The novel claim is that this relevance-selection failure can be trained against, and that the training target is the salience network's sensitivity to interoceptive input — not cognitive techniques, not checklists, not "remember to look at the sat." The intervention must be at the level of the network's calibration, or it will not hold under load.

The stress dimension makes this more specific. Zareyan and colleagues (2021), in a carefully designed crossover study published in *Cerebral Cortex*, demonstrated that acute mild social stress impairs executive function (including selective attention) in a genotype-dependent manner through prefrontal dopamine dysregulation. Zareyan et al. found that "the bandwidth for stress having a facilitative effect on EFs [executive functions] is exceedingly narrow" and that for most individuals, even mild stress degrades the cognitive control systems that regulate which signals get processed. Selective attention — the downstream product of relevance selection — is among the first executive functions to degrade under load. This means the CRNA in a complex case is operating with a salience network that is simultaneously more activated by external events and less capable of precisely filtering them [PMID: 33124661].

The clinical intuition literature confirms the behavioral consequences. Mansoori and colleagues (2025), in a cognitive task analysis of resident physicians making high-stakes fluid resuscitation decisions in a real ICU, found that clinical intuition in novices and trainees is heavily shaped by "cognitive biases" including premature closure, confirmation bias, and "status quo bias." Using Klein's Recognition-Primed Decision model as their framework, Mansoori et al. documented that trainees frequently used volume-based heuristics that overrode evidence-based predictors of fluid responsiveness — a direct demonstration of relevance realization failure, where prior-pattern salience suppresses incoming signal relevance [PMID: 40570242]. Expert intuition works when pattern-recognition systems are calibrated to the actual signal structure of the domain; it fails when they are running on outdated or stress-distorted templates.

## The Protocol

Relevance realization is not a thing you do. It is a thing the brain is always doing, with varying precision. Training it requires improving the sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio of the anterior insula's input — specifically its interoceptive arm — so that body signals compete more effectively against external salience under load.

The protocol is therefore not about cognition. It is about body literacy first.

**Domain 1: Interoceptive baseline.** Before any complex case, take two minutes to conduct what the vault calls an [[anterocept]] scan — a systematic sweep of breath, pulse, tension, and arousal state. Not to change anything, but to establish what you have before the external salience field takes over. This is the calibration step.

**Domain 2: Anchor signals in the body.** The Gap Moment training architecture specifically targets state transitions — the moments between cases, between cases and hallway conversations, between hallway and next induction — as the training windows. In these windows, the external salience load drops, and the brain's relevance network can be briefly retrained through structured interoceptive attention. Sixty seconds. One breath cycle. The minimum effective dose for salience network recalibration.

**Domain 3: During the case.** A single breath-cycle of diaphragmatic breathing during a stable moment is not relaxation. It is a deliberate input to the salience network. Structured breathing activates the posterior insula → anterior insula pathway and temporarily shifts the network's sensitivity balance toward interoceptive signal access. This is not tested directly in the CRNA population. It is a mechanistic prediction based on the Yao et al. architecture, and it should be tested.

---

## The Failure Mode

Relevance realization training fails when it is delivered as a cognitive overlay on an overloaded system. The most common misapplication: "be mindful during the case" — a directive that adds cognitive load without changing the underlying network calibration. The intervention must precede the high-salience event, not accompany it.

The second failure mode is individual. Van der Velden and colleagues (2022), in a randomized controlled fMRI study in *Biological Psychiatry*, showed that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy changed salience network connectivity during ruminative states, specifically improving "the ability to sustain and control attention to body sensations." Van der Velden et al. found this effect mediated clinical improvement — but the study population was patients with recurrent depression, not high-performing clinicians. The training transfer to expert populations under real operational stress has not been established. This is the central gap [PMID: 36328822].

The failure mode therefore is: applying a training protocol calibrated for clinical populations to high-performing non-patient populations without studying the ceiling and floor effects. Expert CRNAs may already have exceptional salience network function in low-load conditions. The question is whether training produces gains precisely when they are most needed — under maximal external salience load.

---

## The Test

**Minimum viable experiment:** Fourteen CRNAs (n=14, powered for a medium effect size). Baseline: MAIA-2 (interoceptive awareness), heartbeat discrimination task (interoceptive accuracy), and a flanker task (selective attention) at rest. Then: a simulated high-fidelity crisis scenario in a simulation center. Flanker task again, immediately post-simulation (under residual cortisol load). This gives a within-subject measure of stress-induced attentional narrowing. Then: 14-day [[gap-moment-training]] NeuroMinute protocol. Repeat the entire sequence.

Primary outcome: does the 14-day protocol reduce the post-simulation flanker task degradation? Secondary: do MAIA-2 scores correlate with performance resilience under load?

This is testable with existing simulation infrastructure. It is not a clinical trial. It is a 14-day proof test with wearables and a validated cognitive battery.

## The Connection

Relevance realization sits at the intersection of every SRL concept that matters clinically. The [[gap-moment-training]] framework identifies transition moments as training opportunities; gap moment detection is relevance realization applied to workflow structure — the ability to recognize that *this* transition is a trainable window. [[Anterocept]] corrects the interoceptive input that feeds the salience network. [[Self-remembering]] maintains dual awareness that keeps the relevance filter from collapsing entirely into task. [[Cognitive-variability-analysis]] can detect when the filter is running narrow — when HRV coherence drops and EEG cognitive metrics show attentional compression.

What this essay adds to the vault: relevance realization is not a philosophical concept floating above the clinical world. It is a measurable neural process implemented by specific anatomy, degradable by specific conditions, and trainable through specific interventions. The gap between Vervaeke's cognitive science and the anesthesia OR is shorter than it looks from either end.

---

*The monitor showed the number the whole time. What didn't show was the filter that decided it didn't matter — and that filter is the skill nobody trains.*
