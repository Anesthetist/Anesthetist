# Notes from the Lab: Awareness Is the Training

*Directed interoceptive awareness is not preparation for autonomic training — it is the training: wherever conscious attention lands on physiological signal, the corresponding neural circuit opens for structural modification, making awareness the primary lever of somatic neuroplasticity.*

---

## The Observation

There is a moment in the first week of the NeuroMinute curriculum that every clinician reaches: they direct attention to the diaphragm — deliberately, for perhaps the first time in years — and something shifts. Not because they performed a technique. Because they looked.

The breath was already happening. The diaphragm was already contracting, the vagal afferents were already firing, the baroreflex loop was already cycling. The body had been running its entire parallel operating system, unobserved, for the length of the career. What changed in that moment was not the physiology.

What changed was who was watching it.

The question this essay addresses is deceptively simple: does it matter whether you watch? The answer from fifteen years of neuroimaging research is that it matters enormously — not because awareness adds a layer on top of physiological process, but because awareness IS the training. Conscious attention directed at somatic signal is not the preparation for neuroplastic change. It is the biochemical event that initiates it.

---

## The Mechanism

The foundational principle is Hebbian: neurons that fire together, wire together. Donald Hebb's 1949 rule describes the cellular mechanism of learning — synaptic connections strengthen in proportion to the correlation of pre- and postsynaptic activity. For any neural circuit to strengthen, both nodes must activate simultaneously.

The implication for interoceptive training is this: the afferent signal from the diaphragm has been arriving at the insular cortex for twenty years. The circuit exists. But without attention — without the cortical engagement that directs resources toward decoding that signal — the postsynaptic activity is too weak to meet the Hebbian threshold. The signal arrives. No wire forms. The circuit stays quiet.

Awareness changes the postsynaptic side of the equation. When a clinician directs conscious attention at the diaphragmatic signal, the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and somatomotor cortex activate together. The Hebbian gate opens. The circuit becomes available for structural modification.

This is not theory. The imaging evidence supports it with unusual consistency across study designs.

Farb, Segal, and Anderson demonstrated in 2012 that Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction — a structured interoceptive attention protocol — altered cortical representations of interoceptive attention in the insula after eight weeks of training. Using fMRI during breath monitoring, Farb and colleagues found that mindfulness-trained participants showed greater activity in the anterior dysgranular insula compared to waitlisted controls, along with altered functional connectivity between the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and posterior insula. Their conclusion: "interoceptive training modulates task-specific cortical recruitment, analogous to training-related plasticity observed in the external senses" [PMID: 22689216]. The insula, the primary cortical region for interoceptive representation, was literally reorganizing around directed attention.

Allen and colleagues in 2012 ran a fully randomized six-week trial of mindfulness training with an active control group — a methodological gold standard in this literature. The finding with the most clinical relevance: participants with the greatest amount of mindfulness practice showed increased recruitment of dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, and right anterior insula during negative affective processing [PMID: 23115195]. Not all participants — specifically those who practiced most. Dose and change were correlated. The more you looked, the more the circuit reorganized.

Fox and colleagues synthesized 21 neuroimaging studies of approximately 300 meditation practitioners in a 2014 meta-analysis and found eight brain regions consistently altered in long-term meditators. The insula appeared as a site of exteroceptive and interoceptive body awareness changes. Effect size across studies was in the medium range (Cohen's d = 0.46). Critically, the regions that showed the most consistent change were precisely those involved in interoceptive attention: insula, hippocampus (memory consolidation of interoceptive experience), and anterior cingulate cortex (self/emotion regulation) [PMID: 24705269].

The clinical translation of this mechanism got direct support from a 2023 RCT by Price, Sevinc, and Farb. Participants received eight weeks of Mindful Awareness in Body-oriented Therapy (MABT) — a structured interoceptive awareness training — versus a no-treatment control. MABT was uniquely associated with insula deactivation during interoceptive tasks (indicating more efficient processing), increased functional connectivity between the dorsal attention network and somatomotor cortex, and connectivity changes that correlated with improvements in self-reported interoceptive awareness (MAIA). Within the MABT group, changes in subjective interoception predicted changes in right middle insula activity — the "primary interoceptive representation region." The authors' conclusion: "interoceptive training enhances sensory-prefrontal connectivity" [PMID: 37891765].

One more study isolates the mechanism with unusual precision. Quadt, Garfinkel, and Critchley tested a protocol called ADIE (Aligning Dimensions of Interoceptive Experience) in autistic adults with elevated anxiety. ADIE combines heartbeat detection tasks — directing conscious attention to cardiac signals — with performance feedback and brief physical activity that transiently increases arousal. The interoceptive training component (not a cognitive or emotional intervention) produced significant reductions in trait anxiety at three-month follow-up, with 31% of the training group meeting criteria for recovery versus 16% of controls. The mechanism proposed: improved regulatory control over internal stimuli via trained interoceptive attention [PMID: 34401684]. Garfinkel's laboratory has elsewhere established that it is not the raw accuracy of heartbeat detection that predicts clinical outcomes — it is the alignment between objective accuracy and subjective awareness, the metacognitive correspondence that only develops through deliberate attention training.

A 2026 scoping review by Rafter and colleagues, covering nine structural neuroimaging studies of mindfulness-based interventions, found the most consistent gray matter changes in the insula, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, posterior cingulate, and temporoparietal junction. Greatest structural changes appeared in studies implementing multi-month interventions or long-term practice. The review appropriately notes insufficient evidence for firm causal conclusions on any individual study, while confirming the convergent pattern [PMID: 42192796].

The convergence is hard to dismiss: attention directed at physiological signal, across multiple populations, multiple training protocols, and multiple neuroimaging methods, consistently produces structural and functional change in the specific circuits that process that signal.

## The Protocol

The practical application within SRL's training architecture follows from the mechanism directly.

Structured Interoception Training (Mode 1 — Interoceptive Attention Training) is not a warm-up before the real protocol. It IS the protocol. Three moves, executable in 60 seconds:

1. **Notice.** Direct attention at a specific somatic signal — the movement of the diaphragm, the pulse in the fingertips, the warmth behind the sternum. Name the body region, not the emotion.

2. **Name.** Label the quality precisely. Not "anxious" — "elevated heart rate with shallow breathing and muscular tension in the jaw." Precision is not clinical fastidiousness; it is the specificity of target that determines which circuit activates.

3. **Track.** Follow the signal across a full breath cycle or ten seconds. The tracking phase is where the Hebbian window stays open — sustained co-activation of attention and signal.

What constitutes success: the clinician can execute this sequence within 60 seconds before, during, or immediately after a clinical event. What constitutes failure: the clinician reports "I don't feel anything" for more than three consecutive sessions.

---

## The Failure Mode

The protocol breaks when the body has learned that body signals mean danger.

Interoceptive suppression — the active down-weighting of internal signal common in high-stress clinical careers — is not merely inattention. It is a trained defensive response. Medical education, through years of requiring performance under physiological distress while prohibiting acknowledgment of that distress, has taught many clinicians that somatic awareness is unsafe. When those clinicians direct attention toward physiological signal, the attention itself triggers arousal, and the arousal triggers avoidance.

In this state, the Hebbian gate does not open. What fires instead is the defensive circuit — and that circuit strengthens.

This is why the first two sessions of the NeuroMinute curriculum emphasize peripheral signals (breath, skin temperature, peripheral sensation) before progressing to cardiac or interoceptive signals with higher emotional valence. Graded interoceptive exposure is not optional in this population. The sequence follows the hormetic principle: well-dosed stress plus recovery equals adaptation; premature overload produces the opposite.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: fourteen clinicians, any acute care specialty, using MAIA-2 administered before and after a 21-day NeuroMinute curriculum. Primary measurement: "Noticing" subscale (items assessing detection of internal body sensations) and "Body Listening" subscale (items assessing deliberate attention to body for insights into emotional and physical state). Secondary measurement: RMSSD change across the 21-day period as autonomic outcome.

Prediction, following the dose-response pattern in Allen et al.: change in MAIA-2 "Noticing" will correlate with change in RMSSD. The clinicians who become better at noticing will show the most autonomic change — not because the protocol is more effective for them, but because the Hebbian gate was open more.

Untestable at small scale: whether structural gray matter changes occur in the insula within 21 days. The literature suggests multi-month timelines for structural changes. Functional changes (connectivity, recruitment patterns) appear earlier.

---

## The Connection

Awareness as neuroplastic surface area is the theoretical foundation beneath every SRL protocol. Without it, the NeuroMinute is a breathing exercise — pleasant, possibly calming, probably not structural. With it, each 60-second session is a Hebbian event: a deliberate co-activation of attention and physiological signal that, repeated across twenty-one days and five repetitions per day, accumulates into measurable circuit change.

This reframe matters for how SRL talks about what it builds. The company is not in the breathing app category. Breathing apps move air. SRL activates the circuit that reads the air moving — and that activation is where the plasticity lives.

Every clinical skill in anesthesia — hemodynamic interpretation, depth-of-anesthesia titration, team communication under load — executes on top of the clinician's capacity to sense their own state. Awareness as neuroplastic surface area is the concept that explains why interoceptive training is not a wellness supplement to clinical training. It is the prerequisite infrastructure on which clinical judgment runs.

*The body has been broadcasting the whole time. The moment a clinician starts receiving — deliberately, with precision, for sixty seconds — the receiver begins to rewire.*
