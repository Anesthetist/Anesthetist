# Notes from the Lab: The Residual — AI, Transaction Costs, and the Irreducible CRNA

*The AI-driven collapse of coordination costs concentrates, not eliminates, the CRNA's value into the one domain that governance architecture mandates remain human — fiduciary judgment under physiological load — and no training system in medicine has ever been deliberately designed to build this capacity.*

---

## The Observation

In a contemporary pediatric cardiac OR, there is now a system that titrates propofol. There is an AI documentation layer that transcribes, structures, and begins populating the chart. There is a predictive hemodynamics monitor that flags instability before the blood pressure drops. There are closed-loop analgesia systems, AI-assisted airway classification tools, and depth-of-anesthesia EEG interpreters.

The machine is absorbing the coordination layer.

And yet the CRNA's salary is not falling. Their licensing board has not been abolished. No algorithm holds a nursing license, carries malpractice liability, or faces a family after a critical event. The anesthesiologist's liability has, if anything, become more concentrated — because when the algorithm makes a recommendation and the human approves it, the human still approved it.

That asymmetry — machine handles coordination, human holds accountability — is not an accident or a regulatory lag. It is the architecture of the AI era in healthcare. And it describes exactly who the CRNA is becoming.

---

## The Mechanism

In 1937, Ronald Coase asked why firms exist at all. If markets are efficient, why bundle people under a single employer rather than contract every function separately? His answer: transaction costs. Coordination inside a hierarchy is cheaper than negotiating every exchange through the market. Search costs, negotiation costs, monitoring costs, enforcement costs — these create the firm.

Ismail, Nicastro, and Tjan (2026), in their manuscript on what they call the Organizational Singularity, argue that agentic AI is now collapsing the marginal cost of coordination, decision-making, and institutional knowledge to near-zero. When coordination is nearly free, Coase's logic inverts. The hierarchy — which existed to reduce transaction costs — becomes the bottleneck. Firms must choose between redesigning around intelligence-dense human teams or being displaced by competitors that do.

But transaction costs do not literally reach zero. Trust costs, liability costs, and regulatory costs persist. Ismail et al. name the gap that remains after AI absorbs the coordination layer: the **Fiduciary Wedge** — the permanent distance between what AI can technically execute and what it can be held accountable for.

In healthcare, this wedge is structurally wide. It is defined by state nursing practice acts, CMS conditions of participation, malpractice doctrine, and the enduring fact that the clinical encounter generates legal and ethical obligations that require a licensed, accountable human at the accountability node.

The peer-reviewed literature confirms what this means in practice. Duarte-Medrano and colleagues (2025), in a systematic review of AI in anesthesiology, found that the optimal integration model "emphasizes a complementary approach where AI augments rather than replaces clinical judgment — combining computational efficiency with the irreplaceable contextual understanding and ethical reasoning of the anesthesiologist." The word "irreplaceable" appeared in a systematic review of the anesthesiology AI literature. It was not a rhetorical gesture.

Olawade and colleagues (2026), reviewing human-in-the-loop AI across healthcare delivery, found that while human-in-the-loop systems consistently produced improved diagnostic accuracy and reduced medical errors compared to fully automated approaches, "implementation requires clear liability frameworks, adaptive training protocols, and quantum-safe cryptographic security." Liability frameworks require named humans with legal standing. The CRNA has that standing. The algorithm does not.

Three forces make this non-trivial in the operating room:

**1. Time compression.** AI systems operating at machine speed compress the interval between signal and recommended response. The clinician must now evaluate AI-generated recommendations faster than coordination tasks ever required. The cognitive tempo of anesthesia has increased, not decreased.

**2. Cognitive load redistribution.** When AI handles routine monitoring and documentation, the clinician's attention is freed from the 98% — and then concentrated suddenly onto the 2% that the AI cannot resolve: the ambiguous presentation, the conflicting data streams, the ethical judgment call, the novel situation outside the algorithm's training distribution. That 2% is the hardest 2%. It arrives without warning, after long periods of apparent calm.

**3. Autonomic consequence.** The physiological literature on vigilance fatigue documents what happens to the nervous system during sustained monitoring with low event frequency followed by sudden high-stakes demands. HRV declines. Interoceptive accuracy decreases. State drift accumulates. The gap between perceived readiness and actual physiological readiness widens — not because the clinician is negligent, but because the nervous system is operating correctly under conditions it was never trained to handle at this level of concentration.

The Organizational Singularity creates this demand. No clinical training system currently addresses it.

## The Framework

Ismail et al. identify five zones of irreducible human function in any intelligence-dense organization: decision architecture (routing judgment vs. prediction), fiduciary authority (liability-bearing approval), context integration (cross-domain synthesis under time pressure), ethical arbitration (value conflicts with no algorithmic resolution), and adaptive response (novel situations outside any training distribution).

Every one of these zones is, at its core, a state-transition problem. Every one requires a nervous system that can move fluidly between sustained vigilance and rapid pattern recognition — what the SRL framework calls the neurogating function. And every one is degraded, specifically and measurably, by the same mechanism: autonomic dysregulation that accumulates below the threshold of conscious awareness.

Sathyakrishna and colleagues (2026), reviewing the implementation gap between clinical AI benchmarks and real-world performance, argued that the field's focus must shift from predictive accuracy to workflow integration — and specifically to "preserving clinician cognition." That phrase contains the entire clinical durability thesis. The AI is only as good as the human reviewing it. The human is only as good as the physiological state they are running on.

Heslin (2026), examining AI documentation in trauma resuscitation, identified automation bias as the first-order risk: clinicians default to AI-generated summaries without applying independent scrutiny. This is not moral failure — it is the predictable consequence of a system that has increased the AI's role in documentation without training the human's capacity for selective critical engagement. The human has not been equipped for the attention regime the AI era creates.

Scott, Carter, and Coiera (2021), reviewing stakeholder attitudes toward clinical AI across 27 studies, found that clinicians were specifically concerned about "personal liability for AI-related error" while consumers worried about "loss of clinician oversight." Both groups understood intuitively what the Organizational Singularity formalizes: when the human approves the algorithm's output, that human must be genuinely present. Genuinely present is a physiological state, not a spatial one.

## The Failure Mode

The Organizational Singularity's failure mode in healthcare has a name: Zillow. In 2021, Zillow Offers bolted AI price prediction onto its existing organizational hierarchy without redesigning workflows around what the AI actually changed. The AI was accurate. The organization was not architected to act on its outputs. The result was a $500 million write-down.

The clinical equivalent is an operating room in which AI has absorbed the routine coordination layer but the CRNA has not been trained for the new attentional regime — sustained vigilance with low apparent workload, sudden concentration of complex judgment. The failure mode is not that the AI malfunctions. It is that the human approving the AI's outputs is physiologically operating in a state incompatible with the judgment the Fiduciary Wedge requires.

This failure mode has a biometric signature. HRV research consistently demonstrates that autonomic downregulation — specifically RMSSD decline over sustained monitoring periods — correlates with slowed reaction time, reduced pattern recognition accuracy, and narrowed attentional scope. The clinician feels fine. The ECG tells a different story.

The confound: this is not the same as the burnout literature's chronic stress pathway. This is within-shift acute state drift. It is invisible to self-report, happens across normal case loads, and is addressable by brief, structured interoceptive interventions — if anyone is measuring it.

---

## The Test

The hypothesis: CRNAs working in high-AI-assist environments (closed-loop drug delivery, AI documentation, predictive hemodynamics) will show greater RMSSD decline over a shift than CRNAs in standard-monitoring environments — not because they are more stressed, but because their coordination load is lower while their exception-response load is concentrated and unpredictable, creating a vigilance pattern that is physiologically expensive in a different way.

Minimum viable experiment: 20 CRNAs, crossover design, 4 weeks per condition (high-AI-assist vs. standard), wrist-worn HRV monitoring throughout shift. Measurement: RMSSD at shift start, mid-shift, post-case; MAIA-2 at baseline and week 4; documented exception events per shift (moments where clinical judgment diverged from AI recommendation). Primary outcome: RMSSD decline correlated with exception event response latency.

If the hypothesis holds — greater HRV decline in high-AI-assist conditions, despite perceived lower cognitive load, associated with slower or degraded judgment at exception events — the Organizational Singularity's Fiduciary Wedge has a biometric signature. That is a fundable study. It is also the argument that has never been made to every hospital system that has purchased clinical AI and not purchased clinical durability training alongside it.

---

## The Connection

The Organizational Singularity is not a disruption story for anesthesia. It is a clarification. It strips away the coordination overhead and reveals the irreducible core of what the CRNA actually is: a fiduciary agent making judgment calls under physiological load, in a regulatory environment that requires a human to own every decision, in an attentional regime that the AI era has made more demanding, not less.

SRL is not building a wellness intervention for this problem. It is building the training substrate for the human that the Organizational Singularity cannot replace. That is a different category — not prehabilitation, not burnout prevention, not stress management — but capability infrastructure for the intelligence-dense clinical organization that healthcare is becoming whether it plans for it or not.
