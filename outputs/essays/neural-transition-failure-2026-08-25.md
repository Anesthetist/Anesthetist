# Notes from the Lab: Neural Transition Failure — The Error Hiding in the Window Between Cases

*The clinical error most likely to follow a difficult case is not produced by the difficulty — it emerges in the transition afterward, when incomplete autonomic recovery carries residual sympathetic activation into the next encounter, silently degrading the prefrontal capacity that safe practice requires.*

---

## The Observation

The difficult intubation ends. After four attempts, the airway is secured. The patient stabilizes. You hand off and walk to the next room. Twelve minutes later, another patient is on your table.

Your chart says you're ready. Your affect is controlled. No one in the room suspects anything unusual.

But your nervous system tells a different story. Cortisol and norepinephrine are still elevated from the previous case. Your heart rate variability is suppressed below your pre-shift baseline. The inter-case window — those twelve minutes of turnover, restocking, and paperwork — was not long enough for the autonomic reset that should have occurred. You carry the previous case's physiology into the next patient's care.

This is neural transition failure: not an acute stress event, but the silent persistence of stress physiology through the window where it was supposed to resolve. In 28 years of clinical anesthesia across transplant, pediatric trauma, and cardiac cases, Randy Graybeal has identified approximately 30–40 naturally occurring transition windows per CRNA shift. Each one is a window where the autonomic system can reset — or fail to. The cumulative effect of consecutive failed resets across a shift is the mechanism through which most shift-long performance degradation builds.

The difficult case got all the attention. The transition window after it is where the risk actually lives.

---

## The Mechanism

The physiological substrate of neural transition failure is a phenomenon Ottaviani, Thayer, Verkuil, and Brosschot mapped in systematic detail in a 2015 meta-analysis of 60 studies published in *Psychological Bulletin* [PMID: 26689087]: perseverative cognition — the mental replay of a stressful event through rumination or anticipatory worry — produces measurable, prolonged physiological activation that outlasts the stressor itself. Across 60 studies, perseverative cognition was associated with elevated heart rate (effect size g=.20–.28), reduced heart rate variability (g=.15–.27), elevated cortisol (g=.32–.36), and elevated blood pressure (g=.45–.51). These are not small effects. They represent ongoing sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis engagement during periods when the clinician believes they have moved on.

Critically, the activation is not metabolic. Verkuil, Brosschot, Tollenaar, Lane, and Thayer demonstrated this distinction directly in a 2016 study published in *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* [PMID: 27150960]. Using a calibration algorithm that filters out HRV changes attributable to physical movement, they identified "additional HRV reduction" (AddHRVr) episodes — prolonged cardiac activation exceeding what movement alone explained — in a sample of 32 participants monitored over 24 hours. AddHRVr episodes averaged 7.5 minutes in duration and were significantly associated with worry and negative affect, not physical exertion. This is direct real-world measurement of residual sympathetic bleed: not theoretical, but detectable in daily life, linked to psychological persistence rather than physical demand.

In a clinical shift, the inter-case transition typically runs 8–15 minutes. The Verkuil and Brosschot data suggest that worry about a preceding difficult case can sustain non-metabolic autonomic activation for that entire window — and beyond.

What does that activation cost cognitively? Van Ast, Spicer, Smith, and Wager clarified the neural mechanism in a 2014 study published in *Cerebral Cortex* [PMID: 25249408]. Using fMRI during a working memory N-back task under social evaluative threat, they found that social threat impaired working memory performance and produced widespread reductions in lateral prefrontal cortex and intraparietal sulcus activation. Critically, threat also decreased functional connectivity between the intraparietal sulcus and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex — the network that supports executive control of complex, competing task demands. When the prefrontal-parietal network is disconnected by stress-induced activation, working memory capacity shrinks, cognitive flexibility narrows, and the clinician's ability to simultaneously hold multiple task streams degrades precisely when the next patient arrives.

Three things change simultaneously in this window: cortisol and norepinephrine remain elevated, HRV is suppressed, and prefrontal-parietal network connectivity is disrupted. This essay isolates the physiological carry-over channel — recognizing that time pressure, patient complexity, and social dynamics of the next case also change simultaneously. The specific claim is that the unresolved autonomic state is a *separable* contributor to the cognitive load the clinician brings into case two, not merely a correlate of general fatigue.

The most direct experimental support comes from Janson-Schmitt, Haessner, Moeller, and Rohleder in a 2025 randomized experiment published in *Biopsychosocial Science and Medicine* [PMID: 41774857]. Forty-seven participants were exposed to two consecutive stress tasks, with the rumination group engaging in guided post-event rumination between tasks. Compared to the control group, the rumination group showed significantly reduced HPA axis habituation (t(45)=−2.01, p=.025) and increased sympathetic nervous system activation over time via rising salivary alpha-amylase levels (t(45)=−2.03, p=.024). Post-event rumination did not merely delay recovery — it actively prevented the biological habituation that would normally reduce the second stressor's physiological impact.

This is the mechanism in laboratory form. Between cases, the CRNA who is mentally replaying the difficult intubation — reviewing decisions, anticipating criticism, anticipating what could have gone differently — is not resting. They are blocking the stress habituation that would prepare the system for what comes next.

The downstream clinical consequence is visible in the error data. Bordini and colleagues, analyzing 2,801 pediatric difficult airway cases from the Pediatric Difficult Intubation registry, found cognitive errors in 17.4% of encounters [PMID: 40447486, *British Journal of Anaesthesia*, 2025]. Fixation error — the inability to disengage from a failing problem-solving strategy and shift to an alternative — was the most common type at 11.5%. Each cognitive error independently increased the adjusted odds ratio for complications by 1.86×, and multiple errors increased severe complications by 2.48×.

Fixation error is neural transition failure in intra-case form: the same failure to shift autonomic and cognitive state that happens between cases is observable in real-time within a case as the inability to disengage from an approach that is not working. The between-case failure and the within-case failure share the same substrate — incomplete resolution of a previous physiological state combined with degraded prefrontal flexibility.

Six confounds change simultaneously after a difficult case: physical fatigue, time pressure, case complexity of the next patient, social evaluation anxiety, physical setup demands, and physiological state. This essay's claim is specifically about the physiological channel — that incomplete autonomic recovery is *independently* operative, not merely a proxy for exhaustion. The Janson-Schmitt experimental design, which controlled for time elapsed and task repetition, provides the cleanest evidence that the psychological rumination channel is causally upstream of the habituation failure.

## The Protocol

Gap Moment Training™ is specifically architected to interrupt neural transition failure at the window where it is most preventable: the inter-case transition. The protocol is not a wellness intervention inserted between tasks. It is an autonomic reset — timed to the window where the system is supposed to recover but doesn't without deliberate intervention.

The minimum effective protocol for NTF prevention at each transition:

1. **Acknowledge the transition explicitly.** One spoken or silently named sentence: "That case is complete. I am entering a different context." This cognitive boundary reduces the mental carry-over that drives AddHRVr.
2. **Three slow nasal exhales** at approximately 6-second intervals. Exhale emphasis activates vagal outflow via the baroreflex. The target is three full respiratory cycles — not necessarily measured, but deliberately paced.
3. **One interoceptive check.** Scan for: heart rate estimate, tension location, breath quality. The goal is not to achieve a specific state, but to establish actual baseline awareness before the next encounter. Anterocept™ — anticipatory interoceptive awareness — requires knowing where you actually are physiologically before you can anticipate where you need to be.

Duration: 60–90 seconds. Insertion points: walking between ORs, hand-washing ritual, supply draw, before entering the next room.

What this protocol does not do: it does not provide crisis intervention for traumatic events. Events above a certain physiological threshold — cardiac arrest management, death of a pediatric patient, severe anaphylaxis — require longer recovery windows than any inter-case transition allows. Positioning GMT as sufficient for high-magnitude events misapplies the intervention and sets up a false test of its efficacy.

## The Failure Mode

Three failure modes are specific to neural transition failure, as distinct from the failure modes of Gap Moment Training in general.

**The invisible accumulation problem.** Because AddHRVr is not consciously perceived, the clinician who has experienced four consecutive transition failures does not know they are carrying residual activation from cases one through three into case five. The system feels normal because the suppressed interoceptive accuracy that accompanies chronic partial activation prevents accurate self-assessment. State drift produces a recalibrated "normal" that is functionally degraded. The clinician entering case five with the accumulated carry-over of four incomplete resets does not report distress — they report being fine. The data say otherwise.

**The repositioning error.** Neural transition failure is a between-case phenomenon, not a post-crisis intervention target. Organizations that add debriefing protocols after adverse events address the right problem at the wrong timescale. The window for preventing the error in the *next* case is the transition window *before* it, not the debriefing after the event that reveals the error. These serve different functions; conflating them produces the illusion that one substitutes for the other.

**The single-reset assumption.** A 60-second reset after one difficult case may be sufficient. A 60-second reset after a shift-long accumulation of partial resets is not. The protocol above describes the maintenance intervention. High-load shifts require reassessment — if the last four cases involved a critical incident, an unexpected emergency, a difficult family conversation, and a pediatric airway challenge, the inter-case reset architecture needs a different dosing structure than a routine eight-case day.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 14 CRNAs, 3 weeks, continuous intraoperative and peri-operative HRV monitoring via wearable chest strap.

**What is measured:** Non-metabolic HRV reduction (AddHRVr, per the Verkuil and Brosschot 2016 algorithm) during each inter-case transition window, compared against each CRNA's individual shift-start HRV baseline.

**Primary question:** In transitions following cases rated as high-stress (Difficulty in Airway Management score, DIFS ≥ 4), does AddHRVr persist for longer than the transition window itself — meaning the next case begins before the reset is complete?

**Secondary question:** Does AddHRVr duration during the transition window predict near-miss frequency or subjective error report in the subsequent case?

**Falsification criteria:** If inter-case transitions show AddHRVr duration equal to or less than transition length (i.e., most transitions complete their reset before the next case begins), the neural transition failure hypothesis requires revision — either the 8–15 minute OR transition is sufficient for the clinician population studied, or the mechanism does not operate as proposed.

This is not a clinical trial. It is a signal-detection study. What it would establish: whether the transition window contains measurable physiological carry-over, and whether that carry-over correlates with any observable clinical outcome. The 14-day window allows within-subject comparison of high-stress vs. low-stress cases as the natural experimental manipulation.

## The Connection

Neural transition failure is the mechanism inside [[state-drift]] — the way slow, undetected autonomic degradation builds one incomplete reset at a time. It is the primary route by which [[clinician-durability]] erodes across individual shifts before it erodes across careers.

[[Gap Moment Training]]™ is explicitly sized to address this window. The 60-second [[neurominute]] format was derived from the amygdala deactivation timing literature (minimum 90 seconds) and is the minimum effective dose for initiating — though not always completing — an autonomic reset. The distinction matters: a 60-second reset starts the process; whether the process completes depends on the magnitude of the preceding stressor, the total cumulative load, and the quality of the reset itself.

What this changes about how SRL understands the system: neural transition failure repositions Gap Moment Training from a wellness practice to a safety intervention. The evidence from Janson-Schmitt et al. (2025), Bordini et al. (2025), and Ottaviani et al. (2015) together suggests that the inter-case window is not downtime — it is active clinical infrastructure, currently unoccupied, where a known physiological failure mode is occurring in real time.

*The CRNA washes their hands between cases — protocol, hygiene, boundary. The nervous system needs the same ritual: not because it feels better to do it, but because the next patient is waiting for a prefrontal cortex that has actually reset, and that reset doesn't happen by itself.*
