# Notes from the Lab: The Three-System Problem — Why Autonomic Regulation Is the Real Competency

*Autonomic flexibility — the capacity to move rapidly between sympathetic activation and parasympathetic recovery while maintaining integrated function — is the strongest physiological predictor of clinical decision quality, and it is objectively measurable and trainable in under ten minutes per day.*

---

## The Observation

The case turns at minute forty-seven.

Nothing catastrophic — a blood pressure trend, a saturation that should not be drifting, an airway that was cooperative and is now something else. The kind of thing that becomes a case report if you do not catch it in the next ninety seconds, and an unremarkable shift if you do.

What determines which it becomes has almost nothing to do with what the CRNA knows. They knew the same things at minute forty-six. The variable is physiological: where is the autonomic nervous system, right now, in this body, managing this room? Is it poised — simultaneously alert and recovered, prefrontal cortex online, capable of generating and evaluating three options in parallel? Or has it already locked into sympathetic dominance, the very state that narrows cognitive aperture to a single threat and compresses the decision window to a single option?

The tragedy is that this transition is largely invisible. The monitor readings the CRNA watches are the patient's. Nobody is watching the CRNA's HRV.

---

## The Mechanism

Autonomic regulation is not a single dial. The autonomic nervous system operates across three distinct functional states — and which state a clinician occupies at any given moment determines not just how they feel, but whether the prefrontal cortex is fully engaged as an executive resource.

**Sympathetic dominance** recruits fast, narrow processing. Elevated heart rate, suppressed HRV, elevated cortisol — the body is mobilized for action. Appropriate for the ten seconds after an unexpected crisis. Deteriorating rapidly if it persists. Awtry et al. (2025), in a multicenter prospective study of 793 surgical procedures across 38 surgeons, found that elevated sympathovagal balance (LF/HF ratio) in the *first five minutes* of surgery was associated with fewer major postoperative complications — adjusted odds ratio 0.63 (95% CI, 0.41-0.98). This is the counterintuitive finding: some sympathetic activation is protective, because it represents appropriate physiological mobilization before the case begins [PMID: 39813063]. Dysregulation — chronically high sympathetic tone throughout, or inability to recover — is a different animal entirely.

**Parasympathetic dominance** supports recovery, integration, and rest. High RMSSD, strong high-frequency HRV, restoration. This is where you want to be *between* cases. Not during them.

**Integrated function** — what Porges calls the ventral vagal state — is the third and rarest mode: simultaneous activation and recovery. High HRV *and* cognitive readiness. Alert without being alarmed. In anesthesia, this is the state that makes everything else possible. It requires active maintenance, because left unmanaged, the demanding environment continuously pulls the system toward sympathetic dominance.

The mechanism linking these states to clinical decision quality runs through the prefrontal cortex. Forte et al. (2021) demonstrated this directly: in 130 healthy participants using the Iowa Gambling Task as a decision-quality index, higher vagally-mediated HRV predicted significantly better decision-making across resting, reactivity, and recovery phases [PMID: 33672004]. The neurovisceral integration model — developed by Thayer and Lane — explains the architecture: the prefrontal cortex exerts inhibitory control over subcortical emotional reactivity via vagal pathways. When vagal tone drops, that inhibitory control weakens. The amygdala has more bandwidth. Decision-making narrows.

Ramírez et al. (2015) refined this further: high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV) moderates the anxiety-attention relationship. In high-anxiety individuals, those with higher HF-HRV showed significantly better attentional control and less risk aversion than those with lower HF-HRV [PMID: 26555079]. The autonomic system is not just a performance correlate — it is a moderating variable. The same cognitive load produces different functional outcomes depending on the vagal baseline going into it.

What this means operationally: Dedmon et al. (2019) documented the decline in real time. During facial nerve dissection in otologic surgery — a high-stakes, precision-dependent moment — SDNN dropped from a resting mean of 43.9 ± 9.2 ms to 22.8 ± 3.1 ms, a reduction of nearly 50%. LF/HF ratio increased proportionally. The same procedures performed in a cadaver lab, without the stakes, produced no such HRV suppression [PMID: 30870371]. The body knows the difference between risk and rehearsal. The question is whether the clinician is managing the former or merely enduring it.

The team dimension adds a further layer. Powezka et al. (2022) found that HRV synchrony within surgical teams — the degree to which team members' autonomic rhythms entrain to one another — correlates inversely with procedure length and positively with non-technical skill scores [PMID: 36433593]. The CRNA is not managing their own system in isolation. The nervous system in the room influences the nervous systems around it. Every unmanaged dysregulation is also a transmission.

## The Protocol

The training target is not high resting vagal tone. It is *flexibility* — the capacity to move deliberately between states, and recover to baseline quickly after each departure.

The evidence-supported pathway toward that target involves resonant frequency breathing: finding the individual respiratory rate (typically 4.5–6.5 breaths per minute) at which heart rate oscillations are maximized, training that rate daily until the baroreflex feedback loop is sensitized, and using that sensitization as the substrate for state-transition work. Van der Zwan et al. (2015) demonstrated in a 76-participant RCT that HRV biofeedback training was equally effective as physical activity and mindfulness meditation for reducing stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, and for improving sleep quality — with a mean training dose of five weeks, daily, at home [PMID: 26111942]. Blase, Vermetten, Lehrer, and Gevirtz (2021), in a systematic review of nine RCTs, documented 64% reduction on the Beck Depression Inventory for HRVB plus treatment-as-usual versus 25% for treatment-as-usual alone [PMID: 33804817]. The training works. The question is whether it is applied in a context — the clinical shift — where it has operational consequence.

The minimum viable protocol for CRNA practice:

1. **Daily resonant breathing** (8–10 minutes) — build the substrate. Find the personal RF. Train the baroreflex loop.
2. **Pre-case gap moment** (60–90 seconds before first incision) — deliberate state-setting. One [[gap-moment-training]] cycle. Not relaxation — activation to integrated function.
3. **Mid-case micro-check** (30 seconds per hour) — interoceptive audit. Where is the system? Can it recover from where it is now?
4. **Post-case recovery window** (5–10 minutes after room close) — active parasympathetic engagement before the next case. Do not carry the prior case's autonomic load into the next one.

Measurement at every stage: RMSSD via wearable. The number is the feedback.

---

## The Failure Mode

The primary misapplication is treating autonomic regulation as a resting-state phenomenon. A CRNA with high baseline RMSSD who has never trained state transitions under load will collapse the same way as one with low baseline RMSSD. Flexibility — reactivity, recovery speed, return-to-baseline time — is the operative capacity, not the resting number.

The secondary misapplication is using autonomic regulation training as stress management — a wellness intervention disconnected from clinical performance. This reframes a genuine physiological competency as a nicety. The evidence does not support that framing. Forte et al. (2021) and Ramírez et al. (2015) demonstrate that the capacity of the autonomic system directly determines decision quality under pressure. This is not downstream of clinical performance. It is the substrate.

The third failure mode is team neglect. Individual autonomic training, applied to a provider who then transmits dysregulation to the room, solves half the problem. The Powezka finding implies that team-level autonomic culture is a measurable and modifiable variable. Individual training is the entry point. Team-level synchrony is the distal target.

---

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: fifteen CRNAs, resonant frequency breathing twice daily (eight minutes, morning and pre-shift), fourteen-day intervention. Wearable HRV monitoring throughout. Primary measure: post-case RMSSD recovery time to pre-case baseline. Secondary measures: self-reported decision confidence on validated Clinical Cognitive Assessment scale, perceived fatigue at shift end.

Hypothesis: within-subject RMSSD recovery time decreases by ≥20% from baseline to day 14. If untrue, report exactly why. If confounded by shift length variation, stratify. If device compliance is the limiting factor, report that honestly.

This is a 14-day proof test. Not a clinical trial. Fourteen days, fifteen people, one wearable, one measurable outcome.

Every concept in this vault ultimately returns to [[autonomic-regulation]]. [[vagal-tone]] is its parasympathetic measure. [[resonant-breathing-frequency]] is its primary training modality. [[gap-moment-training]] is the in-situ delivery mechanism. [[state-transition]] is the process it governs. Training the autonomic system is not one among several priorities. It is the infrastructure on which the others run.
