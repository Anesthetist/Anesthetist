# Notes from the Lab: Before the Crisis — Adversity Visualization as PTSD Prehabilitation

*Embodied adversity rehearsal — feeling failures kinesthetically before they occur — converts the nervous system's experience from novel catastrophe to familiar territory, interrupting the novelty-dependent encoding pathway that transforms clinical crises into PTSD.*

---

## The Observation

Between cases, there is a window. Monitors idle. The room resets. Most clinicians check their phones. Randy Graybeal ran through disasters.

Not abstractly. Not as a mental checklist. He moved his hands — phantom laryngoscope, phantom syringe plunger — and walked himself through what goes wrong. The grade 4 airway that refuses the blade. The hemodynamic collapse at induction. The drug error caught at the last check. He felt the scenario in his body: the weight of the instrument, the specific visual field of an occupied surgical site, the autonomic spike that comes with "this is real." He rehearsed not just the decision tree but the physiological response to the decision tree, and then he rehearsed recovering from that response.

He had no name for this. It wasn't a technique he'd been taught. It was 28 years of operational instinct in a high-stakes domain: the body prepares for what the mind cannot fully anticipate.

What follows is the science behind what he discovered alone.

---

## The Mechanism

The foundation comes from motor imagery research, and it is unambiguous: imagining a skilled action activates the same cortical circuits as performing it.

Arora and colleagues established this pathway with surgical specificity. In a 2009 validation study in *Surgical Endoscopy*, Arora et al. [PMID: 19633892](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-009-0624-y) developed a mental practice (MP) protocol for laparoscopic cholecystectomy that explicitly incorporated visual, cognitive, and kinesthetic cues — not just "imagine the procedure" but feel the grasper tension, the tissue planes, the force feedback. Both experienced and novice surgeons showed significant improvement in global imagery score post-training (experienced: 48 to 53, p = 0.007; novice: 15 to 42, p = 0.005). In 2011, the same group published the first randomized controlled trial demonstrating the performance consequence: across five laparoscopic procedures, the mental practice group consistently outperformed controls on technical ratings at every session, all at p < 0.001. Improved imagery quality correlated directly with better performance (ρ = 0.51–0.62). [PMID: 21245669](https://doi.org/10.1097/SLA.0b013e318207a789)

The mechanism is not metaphorical. Motor imagery activates premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and cerebellum — the same circuitry that coordinates physical execution. The body is not merely watching the rehearsal. It is, at the level of neural firing, practicing it.

Louridas et al. extended this into crisis conditions. In their 2014 randomized controlled trial in the *British Journal of Surgery*, mental practice trainees performed a complex laparoscopic anastomosis in a simulated crisis scenario — unexpected complications layered onto technical demands. Seven of ten mental practice participants improved or maintained performance under added stress. Four of ten in the conventional training group deteriorated. [PMID: 25332065](https://doi.org/10.1002/bjs.9657) The mental practice group didn't just perform better on a calm day. They performed better when things went wrong.

This is the first layer of the adversity visualization mechanism: kinesthetic rehearsal creates motor and decision pathways that remain accessible under autonomic load.

The second layer is subtler and, for CRNAs, more consequential.

Traumatic stress encoding depends heavily on novelty. The amygdala — the threat-detection and memory-consolidation center — assigns emotional significance proportional to how unexpected and uncontrollable an event feels. This is why identical clinical complications produce PTSD in some clinicians and integration in others: the difference is not the severity of the event but the degree to which the nervous system was prepared for it as a *class of experience*.

Adversity visualization is an intervention on the novelty variable. When a CRNA has kinesthetically rehearsed a laryngospasm — felt the spike, felt the response sequence, felt the recovery — the event that arrives is not neurologically novel. The amygdala receives a different signal: *this is a crisis, and I know how this goes.* The catastrophic novelty that drives hyperconsolidation is attenuated.

This mechanism has not been tested in CRNAs specifically. It has been tested in the broader domain of stress inoculation for high-stakes professionals. Maglione et al. (2022) conducted a systematic review of stress control interventions for military, law enforcement, and first responders across 38 controlled trials, 35 cohort comparisons, and 42 case studies. [PMID: 35837521] Interventions with physiological components — including stress inoculation with biofeedback — showed positive effects on return to duty, absenteeism, and distress. The critical variable distinguishing effective from ineffective interventions was the degree to which the preparation engaged the physiological response, not just the cognitive plan.

Cognitive preparation and embodied preparation are not the same. Expert surgeons understand this intuitively. Ibrahim et al. (2015) documented, through qualitative interviews with nine expert orthopaedic trauma surgeons, that these clinicians engage in intensive preoperative mental imagery involving multiple modalities: visual, verbal, and kinesthetic. Participants described a form of preparative activity that is socio-material — interacting with people, instruments, and their own internal states simultaneously. [PMID: 26296405](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12759) The most recent evidence extends this into explicit training contexts: Boulet et al. (2026) randomized medical students to mental imagery versus control before peripheral venous catheter insertion. The mental imagery group achieved 76% first-attempt success versus 52% in controls, with 0% complications versus 12.9%. [PMID: 41726258](https://doi.org/10.5334/pme.2069) The body learns from the rehearsal.

## The Protocol

Randy's adversity visualization runs in seven steps, executable in 3-5 minutes during any interstitial window:

1. **Visualize the case proceeding normally** — establish the baseline. Orient in the body: the room, the table, the monitors, the anesthetic state.
2. **Introduce a specific failure** — not abstract catastrophe but a concrete one: "SpO₂ drops to 84%." "Intubation fails after two attempts." "Inadvertent dural puncture."
3. **What would I notice first?** — train perceptual recognition. The specific visual, auditory, and proprioceptive cues that signal this failure, before the monitor confirms it.
4. **What is happening physiologically in the patient?** — map observable signs to pathophysiology. Not the protocol, but the body.
5. **What are the options?** — branch the decision tree. Two or three, not seven. The ones that are actually available in 45 seconds with one nurse and a surgeon.
6. **Choose and commit** — select and feel the decision. Not "I would think about..." but "I draw the emergency drug, my right hand reaches for the laryngoscope."
7. **Visualize every step of the response** — including the physiological recovery: the autonomic spike, the competent action, the patient stabilizing, the shift back toward regulated state.

Step 7 is the one most often skipped and most essential. The failure scenario must complete. The nervous system must feel the recovery, not just the crisis.

## The Failure Mode

Adversity visualization breaks in two distinct ways.

The first is catastrophizing. Catastrophizing and adversity visualization use the same mental architecture — imagined failure — but activate different autonomic states. Adversity visualization is structured scenario completion: enter crisis, execute response, recover. Catastrophizing is emotional flooding without resolution: enter crisis, spiral, stay. The difference is control of the endpoint. A practice that consistently ends in unresolved crisis is not adversity visualization; it is rehearsed helplessness. Every run must complete with a response.

The second failure mode is abstraction. The literature on motor imagery is clear: performance benefits depend on kinesthetic specificity. Arora et al. found that imagery quality — specifically the ability to generate vivid kinesthetic representations — correlated directly with surgical performance gains. A visualization that stays at the level of "I would manage the crisis appropriately" produces no neurological preparation. The benefit requires feeling the specific actions: this hand position, this dosage, this sequence, this breath.

The practice also fails under time pressure if it has not been built first under time safety. CRNAs who begin this practice immediately before critical procedures — without a history of lower-stakes rehearsal — may increase anticipatory anxiety rather than reducing it. The protocol requires a training arc: start with low-acuity scenarios, build toward high-acuity ones across weeks, not minutes.

---

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 20 CRNAs across two institutions. Randomize to adversity visualization protocol (15 minutes daily for 21 days, using the seven-step structure above) versus standard pre-case preparation.

Measure: (1) HRV during cases with unexpected complications versus without — looking for attenuated HRV drop and faster recovery in the visualization group; (2) PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) scores at 3 and 6 months; (3) subjective distress ratings in the 24 hours following a clinically significant adverse event.

The primary hypothesis: the visualization group will show smaller PCL-5 score elevations following adverse events, and faster return to baseline. Secondary: HRV recovery between cases will be faster in the visualization group across the 21-day arc.

The experiment is executable with wearables, existing PCL-5 instruments, and no additional clinical infrastructure. It is the first study to test adversity visualization as PTSD prehabilitation in a licensed clinical population.

---

## The Connection

No system exists for the prevention of PTSD in frontline medical workers. The second victim phenomenon — documented, named, measurable — has no standard prehabilitative protocol. Debriefing happens after. Support groups meet after. Therapy begins after.

Adversity visualization happens before.

It is Gap Moment Training applied to the most consequential gap: the time between now and the event that will either integrate or wound. It is what [[contemplative-progressive-overload]] looks like when the stressor is imagined catastrophe rather than cold exposure. It is the clinical application of the non-ergodic insight in [[non-ergodic-risk]]: in high-stakes irreversible domains, preparation is not optional — the cost of the unprepared event is not recoverable from the mean.

The body that has already failed a hundred times, and recovered a hundred times, is a different instrument than the one encountering failure for the first time. It is more than more competent. It is less likely to break.

---

*The syringe in Randy's phantom hand, the ceiling of the OR running through his closed eyes during turnover — this was not superstition. This was the most rational thing a high-stakes clinician can do with three idle minutes: teach the nervous system that failure is already familiar.*
