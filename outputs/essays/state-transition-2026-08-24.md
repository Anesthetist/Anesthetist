# Notes from the Lab: The Physiology of Switching

*The clinician's autonomic state at case entry — shaped by every incomplete transition across the shift — is measurably predictive of patient outcomes, making state transition training a patient safety intervention, not a wellness add-on.*

## The Observation

Three state shifts in sequence. The pager sounds at 2:47 AM. Stage 3 sleep — genuine slow-wave, the biological floor of rest. Within 90 seconds: full clinical alertness, hemodynamic assessment, intervention, stabilization. Then a different task: return to the call room and re-enter the same sleep. Not a truncated version. The same depth.

Most people cannot make even one of these transitions cleanly. Experienced CRNAs who have run call for decades can chain all three without autonomic residue — they wake, they act, they return. No destabilization at the seams.

This is the extreme case. The same mechanism governs a Tuesday afternoon. A standard CRNA shift produces 30 to 40 natural transition points: before induction, between cases, walking to the OR, after a difficult intubation, post-critical incident, handoff to PACU. Each is a moment where the autonomic nervous system either completes its reset or carries forward what it hasn't finished.

The case after the emergency case has a particular texture. The hands do the right things. The thinking seems clear, or at least feels clear. But somewhere below the threshold of awareness, the respiratory rate is running two cycles faster than baseline. The heart rate returned to the 70s but has not returned to the 60s where it was six hours ago. Attentional aperture is narrower than the clinical situation requires. The nervous system is still partially in the last room.

That carry-over has a name and a mechanism. It is not fatigue. It is incomplete state transition.

## The Mechanism

A state transition is the movement of the autonomic nervous system between attractor states: sympathetic to parasympathetic, fragmented attention to focused attention, threat posture to safety posture. Stephen Porges' polyvagal framework provides the hierarchy — neuroception (subconscious threat detection) governs which direction the transition moves and whether the move completes.

The central timing constraint is the amygdala. After a genuine threat response — difficult airway, hemodynamic crisis, interpersonal rupture — the amygdala requires a minimum of 60 to 90 seconds before cortical override can reestablish executive function. This is not a behavioral limit. It is imposed by neurophysiology. The limbic alarm system does not respond to intention; it responds to time and to the afferent signals the body sends upward.

What happens when the transition window is compressed — when the next case begins before the previous one resolves physiologically?

Ottaviani, Thayer, Verkuil, Brosschot, and colleagues published a 2015 meta-analysis of 60 studies on the physiological concomitants of perseverative cognition — the mental process of dwelling on a past stressor or anticipating a future one [PMID: 26689087]. The findings: perseverative cognition maintains the stress response beyond the triggering event. Higher systolic blood pressure (effect size g = .45), higher diastolic blood pressure (g = .51), elevated heart rate (g = .28), higher cortisol (g = .36), and reduced HRV (g = .15–.27). This is not rumination as an abstract psychological category. It is the physiological signature of incomplete state transition: the autonomic system continues executing the stress program long after the stressor ends.

Costello and colleagues (2025) refined this with a protocol specifically designed around repeated stress [PMID: 40166904]. They exposed 146 participants to the same stressor twice within a single testing session. Participants with higher trait rumination showed significantly less cardiovascular adaptation from the first to the second stress exposure. The nervous system did not update its baseline. Reactivity from the first exposure carried into the second — not because the second stressor was worse, but because the transition between them was incomplete.

In a clinical shift, this is not a two-event study. It is a compounding series. Fava, McEwen, and colleagues (2019) defined the threshold at which this becomes clinical: allostatic overload, the point at which "the cost of chronic exposure to fluctuating or heightened neural and systemic physiologic responses exceeds the coping resources of an individual" [PMID: 31252304]. McEwen (Rockefeller University) has called this accumulated burden "toxic stress." Its distinguishing feature is invisibility — the physiological cost outpaces what the clinician can self-detect.

Rieger, Stoll, Kreuzfeld, and colleagues (2013) captured this in surgeons [PMID: 23370764]. They measured heart rate and HRV via ambulatory ECG during operating room procedures. Stressed surgeons showed median intraoperative HR of 99.3 bpm; non-stressed surgeons, 63.7 bpm. More telling: stressed surgeons showed reduced autonomic recovery *during sleep*. HRV indices at night, the period of maximal biological recovery, were lower in the high-stress group. The arousal carried through the shift. It carried through the evening. It was still measurable when the body was supposed to be recovering.

**The novel claim that must be separated from established science:** this accumulation is largely invisible to the clinician until it fails visibly. Two mechanisms drive the concealment. First, habituation — under chronic load, the nervous system stops flagging arousal states that would have been salient at lower baseline. Second, interoceptive suppression — a separately documented pattern in high-performing clinicians who learn to attenuate body signal sensitivity to maintain function under pressure. What begins as an adaptive strategy becomes a liability when the suppression masks accumulated dysregulation. The subjective sense of competence and readiness decouples from the physiological reality. This decoupling is the invisible degradation curve.

**The confound that must be stated explicitly:** multiple variables change simultaneously across a clinical shift — fatigue, caloric depletion, accumulated decision load, social friction. The evidence specifically implicates incomplete state transitions as a distinct mechanism because the autonomic signature persists even in conditions where total work hours are low but individual event intensity is high. The marker is not duration. It is whether each transition completed.

## The Clinical Evidence

The data on state transitions and patient outcomes is no longer indirect.

Awtry, Skinner, Polazzi, and colleagues (2025) conducted a multicenter prospective cohort study at four university hospitals in France [PMID: 39813063]. 793 surgical procedures, 38 surgeons, continuous intraoperative HRV monitoring. Sympathovagal balance — the ratio of sympathetic to parasympathetic activity — was measured during the first five minutes of each procedure. Result: increased surgeon sympathovagal balance at case entry was associated with significantly reduced major surgical complications (adjusted OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.98). The effect held after adjusting for surgeon age, patient comorbidities, and surgery type.

This finding requires careful reading. Higher sympathovagal balance does not mean calmer. It means appropriately engaged — the surgeon's autonomic system was in an activated, task-ready posture at the transition into the case. Surgeons normalized to their own baseline; those who entered cases with appropriate sympathetic engagement relative to their individual baseline produced fewer complications. The autonomic state at the transition predicted the outcome of the case.

Bordini, Orsini, Li, and colleagues (2025) provide the error anatomy from the anesthesia side [PMID: 40447486]. Retrospective analysis of 2,801 pediatric difficult airway cases from the PeDI registry. Cognitive errors were present in 17.4% of cases. Fixation errors — locking onto a failing plan and failing to shift to an alternative — were the most common at 11.5%. At least one cognitive error increased complication odds by 1.86. Multiple errors raised them to 2.48.

Fixation is a state transition failure. Not a knowledge failure. Not a skill failure. The inability to shift cognitive state from plan A to plan B when plan A is not working — to recognize that the situation has changed and move. That recognition requires the prefrontal cortex to override the limbic commitment to the current plan. When residual sympathetic activation is high and HRV is low, that override becomes harder. The gate between states is less permeable.

## The Protocol

Gap Moment Training™ operates at precisely these transition windows. A single 60-second NeuroMinute — slow diaphragmatic breathing at approximately 6 cycles per minute — at each natural transition point.

The mechanism: paced breathing at this frequency entrains the baroreflex, triggering parasympathetic rebound via the vagus nerve. Pozzato and colleagues (2025) demonstrated that a 2-minute session of slow-paced breathing (6 breaths/min) produced significant HRV increases in participants with high psychological stress — and those with the highest distress showed the largest autonomic response [PMID: 39778745]. Elbers and McCraty (2025) showed that repeated coherence practice produces improvements in cognitive performance, mood, and energy over weeks — and that the coherent heart rhythm pattern can become the individual's habitual set-point rather than an effortful intervention [PMID: 41445965].

The protocol within a transition window:
- Three slow diaphragmatic breaths at personal resonant frequency
- Body scan: where is the previous case still held? Name it.
- Verbal reset anchor: "That case is complete. This patient is new."
- HRV confirmation if wearable is available: is RMSSD recovering toward baseline?

This is not a relaxation practice inserted into a clinical workflow. It is a reset protocol at the transition point, analogous to a pilot's between-phase checklist. The physiology determines whether the reset completed; subjective sense alone does not.

## The Failure Mode

State transition training breaks under three specific conditions.

**Time compression culture.** OR turnover pressure directly compresses the transition window. A 60-second protocol between cases feels like a luxury when the next patient is already in the room. This is a system-level antagonism, not an individual motivation problem. The institutional incentive structure that minimizes transition time is the same structure that accumulates the physiological debt that produces complications.

**Post-success overconfidence.** After a high-intensity event that resolved well, subjective state is elevated — felt competence and alertness are high. This masks incomplete autonomic recovery. The surgeon who lands a difficult intubation successfully and immediately enters the next case is at highest risk: elevated affect, narrowed attention, and physiological activation that reads internally as readiness.

**The cognitive compartmentalization illusion.** A clinician can mentally "move on" from a previous case while the body continues running the previous case's autonomic program. These are independent systems. Cognitive context-switching is not autonomic reset.

## The Test

14 days. 10 CRNAs in active clinical practice. Wearable HRV monitor worn throughout shifts.

**Baseline condition:** No intervention. Log case start and end times. Record RMSSD at each transition. Track RMSSD trajectory across the shift: ascending (recovery) or descending (accumulation)?

**Intervention condition:** 60-second NeuroMinute at each natural transition, logged via app tap.

**Primary outcome:** Does structured transition practice maintain or recover RMSSD between cases relative to unstructured baseline days?

**Secondary outcome:** End-of-shift RMSSD and subjective fatigue score. Do they diverge (high subjective fatigue with normal RMSSD), confirming the decoupling hypothesis?

This is a proof-of-concept study, not a clinical trial. The measurement burden is low. The population is available. The wearable technology exists. What has not been done is prospective tracking of autonomic state across a clinical shift with and without structured transition protocols.

## The Connection

The Awtry finding is the anchor for everything SRL builds. If surgeon autonomic state at case entry predicts patient complications, and that state is shaped by the cumulative residue of incomplete transitions across the shift, then managing transitions is not a clinician wellness question. It is a patient safety question.

[[gap-moment-training]] is the intervention at the transition point. [[neural-transition-failure]] is the pathology when the transition is missed. [[neurogating]] is the automated detection layer that identifies transitions via biometric patterns — HRV drop, HR spike, respiratory rate shift — and cues the appropriate protocol. [[clinician-durability]] is the career-scale consequence of consistently completed versus consistently failed transitions.

The literature on perseverative cognition, allostatic load, and intraoperative autonomic stress arrives at a single operational conclusion: managing the spaces between clinical events is not secondary to managing the events themselves. The transition is the performance.
