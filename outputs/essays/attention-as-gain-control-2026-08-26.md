# Notes from the Lab: The Gain Dial — Why Attention Is Not a Spotlight

*Attention is not a spotlight that selects what to illuminate — it is a gain mechanism that simultaneously amplifies high-priority signal channels and suppresses low-priority ones, implemented by the cingulo-insular salience network; Gap Moment Training is, at the circuit level, deliberate practice in recalibrating this gain under clinical load.*

## The Observation

Twelve minutes into a complex case. The surgeon makes an unexpected request. Blood pressure is trending down. A ventilator alarm fires — normal variation, not a crisis. The CRNA's gaze moves: monitor, surgical field, IV access, back to monitor.

Somewhere in that sequence, a somatic signal drops out. Not because it disappeared — the diaphragm is still moving, the cardiac rhythm is still registering through palmar afferents — but because the gain on those internal channels got turned all the way down. The body kept transmitting. The nervous system stopped listening.

This is not inattention, and it is not a failure of discipline. It is the brain's gain-control system doing exactly what it was designed to do under resource scarcity: suppress the channels carrying the lowest predicted utility and amplify the channels carrying the highest. The problem is not the mechanism. The problem is miscalibration. The system suppressed the very signals — internal state, arousal trajectory, somatic markers of cognitive load — that would have indicated performance was about to degrade.

## The Mechanism

Attention research spent decades organized around the spotlight metaphor: a beam of selection that can be directed at one target while ignoring everything else. Treisman's feature integration theory, Posner's spatial cueing paradigm, Broadbent's filter model — each varies the architecture but shares the underlying premise that attention works by choosing what enters processing and excluding the rest.

Gain control is a different model. Rather than selecting a target, the brain adjusts the *gain* on competing signal channels simultaneously — amplifying some while divisively normalizing the background. The result looks like selection from the outside (one thing gets processed, others don't) but operates by a different mechanism: not exclusion but differential weighting.

Shen, Louie, and Glimcher (2023) showed at the circuit level how disinhibition — the release of inhibitory interneurons from inhibition — provides fast, dynamic control of network state [PMID: 37261426; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.82426)]. Their disinhibition model reproduces both divisive normalization (the gain-dampening of background channels) and winner-take-all dynamics (the sharp amplification of the highest-value channel) within the same architecture. The biological system can shift between broadened-field and locked-on-target modes through a single disinhibitory motif. This is not a theoretical model — it fits empirical neurophysiological data from rhesus macaque decision-making circuits.

The neuromodulatory implementation runs significantly through acetylcholine. Runfeldt, Sadovsky, and MacLean (2014) recorded action potentials from up to 900 cortical neurons simultaneously using two-photon calcium imaging during ACh perfusion of thalamocortical slices [PMID: 24872527; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00071.2014)]. Their finding: acetylcholine did not simply increase overall neuronal activation. It pruned weak functional connections from spontaneous circuit activity while preserving strong correlations — yielding a more modular, hierarchical circuit structure that biased activity toward feedforward flow. Signal-to-noise ratio improved. The gain on high-reliability inputs went up; the gain on low-reliability inputs went down. This is gain control implemented at the synaptic level.

The precision weighting framework places this within a broader theory. Lao-Rodríguez, Cacciato-Salcedo, and Malmierca (2026) synthesize emerging evidence that the brain does not passively receive signals — it estimates their *reliability* and weights them accordingly [PMID: 42558780; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1887747)]. Neuromodulatory systems — acetylcholine, dopamine, noradrenaline, serotonin — regulate the gain assigned to prediction errors across cortical hierarchies. In this framework, attention is not a spotlight. It is the brain's continuously updated estimate of which signals are worth trusting, expressed as differential gain across channels.

The anterior insula is the critical node. Sojka and colleagues (2024) used task-based fMRI to show that voluntarily directing attention toward cardiac interoceptive signals activates the right anterior insula and bilateral dorsal anterior cingulate cortex [PMID: 39558709; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.neuropsych.20240070)]. The cingulo-insular salience network — AI plus dACC — is the anatomical substrate of deliberate interoceptive attention. Importantly, this activation is *volitionally modifiable*: the gain on interoceptive channels can be consciously turned up, and doing so engages the precise network responsible for signal weighting across the entire system.

Ojeda, Wagner, Maric, Ramanathan, and Mishra (2022) tracked this network in real-world conditions using EEG source imaging during an academic problem-solving task [PMID: 36502931; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2022.108445)]. Their finding: anterior insula → anterior cingulate cortex effective coupling increases precisely at the moment of attention switching — the transition from focused on-task to distracted off-task states. The salience network is the transition detector, and it is measurable via EEG in the field.

Vukosav, Zuber, Tomas, and Kopilaš (2026) complete the picture: affective states themselves function as precision weights that dynamically adjust internal perceptual priors [PMID: 42512471; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci16070696)]. A CRNA whose autonomic arousal is rising is not merely "stressed" — their internal precision estimates are shifting, altering which signals the brain treats as reliable inputs to decision-making. If interoceptive channels are habitually suppressed under load (the interoceptive suppression hypothesis), the brain loses the internal reference channel that calibrates its own gain adjustments. The system continues weighting, but without the feedback that would tell it its weighting is drifting.

**The novel claim, stated precisely:** Somnistics protocols do not produce relaxation. They train deliberate adjustment of gain on interoceptive signal channels — using the anterior insula and cingulo-insular salience network as the training substrate — so that the brain's precision weighting system retains access to internal state data under high clinical load. This is a different mechanism from "mindfulness reduces stress," and it should be evaluated with different measurement tools.

*Confound statement explicitly held:* The neural mechanism evidence cited here is drawn primarily from neurological and psychiatric populations (functional neurological disorder patients, recovering alcoholic men, laboratory experimental paradigms). Extension to healthy, expert clinical performers under occupational load is mechanistically plausible but has not been directly demonstrated by any of the cited studies. The circuit is the same; the magnitude of training effects in expert populations under real clinical conditions requires a dedicated study.

## The Protocol

A gap moment understood through the gain dial:

1. **Open the gap.** A genuine 60-second transition: pre-case checklist complete, surgeon scrubbing, post-induction stabilization window.

2. **Turn up interoceptive gain.** Direct voluntary attention to diaphragmatic movement, cardiac rhythm, and muscle tension at jaw and shoulders — in that order. The anterior insula activates. The gain on internal channels rises.

3. **Normalize the background.** Three breath cycles at resonant frequency (~5.5 BPM). Sympathetic driving drops. The autonomic signal-to-noise ratio settles.

4. **Read the internal state.** Not "how do I feel?" but "what is the system reporting?" Somatic signal or silence? Diaphragm moving freely or held? Cardiac rate perceived or absent?

5. **Re-enter.** With interoceptive channels at higher gain, the salience network has more data to work with for signal weighting during the next case.

**Success criterion:** Detection of at least one specific interoceptive signal within 30 seconds of Step 2 initiation. Not "felt calm" — "could feel something."

**Failure criterion:** Interoceptive channels remain silent through the end of Step 3. This indicates autonomic suppression at the level of diaphragmatic blindness and suggests the protocol has not yet established the baseline interoceptive access necessary for gain training.

## The Failure Mode

Gain control fails in two directions, and each has a distinctive clinical signature.

**Gain lock (sympathetic):** Under extreme cognitive load or acute stress, the gain system locks — amplifying threat-relevant external channels and suppressing all internal channels simultaneously. The clinician is processing everything about the environment and nothing about their own state. Decision quality degrades not because external information is lost, but because the internal reference signal — the one that would indicate processing capacity is falling — has been suppressed. This is the mechanism behind the interoceptive suppression hypothesis: it is not an accident or a bad habit. It is the gain system optimizing for the wrong objective.

**Gain suppression (dorsal vagal):** In prolonged overload, the brain shifts into protective suppression — turning down gain on most channels, not just interoceptive ones. This is the "running on autopilot" experience. Emotional blunting. Decisions that feel like they're being made from a distance. The gain control system has not failed; it has implemented a different optimization. Minimizing metabolic expenditure at the cost of performance bandwidth.

The misapplication of gain-control training is treating it as a tool for achieving calm. It is not. It is a tool for maintaining *access to the dial* — ensuring the gain system remains voluntarily adjustable rather than locked in states where voluntary modulation becomes unavailable.

## The Test

Fourteen CRNAs, mixed seniority, 21 days, wearable HRV monitor, MAIA-2 pre and post.

- **Days 1-4:** Baseline. Record time-to-first-somatic-detection (TTFSD) at genuine gap moments — how long after a cognitive task before the CRNA can identify a specific interoceptive signal. Hypothesis: baseline TTFSD >45 seconds in clinically active CRNAs.

- **Days 5-21:** Gap moment protocol above, minimum 3x daily. Track TTFSD after each protocol instance.

- **Primary measure:** Change in TTFSD from baseline (Days 1-4) to Days 18-21. Hypothesis: ≥30% reduction.

- **Secondary measure:** MAIA-2 body listening subscale change. Hypothesis: ≥7-point improvement.

- **Falsification condition:** No TTFSD change after 21 days means the protocol did not engage the anterior insula pathway effectively — not that gain control is the wrong model, but that this delivery failed to train the target mechanism.

Note: TTFSD is not a validated instrument. This essay proposes it as a minimum viable measure for the specific mechanism. It would need psychometric development before becoming a clinical tool.

## The Connection

Attention-as-gain-control is the mechanistic bridge between several concepts that currently connect only descriptively in the vault. It explains *why* polyanchora works (multiple interoceptive anchors maintain redundancy when one channel gets suppressed under load). It explains *why* the neurominute uses 60 seconds (sufficient to shift ACh-mediated circuit gain weighting without requiring protocol duration that exceeds available gap moments). It explains *why* neurogating targets HRV and respiratory rate specifically (these are readouts of the gain system's current settings, not the system itself).

It also identifies a measurement gap: MAIA-2 measures sensibility (self-reported), and heartbeat counting tasks measure accuracy. Neither measures real-time gain adjustability under load — which is what this concept identifies as the trainable variable. A new assessment instrument is implied.
