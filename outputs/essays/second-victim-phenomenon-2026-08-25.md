# Notes from the Lab: The Second Victim — When the Clinician Becomes the Casualty

*Second-victim phenomenon is as much an autonomic event as a cognitive one — the body carries the adverse event forward into every subsequent case, and no current support protocol is designed to address what's happening below the neck.*

---

## The Observation

The intubation goes wrong. A patient desaturates faster than the monitor updates. You recover the airway. The patient survives. You wash your hands, return to the room, and begin the next case.

Something is different. Not in the patient in front of you — in you.

The next laryngoscopy feels heavier. The moment before the blade goes in, a half-second of hesitation appears where there was none before. Your breath shortens without your choosing it. The pulse oximeter alarm three bays over makes you flinch in a way it didn't yesterday.

This is not burnout. This is not compassion fatigue. This is a body remembering what the mind has been instructed to set aside.

Albert Wu named it in 2000: the "second victim" — the provider who, after an adverse patient event, becomes a casualty of that event themselves. Susan Scott operationalized the concept in 2010, providing the epidemiological framework that moved second-victim from a clinical observation to a researchable phenomenon. The field has spent the intervening fifteen years studying it, measuring it, validating tools to screen for it.

What it has not done is address the body.

---

## The Mechanism

The second-victim experience has two simultaneous tracks. The track that receives almost all clinical attention is cognitive-emotional: shame, self-doubt, intrusive re-experiencing, fear of future errors. These are real, clinically significant, and well-documented. Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, Busch et al. (2023) developed the first clinimetrically validated screening tool specifically designed to assess second-victim distress — the WITHSTAND-PSY Questionnaire — distinguishing two critical facets: the emotional impact of the adverse event itself, and the current emotional state of the provider [PMID: 38118426; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1159/000535006)]. The tool demonstrated strong psychometric properties and marks a meaningful step toward systematic identification of second victims. The emphasis is on mood, anxiety, and depression.

The second track — the autonomic track — is rarely named separately, even though Ayvat et al. (2025) documented it in cross-sectional form: among 175 anesthesia and intensive care professionals, high levels of both psychological *and physical distress* were significantly associated with reduced professional self-efficacy, increased absenteeism, and heightened turnover intentions [PMID: 40725941; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000043375)]. Physical distress. Not psychological distress as a proxy for physical symptoms — physical distress measured independently and shown to correlate with career outcomes.

The mechanism is not mysterious. An adverse event is a threat stimulus. The autonomic nervous system responds to threat stimuli the same way it has for 400 million years: fight, flight, or freeze. Heart rate accelerates. Respiratory rate changes. HRV drops — sometimes acutely, dramatically. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis initiates a cortisol cascade that can persist for 24-72 hours in high-arousal states. None of this waits for the cognitive review.

What makes second-victim physiology distinctive — and dangerous — is the confound of professional context. CRNAs are trained to suppress autonomic read-through. The interoceptive suppression hypothesis [[interoceptive-suppression-hypothesis]] describes exactly this: elite clinicians develop exceptional capacity to filter out somatic signals during task execution. This is adaptive when the task demands full attentional resources. It becomes maladaptive when the task is over and the somatic signals — the unprocessed residue of the adverse event — remain in the body without being registered.

The CRNA walks out of the adverse event case and into the next one carrying an autonomic load they cannot feel. This is not a metaphor. It is a measurable state: elevated catecholamines, suppressed HRV, altered respiratory patterns, heightened startle response. The body is in a different operating mode than yesterday. The clinician, having learned to ignore such signals, proceeds as if it is not.

This confound isolates one critical variable: not the adverse event itself, but the gap between what the body is carrying and what the clinician is able to perceive it is carrying. The mechanism is: adverse event → autonomic dysregulation → interoceptive suppression of that dysregulation → continued high-performance task demands → cumulative dysregulation → eventual system failure (burnout, error, departure).

This is not speculation. Thompson et al. (2022) documented second victimhood in approximately half of all healthcare providers and noted the anesthesia specialty's particular vulnerability due to the immediacy and hands-on nature of adverse events [PMID: 34980539; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jopan.2021.05.005)]. The vault's cross-sectional anesthesia data shows that 17-19% of anesthesia providers report never fully recovering from a significant adverse event [[anesthesia-second-victim-2024-cross-sectional]]. These are not clinicians who went through the debriefing and remained symptomatic in their thinking. These are people whose bodies never returned to baseline.

## The Protocol

Current second-victim support operates almost entirely in the cognitive-verbal register: peer support conversations, employee assistance programs, group debriefings, psychological counseling. High and Forest (2025) documented a peer support implementation in which 20% of clinicians reported an emotionally distressing work event in the prior month and only 20% felt adequate support was available. Their rapid rollout of a peer support program improved perceived support to 77% post-implementation — a meaningful advance [PMID: 39949643; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.78854)]. The intervention was relational and verbal.

None of it addresses the body between the event and the debrief.

The intervention gap is the acute window: the 60-120 minutes immediately following an adverse event, before any formal support structure activates, when the autonomic system is still in high-activation mode and the clinician is typically returning to work. This window is where the pattern is set. If the body does not receive an explicit signal to downregulate, it defaults to the only available output: suppression. And suppression is not resolution — it is deferred carryover.

Gap Moment Training [[gap-moment-training]] addresses this window directly. The NeuroMinute™ protocol in its acute application is not a meditation technique. It is a physiological interrupt: six to twelve breath cycles at resonant frequency (4.5-6.5 BPM for most adults), which maximally activates respiratory sinus arrhythmia, generates a measurable HRV spike, and signals — via vagal tone [[vagal-tone]] — that the threat has passed. This is not self-soothing. This is a direct input into the autonomic system using the one voluntary lever that crosses the blood-brain barrier without pharmaceutical help.

The proposed acute protocol for second-victim response:

1. **Recognition** (0-5 min post-event): Name the state. "I had an adverse event. My nervous system is responding. This is physiology, not judgment." Interoceptive labeling — not suppression, not avoidance.
2. **Physiological interrupt** (5-15 min): Minimum one NeuroMinute at resonant frequency before returning to the next case. Not optional. Not "if you feel like it." A required recovery step, like washing hands.
3. **Structured debrief** (within 4-24 hours): Peer support or formal debrief in the cognitive-verbal register, as currently practiced — now layered on a body that has had a partial physiological reset, rather than one that is still running hot.

What constitutes success: measurable HRV recovery toward pre-event baseline within 30 minutes. What constitutes failure: HRV remaining suppressed through the next case, clinician unable to distinguish their arousal level from normal.

---

## The Failure Mode

The protocol fails when the institutional culture does not allow the five-minute window.

This is not a theoretical failure. Bexten et al. (2026) articulated the gap in an ICU educational framework: one of eight competency domains for new ICU providers was the second-victim phenomenon — yet the framework had to explicitly argue for *any* structured time for psychosocial competence development during onboarding [PMID: 42553591; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2026.1872115)]. The argument had to be made. That says something about the baseline assumption.

In high-throughput anesthesia environments, the five-minute window between cases is already contested. If the next patient is in the room, the pressure is immediate. The protocol breaks not because the clinician doesn't want to use it but because the environment hasn't named it as necessary.

A second failure mode: the protocol works for discrete events but cannot address chronic low-grade second-victim accumulation — the slow erosion from repeated near-misses, sub-threshold adverse outcomes, and high-acuity cases where nobody codes but everybody walked the edge. Congdon et al. (2025) confirmed in the most current CRNA-specific burnout synthesis that moral distress was the third most common burnout contributing factor [PMID: 40440194; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.70278/AANAJ/.0000001016)]. Moral distress is cumulative, not event-specific. The acute protocol addresses the discrete event. The chronic form requires something closer to the full stress inoculation model [[stress-inoculation-training]]: graduated, deliberate, regular autonomic recovery practice that builds reserve before the acute event.

You cannot spend what you have not saved.

## The Test

**Minimum viable experiment:** 14 CRNAs. Two sites matched for case complexity and volume. One site adds a mandatory 10-minute post-adverse-event protocol (structured recognition + physiological interrupt). The other site receives verbal peer support only (current standard).

Measure at 30 days: SVEST (Second Victim Experience and Support Tool) scores; HRV (RMSSD) measured via wearable in the 30 minutes pre-event and 60 minutes post-event on adverse-event days; next-case interoceptive accuracy (brief MAIA-2 subscale completion). Secondary: self-reported confidence for subsequent similar procedures.

Hypothesis: the protocol site will show measurably faster HRV recovery toward pre-event baseline and higher next-case confidence ratings, with no statistically significant difference in SVEST scores at 30 days (implying the physiological channel is not the same as the cognitive-emotional channel, and both matter independently).

If untestable at small scale: the barrier is institutional — getting site approval for a mandatory five-minute window. That is an administrative research question, not a physiological one.

## The Connection

The second-victim essay connects the two streams SRL has kept separate in prior writing. The durability literature ([[clinician-durability]], [[interoceptive-suppression-hypothesis]], [[state-drift]]) has focused on chronic erosion. The acute intervention literature ([[gap-moment-training]], [[neurominute]]) has focused on in-the-moment tools. Second-victim phenomenon is the bridge: an acute event that becomes a chronic pattern if the body does not close the loop.

It also names the autonomic co-regulation dimension [[autonomic-co-regulation]] that gets lost in purely cognitive second-victim support: when a CRNA returns to the next case still running hot, their nervous system is no longer a stable regulatory presence for the patient. The patient's nervous system is co-regulated, in part, by the clinician's. A second victim who is not physiologically recovered is not just a clinician at risk — they are a co-regulatory variable in the subsequent patient's autonomic environment.

That reframe — from "support the clinician" to "the clinician's recovery is part of patient care" — is what makes second-victim a systems issue, not a wellness perk.

---

*Somewhere in the next case, the body is still in the last one. The only question is whether anyone will give it five minutes to catch up.*
