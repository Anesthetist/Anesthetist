# Notes from the Lab: Act to State — The Decision Architecture Expert Clinicians Already Use

*Expert CRNAs under physiological uncertainty don't predict and plan — they effectuate: acting from available means, absorbing only reversible loss, leveraging surprises as data — and the degree to which they can do this, rather than insist on predicted outcomes, is what separates high-function clinical decision-making from brittle, uncertainty-intolerant pattern-matching.*

---

## The Observation

Seven hours into a complex spine case. The attending has stepped out. MAP is sliding — 58, now 54 — despite a pre-bolus that should have held it. The chart shows a clean history. Nothing on the surgical field explains it.

The CRNA doesn't wait for certainty. They work what's in the room: the patient's current pressure, the drugs on the tray, their own patterned read of how this body is moving. They choose a small, reversible intervention — phenylephrine, 100 mcg — and watch. Not because they know it's right, but because it's affordable to try and observable in 90 seconds. If it works, great. If the pressure keeps falling, they've lost nothing irreversible and now have new information.

This is not improvisation in the pejorative sense. It is a specific decision architecture. Sarasvathy (2001) — a management theorist, not a clinician — named it *effectuation*. The clinical community has not yet recognized that they've been doing it for decades.

---

## The Mechanism

In 1999, Saras Sarasvathy gave expert entrepreneurs a set of business problems and recorded how they reasoned. She expected to find planning — market analysis, projections, resource-gathering before action. What she found instead was the opposite: experts started with what they had (available skills, relationships, resources), accepted loss up to a tolerable threshold rather than predicting optimal outcomes, and treated contingencies as data to exploit rather than variance to eliminate. She called this effectuation — reasoning from the future state they could shape rather than the future state they could predict.

Five principles define it:
- **Bird in hand** — start with available means, not ideal means
- **Affordable loss** — commit only what you can afford to lose, not the minimum required to win
- **Crazy quilt** — build the team from who is actually present, not who would be ideal
- **Lemonade** — treat surprises as information, not failure
- **Pilot in the plane** — control the controllable; release the uncontrollable

Battle (2026) documented this exact framework operating in physician-entrepreneurs navigating healthcare innovation — 21 physicians who translated clinical insights into institutional change by applying effectual principles [PMID: 41618617]. The five Sarasvathy principles appeared across all cases not as deliberate strategy but as the natural decision logic of experts who had already learned, through clinical practice, that the future is not predictable in real time.

The clinical literature arrives at the same structure from a different direction. Marsden and colleagues (2023), studying pre-hospital blood transfusion decisions in air ambulance physicians, found that expert clinicians make recognition-primed decisions incorporating significant uncertainty — selecting cues, incorporating experience, and working within constraints of incomplete outcome information [PMID: 37704359]. The recognition-primed decision (RPD) model, first described by Klein in field observations of fireground commanders, describes experts not comparing options but rapidly simulating a single chosen action's likely consequences and adjusting if the simulation fails. This is effectuation operating under a physiological time constraint.

Croskerry and colleagues (2014) establish that clinical decision-making is neither purely intuitive (System 1) nor purely analytical (System 2), and that the relevant question is not which system to use but when each is adaptive [PMID: 24362398]. Effectuation provides a framework for that adaptive switching: affordable loss thinking slows the commitment when reversibility is low; bird-in-hand thinking accelerates action when waiting for more information costs more than acting on current information. The mechanism is not gut feeling — it is structured uncertainty management.

The failure to effectuate has a measurable clinical signature. Johnson and colleagues (2022), studying residents and attendings at an academic medical center, found that intolerance of uncertainty correlates with higher reported burnout, greater reluctance to disclose uncertainty, and deterioration of the learning environment [PMID: 35666840]. Begin and colleagues (2022), in a large study of primary care physicians at Massachusetts General Hospital, found that physicians with higher tolerance for uncertainty received higher patient experience scores for listening and overall ratings — suggesting that uncertainty tolerance is not merely a personal trait but a clinical performance variable [PMID: 36048444]. Kim and Lee (2018), reviewing the medical education literature, describe intolerance of uncertainty as a driver of cognitive bias, ineffective communication, and inappropriate resource use, and argue that developing uncertainty tolerance is a core clinical competency [PMID: 30180505].

The synthesis: what clinical training calls "tolerance for uncertainty" and what Sarasvathy calls "effectuation" are the same construct viewed from different disciplines. The clinical literature measures its absence (intolerance → burnout, poor performance, resource overuse). The entrepreneurship literature describes its architecture (the five principles that constitute skilled action under radical uncertainty). Neither field is fully reading the other. SRL's claim is that naming the full structure — not just tolerating uncertainty, but effectuating inside it — creates a trainable target.

## The Framework

**Effectuation as Clinical Protocol: Five Principles in the OR**

**1. Bird in Hand.** Before any decision, inventory what is actually available: the patient's current vitals, the OR team's present composition, the drugs on the tray, your own interoceptive read of the case's momentum. Work from this list, not from an imagined ideal resource set. The CRNA who delays action waiting for a perfect pharmacological picture loses the window that the affordable action could have opened.

**2. Affordable Loss.** Commit only to reversible interventions first. This is not timidity — it is minimum effective dose operationalized as epistemology. A 100 mcg phenylephrine bolus is affordable to observe; a decision to cancel the case is not reversible without cost. The smaller, observable action comes first. If it fails, you have information. If it succeeds, you have a state transition. Neither outcome is wasted.

**3. Crazy Quilt.** Your resource network is whoever is actually in the room. The circulator, the surgical resident who has mentioned something about the patient's history, the attending reachable by intercom. Build from the actual team, not the hypothetical consultant who isn't present. The crazy quilt principle prevents the cognitive cost of mourning absent resources and redirects attention to available ones.

**4. Lemonade.** The unexpected MAP drop, the unfamiliar response to a standard dose, the patient who is lighter than predicted — these are not complications. They are signals. The effectuating expert treats physiological surprise as high-bandwidth data about the specific body in front of them, upgrading their model rather than forcing the situation into the expected pattern. This is anterocept in its most acute form: the body is telling you something before the monitor confirms it.

**5. Pilot in the Plane.** You cannot control the patient's underlying physiology. You can control the next intervention, the communication to the surgeon, the adjustment to monitoring frequency. The effectuating CRNA's attention structure stays inside the controllable action space rather than leaking into the uncontrollable outcome space. This is the neural architecture of state management: autonomic resources allocated to action, not anxiety.

Bond and Cooper (2006), reviewing the recognition-primed decision literature in emergency nursing contexts, note that expert emergency decisions are recognition-based and that cognitive resources, situational awareness, stress, and task complexity all influence the process [PMID: 16879547]. Each of these influences is addressed by one of the five effectuation principles: bird-in-hand focuses cognitive resources, crazy quilt manages situational scope, affordable loss modulates the stress response, lemonade integrates new task complexity rather than fighting it.

## The Failure Mode

Effectuation breaks when the clinician is operating in *causation mode* under conditions that require effectuation. Causation — Sarasvathy's term for the alternative — is planning from a known goal: selecting the optimal means to a specified end. Causation is appropriate when the environment is predictable, the goal is stable, and the time horizon allows market research or pre-case preparation. Anesthesia induction, on a planned case with a complete pre-op, is largely causal. Seven hours in, pressure sliding — that is not.

The clinician trained primarily in causal logic who encounters radical physiological uncertainty tends toward one of two failure modes: action paralysis (waiting for certainty that will not arrive) or commitment escalation (doubling down on the original diagnosis rather than reading the new data). Both are intolerance-of-uncertainty responses that the Johnson (2022) and Begin (2022) literature identify as associated with burnout and clinical underperformance.

The second failure mode is applying effectuation where causation is appropriate. A scheduled case with abundant pre-op information and a cooperative surgical timeline rewards careful planning. Defaulting to improvisation there — not reading the chart, not pre-calculating drug weights, treating the pre-op as irrelevant — is not effectuation, it is negligence. The competency is knowing which mode the current moment requires.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 10 experienced CRNAs (5+ years of independent practice), 14 days. Each CRNA journals three decisions per shift, categorizing each by mode: causal (goal specified, optimal means selected) or effectual (start from available means, affordable commitment). At the end of each shift, they record their end-of-case HRV (RMSSD proxy via wearable) and a single-item uncertainty tolerance self-rating (1-10).

Hypothesis: shifts with higher proportion of effectual decisions under high-uncertainty conditions (patient complexity score ≥ 3) will correlate with higher end-of-shift RMSSD and higher self-rated uncertainty tolerance.

Success: correlation r > 0.4 between effectual-decision proportion and RMSSD on high-complexity shifts. Failure: no correlation, or inverse correlation (effectual decisions associated with lower RMSSD — suggesting effectuation is itself stressful rather than regulatory). If failure: examine whether the journal classification is reliable, or whether a third variable (case duration, sleep deprivation) is dominating.

## The Connection

Effectuation-style experimentation is the decision engine that runs SRL's demand sprints at the founder level and CRNA practice at the clinical level. Randy's Four Questions framework (what do I have, what can I afford to lose, who showed up, what surprised me?) is the same architecture. The Commoncog framework Cedric Chin articulates around act-to-learn under uncertainty is effectuation for knowledge workers.

This connects to [[minimum-effective-dose]] (affordable loss as pharmacological principle), [[anterocept]] (lemonade as anticipatory interoception), [[state-transition]] (effectuation as transition mechanism), and [[demand-biomarkers]] (reading market signals the way an effectuating CRNA reads physiological signals). The through-line: expertise is the capacity to act with integrity inside radical uncertainty — not because you know what's coming, but because you know how to respond to whatever does.
