# Notes from the Lab: What NANDA Cannot Name

*NANDA-I — the diagnostic language embedded in every hospital EHR in America — has no vocabulary for the four autonomic-interoceptive deficits that the neuroscience literature identifies as precursors to clinical error; the absence is not a gap in nurses but a gap in the field's ability to name, fund, and prevent what it already sees.*

---

## The Observation

Picture this: a CRNA with 22 years of experience sits in a simulation debrief. She has spent the last eight hours managing ventilators, titrating volatile agents, monitoring waveforms that represent another human being's autonomic state with millisecond precision. The debrief facilitator asks: "How was your breathing during that laryngospasm case?"

She goes quiet. Then: "I don't know. I wasn't tracking it."

This is the clinical paradox that sits at the center of everything Somnistics Research Labs builds. The most autonomically sophisticated practitioners in healthcare — certified registered nurse anesthetists who are literal closed-loop physiological controllers of other people's nervous systems — often cannot describe their own internal state with any precision at all.

The field has a name for the patient's problem. It does not have a name for this one.

Upendra and Kaur (2026) documented it systematically. In a mixed-methods study of 100 emergency nurses using the Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA), they found that most nurses reported only moderate levels of body awareness despite working in environments of continuous physiological threat. More striking: qualitative themes included "mind-body disconnection" as a consistent finding — nurses who described their bodies as something that happened to them rather than something they inhabited. The body was sending signals. The nurses were not receiving them.

---

## The Mechanism

The scientific vocabulary for what these nurses were experiencing was established by Garfinkel et al. (2015). Their landmark contribution — in a normative sample of N=80 — was the empirical dissociation of three distinct interoceptive dimensions that are routinely conflated:

1. **Interoceptive accuracy** — objective performance on behavioral tests of heartbeat detection
2. **Interoceptive sensibility** — self-reported beliefs about one's interoceptive ability (what questionnaires like the MAIA measure)
3. **Interoceptive awareness** — metacognitive correspondence between accuracy and sensibility; the degree to which a person knows what they know about their body

Garfinkel, Seth, Barrett, Suzuki, and Critchley demonstrated that these three dimensions were empirically distinct and dissociable. A clinician could have high accuracy but low sensibility, high sensibility but low awareness, or — the most clinically dangerous pattern — high confidence in their self-perception of internal state that does not correspond to objective physiological reality.

This is not a personality trait. It is a measurable, trainable dimension of clinical performance.

Mehling et al. (2012) built the instrument that makes this measurable in practice. The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA), developed at UCSF's Osher Center for Integrative Medicine, is a 32-item self-report scale assessing eight dimensions of interoceptive body awareness. It provides the measurement infrastructure that any clinical intervention — or any new diagnostic category — would need.

What the measurement shows is uncomfortable. Wickens (2026) analyzed how interoception, allostatic regulation, and autonomic arousal mediate moral distress and ethical decision-making in nursing. Drawing on Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis and predictive processing frameworks, Wickens argued that ethical clinical judgment is not a cognitive process that happens to have emotional interference — it is a whole-body, somatically embedded process in which interoceptive signals are constitutive, not incidental. When those interoceptive signals are chronically suppressed or disconnected — as the Upendra and Kaur data suggest they are in many clinical nurses — moral perception itself is impaired.

Chronic threat, Wickens documents, creates what she calls "affective distortion" of clinical judgment through the very limbic mechanisms that make nurses capable of sustained empathic care. The same systems that allow a nurse to feel with a patient become dysregulated under chronic professional stress.

The physiological evidence converges. Zhou, Chen, and Lin (2025) conducted a narrative review of mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) specifically in emergency department nurses, finding that MBSR "improves interoceptive awareness and autonomic balance, as evidenced by physiological markers such as heart rate variability." The mechanism runs in both directions: improved interoceptive awareness improves HRV, and improved HRV improves interoceptive awareness. The two systems are coupled.

Mensinger et al. (2024) demonstrated this coupling in the other direction. In a pilot feasibility trial of heart rate variability biofeedback (HRVB) with 28 healthcare workers — 79% nurses — HRVB improved "interoceptive sensibility, mindful self-care, body appreciation, intuitive eating, stress, and resilience." Participants reported that the practice helped them "relax and connect better to their body's signals and experiences." The body-signal loop, when made legible through biofeedback, begins to close.

Zafiriou et al. (2026) contributed a finding that is clinically disorienting but epistemologically important. In a prospective simulation study of anesthesia professionals — including nurse anesthetists — they measured HRV during a standardized pediatric laryngospasm crisis scenario. They found no significant association between resilience scores (Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale) and physiological stress response measured by HRV. Resilience, as conventionally assessed, does not predict autonomic response. This suggests the field is measuring the wrong thing. What the profession calls "resilience" and what the nervous system calls "regulation" are not the same variable.

**The confound that must be stated explicitly:** These studies span populations — emergency nurses, COVID-era healthcare workers, anesthesiologists and nurse anesthetists in simulation. Clinical context, specialty, years of experience, and measurement timing all vary. The convergence across these different samples is suggestive but not conclusive. The specific CRNA population has not been studied with the interoceptive measurement battery the literature now makes available. That is precisely the gap.

## The Framework

NANDA International (NANDA-I) maintains the taxonomy of nursing diagnoses — more than 267 diagnoses (as of the 2021-2023 edition) covering the full spectrum of human responses to health conditions. Every hospital EHR in America is built around this language. AANA CE courses are mapped to it. Nursing certification requires fluency in it. If a phenomenon cannot be named in NANDA-I, it effectively does not exist in the institutional infrastructure of nursing practice.

Tavares and Caldeira (2026) demonstrated what is at stake in diagnostic language through their analysis of a recent NANDA-I terminology shift: from "Spiritual Distress" to "Impaired Spiritual Well-Being." Their analysis showed that what appears to be a minor terminological refinement carries major clinical consequences — the new language, while more relational and preventive in framing, risks obscuring the acute severity of spiritual suffering that requires immediate intervention. Diagnosis language is not neutral. It shapes what clinicians see, what they document, what they fund, and what they treat.

Coutinho Borges et al. (2026) provided the methodological template for how new NANDA-I diagnoses are developed and validated. Their work developing the "Excessive Caregiving Burden" diagnosis used middle-range theory construction anchored in Betty Neuman's Systems Model, identifying 26 clinical indicators, 23 related factors, and 16 theoretical propositions before submitting to formal validation. The process is rigorous — and it is navigable.

The NANDA-I gaps that matter for this research domain are four:

**1. Impaired Autonomic Flexibility** — No current NANDA-I diagnosis captures the reduced capacity to shift between sympathetic and parasympathetic states on demand. This is what heart rate recovery (HRR) measures — the speed at which a CRNA's nervous system returns to baseline after arousal. It is not "Fatigue" (00093), not "Stress Overload" (00177), not "Anxiety" (00146). Those diagnoses capture states. Impaired Autonomic Flexibility is a capacity — a measurable, trainable, degradable capability.

**2. Impaired Interoceptive Awareness** — No NANDA-I diagnosis names the inability to accurately detect, interpret, and respond to internal physiological signals. Garfinkel et al.'s (2015) three-dimensional framework gives this phenomenon a precise scientific architecture. Mehling et al.'s (2012) MAIA provides the measurement tool. The clinical evidence — Upendra and Kaur (2026), Wickens (2026) — documents its prevalence and clinical significance. The diagnosis does not exist.

**3. Risk for Autonomic State Drift** — No NANDA-I diagnosis captures the progressive, undetected migration from optimal to suboptimal autonomic regulation across a clinical shift. This is not fatigue (which is experienced and reported). State drift is characteristically undetected — the clinician reports not feeling stressed while physiological markers show significant degradation. It is a surveillance failure within the body's own sensing apparatus.

**4. Readiness for Enhanced Autonomic Self-Regulation** — This is the health-promotion diagnosis that does not exist. NANDA-I has "Readiness for Enhanced Coping" (00158) and "Readiness for Enhanced Resilience" (00212), but no diagnosis that specifically names the opportunity to build HRV-measurable autonomic regulatory capacity through evidence-based training.

---

## The Failure Mode

There are two ways this framework breaks.

The first is the naming trap. A new diagnosis can be named so broadly that it collapses into existing diagnoses, or so narrowly that it cannot be coded in practice. "Impaired Autonomic Flexibility" must be distinguished from existing diagnoses by its defining characteristics — specifically, that it is a capacity measure (how quickly the nervous system recovers) rather than a state measure (how stressed the person currently is). The measurement is HRR and RMSSD, not the MBI.

The second failure mode is the evidence problem. NANDA-I's submission process requires concept analysis, literature review, expert panel review, and field testing. The evidence base for interoceptive assessment in nurses exists (Mehling 2012; Upendra & Kaur 2026; Mensinger et al. 2024) but the evidence specifically linking impaired autonomic flexibility to clinical error in CRNAs does not yet exist in a form that NANDA-I would accept. The Zafiriou et al. (2026) null finding — resilience not predicting HRV response in nurse anesthetists — is methodologically important precisely because it demonstrates that the existing measurement tools (standard resilience scales) are insufficient to capture the phenomenon. This is a strength of the argument for new diagnostics, not a weakness. But it means the evidence-building work is incomplete.

A new NANDA-I diagnosis cannot be submitted on theoretical grounds alone. It requires clinical data.

---

## The Test

The minimum viable experiment is this: a cross-sectional study of 40 CRNAs, stratified by years of clinical experience, completing a 90-minute assessment battery at the start and end of a clinical shift. Measures: MAIA-2 (interoceptive sensibility), HRR at 1 minute post-case (autonomic flexibility), and RMSSD across shift (state drift tracking via wearable).

Primary hypothesis: RMSSD declines significantly (p < .05) across the shift in CRNAs who score below median on MAIA-2 "Body Listening" and "Trusting" subscales at shift start. This would establish the predictive relationship between baseline interoceptive sensibility and intrashift autonomic state drift — the evidence base needed for a NANDA-I concept analysis submission.

Secondary hypothesis: HRR at 1 minute post-case correlates inversely with perceived workload (NASA-TLX), independently of years of experience.

What constitutes failure: no relationship between MAIA-2 subscales and RMSSD decline. This would challenge the interoceptive sensibility → autonomic regulation link in CRNA-specific context and would require reexamination of which dimension of interoception is the operative variable for this population.

What constitutes success: the data enables the first NANDA-I concept analysis paper specifically proposing "Impaired Autonomic Flexibility" as a new nursing diagnosis, with CRNAs as the index population and HRR as the defining characteristic measure.

## The Connection

This is not primarily a research project. It is a language strategy.

If Somnistics Research Labs successfully proposes and validates "Impaired Interoceptive Awareness" and "Risk for Autonomic State Drift" as NANDA-I diagnoses, every nurse in the world will eventually encounter these terms in their EHR, their CE requirements, and their certification exams. The Somnistics measurement infrastructure — MAIA-2 adapted for CRNA context, HRR benchmarks, structured interoception training — becomes the operational definition of those diagnoses.

The vault concepts [[interoceptive-suppression-hypothesis]], [[state-drift]], [[diaphragmatic-blindness]], and [[clinician-durability]] each map to one of the four proposed diagnoses. The NANDA submission process is a 3-5 year institutional investment. The research described in The Test section is where that investment begins.

What Tavares and Caldeira (2026) demonstrated about spiritual distress diagnoses applies here: the language that nursing uses to name suffering determines what nursing can treat. The four autonomic deficits are already being experienced by nurses and CRNAs in every hospital in America. The profession is living inside a vocabulary that cannot name them.

The body has always known. The field is catching up.
