# Notes from the Lab: Secure Attachment to Reality — The Somatic Root of Epistemic Stability

*What Randy Graybeal calls 'secure attachment to reality' — the capacity to engage with overwhelming complexity without ideological collapse or dissociative retreat — has a measurable somatic substrate: interoceptive accuracy and vagal flexibility are the physiology of epistemic stability.*

---

## The Observation

Randy identified the quality while reading Dr. Pippa Malmgren's geopolitical writing — the way she moved through contradictory, volatile information with a tone that was neither panicked nor dismissive. He named it: secure attachment to reality. Not psychological attachment to other people, but attachment to *what is actually happening*, regardless of how disturbing or ambiguous that thing is.

He has watched the inverse in the OR. Not among bad clinicians — among excellent ones. The CRNA who knows everything there is to know about airway management but, the moment something doesn't fit the plan, collapses into one of two failure modes: defensive certainty (insisting the data is wrong, the equipment is malfunctioning, the patient is unusual) or flooding (a cognitive freeze where the sheer volume of contradiction overwhelms the system's capacity to process any of it). Both failure modes look like clinical deficits. Neither is a knowledge problem. Both are somatic ones.

The question the concept demands: what is the physiology of the capacity to stay epistemically present — to hold complexity without either rigidifying around a false certainty or dissolving into it?

---

## The Mechanism

The starting point is counterintuitive: intolerance of uncertainty is not, at its root, a cognitive phenomenon. Freeston and Komes (2022) proposed what they call the **somatic error theory of intolerance of uncertainty**, arguing that the felt experience of uncertainty is fundamentally an interoceptive signal — a *felt sense of unsafety* arising from the body, not a judgment from the intellect [PMID: 36512913]. The mind then interprets this somatic alarm as a reason to seek certainty — through rigid conclusions, defensive ideation, or behavioral avoidance. The problem originates in the body's error signal, not in the person's reasoning capacity.

This matters enormously for clinical training, because it reframes the target. If intolerance of uncertainty were a cognitive bias, the intervention would be cognitive: teach people to sit with ambiguity, expose them to uncertain outcomes, change their beliefs about uncertainty's meaning. That approach has limited success. But if IU originates as a somatic error signal — a misfire in the interoceptive system's read of what uncertainty *feels like* — then the intervention is fundamentally physiological: train the body to register uncertainty without triggering the defensive alarm.

The autonomic mechanism runs through the vagus nerve. Magnon and colleagues (2022) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies and found a significant positive association between vagally-mediated heart rate variability and executive functioning (r = .19), with a critical nuance: HRV predicts cognitive inhibition and cognitive flexibility *more strongly* than working memory [PMID: 36030561]. This is the neurovisceral integration model — Thayer and Lane's insight that the vagal system, via its prefrontal connections, mediates the brain's capacity to inhibit irrelevant signals and sustain flexible attention on what matters. Higher vagal tone means better prefrontal inhibitory control means greater capacity to hold multiple perspectives in tension without collapsing.

Forte, Morelli, and Casagrande (2021) tested this directly. In 130 healthy adults completing the Iowa Gambling Task — a decision-making paradigm requiring tolerance of ambiguous feedback — good decision-makers showed significantly higher vagally-mediated HRV in resting, reactivity, and recovery phases [PMID: 33672004]. The body's regulatory capacity was measurable *before the difficult decision began*. The clinician who handles ambiguity well is not performing a cognitive feat in the moment; they have a physiology that allows flexibility to operate.

The interoceptive dimension links these findings. Furman and colleagues (2013) found that depressed women with impaired heartbeat perception — a measure of interoceptive accuracy — showed both reduced positive affectivity and difficulty in decision-making [PMID: 23972662]. Their conclusion: when the body's internal signals are not accurately perceived, the brain loses the feedback it needs to make quality decisions. Bodily data, properly registered, is not just an emotional resource — it is an epistemic one.

The final layer concerns the tolerance of ambivalence specifically — the capacity to hold *contradictory* information simultaneously. Verrienti (2026) reviewed the neural basis of emotional ambivalence, identifying a distributed network including the insula (the primary interoceptive cortex), the anterior cingulate cortex (conflict monitoring), prefrontal regions (valuation and regulation), and temporoparietal networks involved in social-cognitive integration [PMID: 42411784]. The capacity to tolerate ambivalence — to hold conflicting signals without prematurely resolving them — is neurologically inseparable from interoceptive processing. The insula is not incidental to ambivalence tolerance; it is the substrate.

The picture that assembles: **secure attachment to reality is the behavioral expression of a well-regulated interoceptive system.** A clinician who can engage with geopolitical chaos, or with an unexpected intraoperative event, or with a patient whose presentation contradicts the expected pattern, without either rigid collapse or dissociative flooding — that clinician has a body that registers uncertainty as a navigable signal rather than an emergency alarm. The difference between Malmgren's writing and the frozen OR moment is not intelligence. It is vagal tone.

But this relationship is not monotonic. Grol and De Raedt (2020) found that higher resting HRV related to *reduced* flexibility in shifting away from negative emotional information in anxious individuals — because for some individuals, greater affective flexibility enables attentional *avoidance* of negative signals rather than engagement with them [PMID: 32462431]. This is the critical confound: the apparent calm of some high-performers under complexity may not be secure attachment to reality — it may be dissociation with efficient neural packaging. Both can look identical from the outside. Only the direction of the somatic training determines which one it is.

---

## The Protocol

The somnistics training sequence addresses this in three phases, each building the somatic substrate for the next.

1. **Interoceptive baseline.** Establish accurate body-signal detection using MAIA-2 measurement (Not-Worrying, Self-Regulation, and Emotional Awareness subscales) and cardiac cycle awareness tasks. This reveals whether the person's interoceptive system is already performing some form of suppression — which is the common clinical pattern.

2. **Vagal conditioning.** Resonant frequency breathing at the individual's personal frequency (typically 4.5-7.0 BPM, determined via HRV biofeedback) for 20 minutes per session, 4-5 sessions per week. Lehrer's protocol establishes that vagal tone increases reliably with this training. Higher vagal tone directly supports the prefrontal inhibitory capacity that Magnon et al. demonstrate is necessary for cognitive flexibility.

3. **Intolerance of uncertainty exposure under somatic anchor.** Using gap moments — the existing 30-45 second clinical transitions in the workday — as deliberate exposure points for engaging with genuinely uncertain situations while maintaining somatic regulation through breath anchor. The training is not to think about uncertainty differently; it is to *feel differently* while thinking about uncertainty, in the body, in real clinical time.

Track progress with the IUS-12 (Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale, short form) and MAIA-2 reassessment at 30 and 90 days.

---

## The Failure Mode

This breaks under sustained allostatic load.

The prefrontal inhibitory control that enables epistemic flexibility is metabolically expensive. Sleep debt, cumulative allostatic burden, and chronic sympathetic activation all degrade vagal tone — and with it, the capacity for the sustained PFC engagement that uncertainty tolerance requires. The CRNA in year fourteen of night shifts and complex cases may have, paradoxically, *lower* baseline vagal tone than a SRNA — not because of anything they are doing wrong, but because of the cumulative cost of the environment.

The second failure mode is training the wrong construct. High-performing individuals often have well-developed interoceptive *sensibility* (strong subjective awareness of body signals) but degraded interoceptive *accuracy* (poor correlation between perceived and actual physiological state). A clinician who feels very in-touch with their body but whose felt sense is systematically miscalibrated will have confident but wrong somatic error signals. Their certainty under complexity is real; its accuracy is not.

Distinguish secure attachment to reality from **managed dissociation** — the ability to stay emotionally flat under complexity through systematic suppression of interoceptive signals. Both look like "calm under pressure." The physiological signature differs: the former shows high vagal tone and high interoceptive accuracy; the latter shows lower vagal tone, lower interoceptive accuracy, and MAIA-2 Not-Distracting scores paradoxically elevated (active suppression mislabeled as equanimity).

---

## The Test

Thirty CRNAs, divided into intervention (n=20) and wait-list control (n=10). Four-week protocol: daily resonant frequency breathing (20 minutes, individual frequency determined via Pausality biofeedback) plus weekly MAIA-2 and IUS-12 assessment.

Hypothesis: Intervention group will show significant IUS-12 reductions at 4 weeks, and HRV gain will partially mediate IU reduction — meaning the HRV increase is the mechanism through which uncertainty tolerance improves, not a parallel effect.

Secondary measure: Iowa Gambling Task performance at baseline and 4 weeks, comparing decision quality across intervention and control arms.

The design is small, short, and inexpensive. The hypothesis is testable. The measurement instruments are validated and in active use.

What would falsify it: IUS-12 reduction in the intervention group without corresponding HRV gains, suggesting a non-vagal pathway (such as cognitive exposure or expectation effects). That null result would be as informative as a positive one.

## The Connection

SRL has named many outcomes for its training: vagal tone, HRV coherence, interoceptive accuracy, gap moment density, resonant frequency calibration. These are all correct, but they are not the highest-order frame. Randy's concept of secure attachment to reality names the endpoint: a clinician who can engage with whatever is actually happening — in the body, in the OR, in the profession, in the world — without defensive escape in either direction.

This connects to [[relevance-realization]] (the cognitive machinery that determines what matters), [[interoceptive-literacy]] (the body-signal reading that feeds that machinery), and [[self-remembering]] (the meta-awareness that keeps the system calibrated under load). But it goes one level above all of them. Those are mechanisms. Secure attachment to reality is the outcome — the functional expression of a clinician who has trained their nervous system to stay present with what is.

In Vervaeke's terms: relevance realization in service of engagement rather than retreat.

In clinical terms: the CRNA who, when everything changes, gets more organized rather than less.

---

*What does it feel like from the inside? The heart rate rises — the body knows the situation is real. But it doesn't bolt the doors. The increased signal is available, legible, and navigable. The OR comes into focus, not out of it.*
