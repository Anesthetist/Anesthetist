# Notes from the Lab: CO2 Tolerance — The Biomarker That Predicts Who Breaks Under Pressure

*CO2 tolerance is not a measure of breath control; it is a measure of how much physiological pressure a nervous system can absorb before cognition cracks — and a timed breath-hold is the simplest pre-procedural test of that threshold available to any CRNA without hardware.*

---

## The Observation

A difficult intubation does not announce itself.

It begins the moment the laryngoscope enters and the glottis is not where expected. The clock inside the clinician's body starts running: SpO2 ticking, laryngeal anatomy shifting, the team watching. And somewhere in that first five seconds, most clinicians stop breathing.

Not as a technique. Not deliberately. The breath simply stops, arrested by the weight of concentration — and the body's own CO2 begins to rise.

What happens next is the test. Whether that rising CO2 triggers a cascade of sympathetic override — narrowed decision window, premature withdrawal, or performance freeze — or whether it simply registers as information, another data point among many: this depends on a neurological threshold most clinicians have never been told exists.

The literature calls it hypercapnic ventilatory response. SRL calls it the one biomarker you can measure in three minutes, in a locker room, before the first case of the day.

---

## The Mechanism

The brainstem contains two populations of neurons that together govern the relationship between breath and heart rate. Martinez, Dutschmann, Epercieux, Gourjon, and Joulia (2025) [PMID: 40096873] describe the circuit precisely: the **pre-Bötzinger complex** inhibits cardiac vagal motor neurons during inspiration — the heart accelerates on the inhale — while the **Kölliker-Fuse nucleus** mediates partial glottal constriction during early expiration — the heart decelerates on the exhale. These two nuclei, working in rhythmic opposition, generate what Martinez and colleagues call "respiratory heart rate variability": the visible, measurable signature that the autonomic nervous system is running in dynamic balance.

This is not metaphor. It is the anatomy of composure.

When arterial CO2 rises — during breath holds, during cognitive overload, during the involuntary apnea of a difficult intubation — central chemoreceptors in the brainstem detect the pH shift and issue a ventilatory override signal. How sensitively those receptors fire is the **hypercapnic ventilatory response (HCVR)**. This gain setting varies 3-5x between individuals. It is shaped by genetics, by chronic sleep debt, by unresolved allostatic load. And it is trainable.

Goossens, Leibold, Peeters, Esquivel, and colleagues (2014) [PMID: 24646808] established this with functional MRI: they imaged three populations during a CO2 challenge — panic disorder patients, healthy controls, and trained breath-hold divers. The findings confirmed what the freediving literature had long suggested but never before imaged in neural tissue. Panic patients showed the *highest* brainstem activation in response to rising CO2. Trained divers showed the *lowest*. The three groups form a spectrum, bounded at one end by catastrophic appraisal and at the other by integrated physiological information. The divers' brainstems had reorganized. Not suppressed — *reorganized*. The alarm architecture was identical across groups. The *gain on the alarm* had been durably reduced.

Arce-Álvarez, Veliz, Vazquez-Muñoz, and colleagues (2021) [PMID: 33716781] found the same pattern in competitive swimmers: trained swimmers showed significantly lower hypoxic ventilatory responses and sustained meaningfully longer voluntary breath holds compared to untrained controls. The adaptation was not willpower or tolerance through gritting. It was a reproducible reduction in chemoreceptor sensitivity that transferred across all voluntary apnea contexts.

The clinical implication, stated plainly: the CRNA whose CO2 tolerance has been trained — through deliberate breath-hold practice, resonant breathing protocols, or decades of intuitive self-management under load — perceives rising CO2 during a difficult airway as *information*. "I am four seconds into this attempt. The blade is shallow. The arytenoids are visible. I have twelve seconds before I need to call it." The CRNA whose tolerance is untrained perceives the same CO2 signal as *threat*. Executive function narrows. The decision window shrinks. They override the signal and push through, or they abort prematurely and disrupt the procedure.

The confound that must be named: CO2 tolerance does not operate in isolation. Three variables change simultaneously in every breath-hold training study — training volume, resting vagal tone, and deliberate practice of apnea technique. This essay isolates CO2 sensitivity as the primary measurable marker; the adaptation likely involves all three pathways concurrently. SRL's argument is not that CO2 tolerance alone explains clinical composure, but that voluntary breath-hold practice trains all three simultaneously — which is precisely why it is an efficient minimum-effective-dose intervention.

Martinez et al. (2025) [PMID: 40096873] established the underlying mechanism: regular breath-hold training increases cardiac vagal tone through Hebbian plasticity in the respirHRV brainstem circuit. Critically, this increase occurs *without structural cardiac remodeling* — unlike aerobic exercise training, which requires weeks of increased cardiac wall stress to produce vagal adaptations. Breath-hold training accesses the brainstem directly via volitional glottal closure. The implication for clinical populations: CO2 tolerance training is prescribed to bodies where high-intensity cardiovascular training carries additional risk.

Dujic, Ivancev, Heusser, and colleagues (2007) [PMID: 17991789] addressed the concern that trained divers might be paying a sympathetic cost for their CO2 desensitization — running at chronically elevated baseline sympathetic activity. Using microneurography to measure muscle sympathetic nerve activity directly, they found identical resting sympathetic outflow in elite divers compared to non-diver controls. The blood pressure was the same. The resting autonomic baseline was intact. The CO2 training produced durable adaptation, not sympathetic compensation.

And the final mechanism piece: Zaccaro, Piarulli, Laurino, and colleagues (2018) [PMID: 30245619], in a systematic review spanning 15 studies of slow breathing techniques, found that breathing below 10 breaths per minute reliably increased HRV and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, increased EEG alpha power, decreased EEG theta power, and increased prefrontal and subcortical brain activation. Slow breathing is not rest. It is controlled exposure to the CO2 accumulation that occurs in the pause between breaths — repeated, mild, voluntary. Each breath-hold cycle is a micro-dose of the stimulus that, over weeks, shifts the chemoreflex threshold in the direction of the trained diver.

## The Protocol

**The BOLT Score as Pre-Procedural Biomarker**

The Body Oxygen Level Test (BOLT) measures the comfortable pause — not maximum apnea, but the duration to the *first involuntary urge to breathe* after a normal exhalation. It takes three minutes. It requires no hardware.

1. Sit quietly for two minutes.
2. Take a normal breath in through the nose.
3. Exhale normally.
4. Pinch the nose. Start the timer.
5. At the first clear *involuntary diaphragm contraction* or *urge to breathe*, stop.
6. Record the time.

A BOLT score below 20 seconds indicates elevated chemosensitivity — the brainstem alarm fires quickly, CO2 reserve is low. Scores of 25-40 seconds indicate adequate reserve. Scores consistently above 40 suggest sustained adaptation toward the trained end of the sensitivity spectrum.

The score taken before a high-complexity case is today's autonomic availability reading. Tracked across weeks, it is the adaptation curve.

**Daily Training Protocol (minimum effective dose):**
1. Three voluntary breath holds to first urge only — not to maximum. Recover fully between holds.
2. Immediately follow with 5 minutes of slow nasal breathing at 4-6 breaths per minute.
3. Five days per week.
4. Track BOLT score weekly.

Success: ≥5-second BOLT improvement at 8 weeks. Plateau: <2-second improvement over 4 consecutive weeks — signal to review sleep quality and allostatic load before escalating protocol intensity.

## The Failure Mode

CO2 tolerance training produces the wrong outcome when the dose is wrong.

**Maximum-hold training** is the most common misapplication. The clinical benefit derives from the mild, repeated exposure to the first urge to breathe. Pushing to maximum apnea regularly adds physiological stress without additional adaptive benefit and introduces risk — vasovagal events, shallow water blackout in aquatic training contexts. The literature supports training to the first urge, not the last gasp.

**BOLT as a rank, not a trend.** The score varies with sleep quality, altitude, caffeine, time of day, and baseline anxiety. A low score on a difficult morning is that morning's data. As a week-over-week trend, BOLT is meaningful. As a single-session verdict on a clinician's resilience, it misleads.

**Confusing composure with competence.** CO2 tolerance buys cognitive clarity under physiological pressure. It does not replace technical skill. The test of training value is whether clearer cognition translates to better decisions — not whether the clinician can remain physiologically calm while making poor ones.

**Population means don't predict individual trajectories.** Every freediving study is built on group averages. Some practitioners show BOLT improvement in four weeks; others in twelve. SRL's application requires individual longitudinal tracking. Applying a group protocol as a fixed timeline will produce premature failure attributions in slow responders.

## The Test

Ten CRNAs. Fourteen days. Pausality for tracking.

- **Baseline (Day 1):** BOLT score, resting 5-minute RMSSD, MAIA-2-CRNA pre-procedural subscale.
- **Intervention:** Daily 5-minute breath-hold protocol (3 holds to first urge + 5 minutes resonant nasal breathing), logged via Pausality app.
- **Outcome measures:** Weekly BOLT score; daily RMSSD trend (wearable); self-reported pre-case composure rating (0-10, taken in the locker room on procedure days only).
- **Primary endpoint:** Correlation between Day-14 BOLT score and mean self-reported composure on procedure days 8-14, controlling for case complexity rating.
- **Secondary endpoint (signal extraction):** Does that morning's BOLT score predict that day's reported composure? If yes, the pre-case BOLT reading is a viable Pausality dashboard feature.

This is not a clinical outcomes study. It is a signal extraction study: does the BOLT → composure correlation hold in CRNAs? It is 14 days and 10 participants — the minimum data needed to justify a funded prospective study and the first entry in the SRL longitudinal CO2 dataset.

## The Connection

CO2 tolerance is where the [[somnistics]] system becomes measurable without a wearable. A breath-hold before a case, tracked across months, is the simplest longitudinal autonomic biomarker in the [[demand-biomarkers]] stack. It threads directly from [[minimum-effective-dose]] training (daily 5-minute sessions) through [[interoceptive-literacy]] (reading the apnea urge as information rather than threat) to [[clinician-durability]] — the career-length arc of a nervous system that gets stronger under load rather than wearing through.

The connection to [[hormesis-durability-model]] is precise: voluntary CO2 stress is a controlled micro-adversity. Each comfortable breath hold is a deliberate dose of the same hypercapnic stimulus that every difficult airway delivers involuntarily. Practiced systematically, the same brainstem circuits that generate panic under load reorganize to generate information. That difference — between an alarm and a data point — may be the physiological signature of a 28-year career versus a 12-year career.
