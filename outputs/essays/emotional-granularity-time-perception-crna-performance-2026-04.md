# Notes from the Lab: The Color of the Seconds

*Emotional Granularity, Time Perception, and the CRNA Who Learned to Slow the Clock*

---

*Emotional granularity — the capacity to construct fine-grained emotional categories in real time — functions as a temporal dilation tool for clinicians under pressure. The CRNA who can name what they feel with precision experiences more subjective time per objective second, because the insular cortex that constructs emotional categories is the same neural hub that generates the subjective sense of duration.*

---

## The Observation

[RANDY: A clinical moment where time distorted during a critical case — the seconds between recognizing a desaturation and acting on it, or a moment where the room compressed and expanded simultaneously. What color was the feeling? Not "stress" — something more specific. The granularity of that moment is the opening.]

Every CRNA knows the phenomenon. The monitors alarm. The oxygen saturation drops. And time does something strange — it stretches like taffy in one dimension (the seconds between recognition and action feel impossibly long) while compressing in another (the entire event, in retrospect, lasted forty-five seconds that felt like four minutes or four seconds depending on which axis you measure). The experience is not fear, exactly. Not anxiety. Not even arousal in the generic sense. It is something more specific — a textured, layered state that the English language does not have a single word for, because the English language was not designed inside an operating room.

The clinical literature calls this tachypsychia — the subjective distortion of time under acute stress. But the word describes the phenomenon without explaining the mechanism. And the mechanism, it turns out, is not about time at all. It is about emotion. Specifically, it is about how many colors of emotion your nervous system can construct in the seconds that matter most.

---

## The Vocabulary Problem

In 1987, Richard Lane and Gary Schwartz proposed something elegant: emotional awareness develops in stages, like cognition itself. Their five-level model, published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*, mapped a progression from primitive bodily sensation to nuanced meta-emotional awareness — a developmental ladder from "my stomach hurts" to "I am experiencing a blend of anticipatory vigilance and procedural confidence tempered by awareness that I am more fatigued than I was four hours ago" (Lane & Schwartz, 1987; DOI: 10.1176/ajp.144.2.133).

Level 1: Emotions experienced only as bodily sensations. Stomach ache. Muscle tension. No label.

Level 2: Emotions experienced as action tendencies. The urge to flee. The urge to fight. Body-level but without cognitive differentiation.

Level 3: Single, undifferentiated emotions. "I feel bad." "I feel stressed." The vocabulary is binary — good or bad, on or off.

Level 4: Multiple simultaneous emotions recognized. "I feel both vigilant and uneasy — the vigilance is serving me but the unease is degrading my fine motor control."

Level 5: Full awareness of one's own and others' complex emotional states. Meta-emotional awareness. The capacity to feel what you feel, know what it means, and recognize how it is shaping your performance in this moment.

Most clinicians, when asked about their own emotional state during a case, operate at Level 3. "Stressed." "Fine." "Focused." These words are not wrong. They are just too blunt to be useful. It is the difference between monitoring a patient's hemodynamics with a blood pressure cuff and monitoring them with an arterial line, continuous cardiac output, and a transesophageal echo. The resolution of the instrument determines the precision of the intervention.

Lisa Feldman Barrett named this resolution in 2017. She called it **emotional granularity** — the capacity to construct fine-grained, differentiated emotional categories rather than broad, undifferentiated ones (Barrett, 2017; ISBN: 978-0544133310). Barrett's theory of constructed emotion argues that emotions are not hardwired reactions triggered by the world. They are active constructions of the brain — predictions assembled from three ingredients: interoceptive signals (what the body is doing), prior experience (what similar states have meant before), and context (what is happening right now).

The implication is profound: emotions are not things that happen *to* you. They are things your brain *builds*. And the precision of the construction depends on the vocabulary — the conceptual repertoire — available during the build.

A person with low emotional granularity experiences their affective life in crayon — a few broad strokes. Stressed. Happy. Anxious. Sad. A person with high emotional granularity experiences it in oil paint — layered, blended, specific. The difference is not aesthetic. It is functional. Barrett's research demonstrates that people with higher emotional granularity show better emotion regulation, fewer depressive episodes, less reactive aggression, and more adaptive coping under stress. The resolution of the feeling determines the precision of the response.

## The Color Wheel

Marc Brackett understood that granularity must be trained, not merely described. As founding director of the Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence, Brackett developed the **Mood Meter** — a two-axis color wheel that maps emotions along pleasantness (horizontal) and energy (vertical) into four quadrants:

**Red** (high energy, low pleasantness): anger, anxiety, frustration, alarm — the states that scream.

**Yellow** (high energy, high pleasantness): excitement, joy, optimism, flow — the states that sing.

**Blue** (low energy, low pleasantness): sadness, fatigue, depletion, resignation — the states that drain.

**Green** (low energy, high pleasantness): calm, contentment, serenity, gratitude — the states that sustain.

Within each quadrant, the Mood Meter populates dozens of emotion words at increasing levels of specificity. Not just "red" but *which* red — is this alarm (imminent threat perceived) or indignation (violation of professional standards) or exasperation (repeated systemic failure)? Not just "green" but *which* green — is this relief (threat passed) or equanimity (acceptance regardless of outcome) or groundedness (stable connection to the present)?

Brackett's RULER framework — Recognizing, Understanding, Labeling, Expressing, and Regulating — places the color wheel at the center of a practice. The insight embedded in the sequence is not obvious until you work with it: recognition precedes regulation. You cannot modulate what you cannot name. The color comes before the control.

Now move this into an operating room. Between cases. The thirty-second walk from OR 3 to OR 7. What color are you? Not in general. Right now. What quadrant? What shade within the quadrant? And — here is where amplitude enters — how saturated is the color? A pale yellow is contentment; a screaming yellow is euphoria. A dark red is simmering frustration; a blazing red is acute alarm. The position on the wheel names the emotion. The saturation measures its amplitude. Both matter. Both are trainable.

## The Diversity Finding

In 2023, Katie Hoemann, Yeasle Lee, Peter Kuppens, Maria Gendron, and Ryan Boyd published a finding that connects emotional granularity to something unexpected: the diversity of a person's daily experience. Using natural language processing methods to analyze descriptions of everyday events across three studies — in English and Dutch, written and spoken — they demonstrated that participants who encountered a more varied and balanced set of contexts and activities constructed more differentiated and nuanced negative emotions (Hoemann et al., 2023; *Affective Science*, 4, 291–306; DOI: 10.1007/s42761-023-00185-2).

The finding runs in a direction that matters for clinicians. It is not that granular people seek more diverse experiences. It is that diverse experiences build the conceptual repertoire that enables granularity. Each new context — each patient, each case, each complication, each recovery — adds a new shade to the palette. The CRNA with 10,000 cases has encountered more emotional contexts than almost any professional on earth. The raw material for extraordinary granularity is already there. What is missing is the practice of naming it.

Hoemann's finding also contained an asymmetry: experiential diversity predicted granularity for negative emotions but not consistently for positive ones. This makes clinical sense. The operating room generates an extraordinary diversity of threat-adjacent experiences — each case a unique combination of patient physiology, surgical demands, pharmacological responses, and interpersonal dynamics. The negative-emotion palette is forced to diversify by the nature of the work. But the positive palette — the shades of satisfaction, pride, connection, flow — often goes undifferentiated because there is no practice of attending to it. "Good case" covers everything from quiet competence to transcendent clinical artistry. The positive side of the wheel is undertrained.

## The Temporal Bridge

Here is where the essay turns from emotion to time, because the two are not separate.

Orestis Giotakos, writing in *Psychiatriki* in 2025, reviewed the insular cortex as a convergence hub linking three functions that neuroscience has traditionally studied in isolation: interoceptive processing, time perception, and self-representation (Giotakos, 2025; DOI: 10.22365/jpsych.2025.026). The finding that binds this essay together: interoceptive accuracy correlates with both vagal activity and time perception accuracy. The organ that senses the body's interior is the organ that constructs the subjective sense of duration.

Bud Craig established the architecture in 2009. The anterior insular cortex generates what Craig called "global emotional moments" — successive integrations of all interoceptive, emotional, and cognitive data into discrete pulses of subjective time. Each pulse is a snapshot. The rate of snapshot generation determines the subjective sense of how fast time passes. More snapshots per objective second means more subjective time — time dilates. Fewer snapshots means time compresses.

What governs the rate? Arousal and interoceptive engagement. Under acute stress, arousal drives the snapshot rate up — the internal pacemaker accelerates — and time subjectively dilates. This is tachypsychia. The seconds stretch. But the dilation is chaotic — uncontrolled, anxiety-driven, paired with tunnel vision and degraded executive function. The time stretches but the clinician cannot use the extra seconds because the cognitive architecture is compromised.

Now consider a different kind of dilation. Not the arousal-driven acceleration of the pacemaker under threat, but the interoceptive-driven density of the snapshot under trained attention. The fronto-striatal clock model described in the vault's temporal distortion synthesis identifies slow breathing as a temporal engineering mechanism: parasympathetic activation modulates dopaminergic tone in the mesolimbic system, which modulates the pacemaker rate, which modulates the subjective sense of duration. Slow breathing at resonant frequency does not just calm the nervous system. It literally slows the internal clock — producing the phenomenon where sixty objective seconds feel like ninety (Lehrer, Vaschillo & Vaschillo, 2000).

But there is a second pathway — and this is where emotional granularity enters as a temporal tool. The predictive processing model of time perception (Fountas, Seth & Roseboom, 2022; *Neural Computation*; DOI: 10.1162/neco_a_01514) demonstrates that perceived duration is a function of prediction error: the more novel information processed per unit time, the longer the duration feels. This explains why crises feel long (maximum prediction error) and routine feels short (minimal surprise). It also explains why emotional granularity would dilate time.

A clinician operating at Lane and Schwartz's Level 3 — "I feel stressed" — processes one emotional prediction per snapshot. A clinician operating at Level 5 — "I feel a blend of procedural confidence in my airway skills, rising alertness about the patient's hemodynamic trend, mild frustration at the incomplete handoff, and a layer of physical fatigue that I know is affecting my fine motor threshold" — processes multiple differentiated predictions per snapshot. Each differentiated emotion is information. Each piece of information generates a prediction that requires updating. The prediction-error density per unit time increases. Time dilates — not through chaotic arousal, but through granular attention.

This is the bridge. Emotional granularity is a temporal dilation tool. The clinician who names what they feel with precision generates more informational content per insular snapshot, which produces the subjective experience of having more time. Not the panicked dilation of tachypsychia. The controlled dilation of trained attention — where time expands and executive function remains intact.

## The Practice: Coloring the Gap

What does this look like operationally? Consider the gap moment — the thirty seconds between cases, between intubation and maintenance, between emergence and extubation. The transition windows that Gap Moment Training identifies as the most valuable real estate in a clinician's nervous system.

The standard NeuroMinute protocol begins with breath. Five breaths at resonant frequency. Orient, deepen, return. This already modulates time perception through the parasympathetic-dopaminergic pathway — the slow-breathing temporal dilation described above.

Now layer an emotional color wheel check on top of it. Not a formal assessment. A two-second internal query: *What color am I? What quadrant? What shade? What amplitude?*

Breath one: Orient. Feel the body. Notice the color — am I red, yellow, blue, green?

Breath two: Name the shade. Not "stressed" — is this alarm or vigilance or frustration or depletion? The naming itself generates prediction error because it forces the brain to differentiate a signal it was previously processing as a single blob. Time dilates.

Breath three: Assess amplitude. How saturated is this color? A 3 out of 10 vigilance is useful; an 8 out of 10 vigilance is degrading performance. The amplitude assessment adds another layer of informational density. Another prediction to update. More subjective time.

Breaths four and five: Deepen and return. The state has been named. The amplitude has been assessed. Now the Gross process model kicks in: because the emotion was caught antecedent to full construction (Gross, 1998; DOI: 10.1037/1089-2680.2.3.271), cognitive change is available. The CRNA can reappraise — not suppress, not ignore, but reframe: "This is a 7 out of 10 frustration about the handoff, and it is not relevant to the airway I am about to manage. I am going to keep the frustration on the shelf and let the vigilance — which is at a 4, which is useful — drive the next five minutes."

The entire sequence takes sixty seconds. But because of the temporal dilation produced by both slow breathing (parasympathetic pathway) and emotional granularity (predictive processing pathway), the subjective experience is longer. The clinician arrives at the next case with more perceived time behind them than the clock would suggest. They feel less rushed. Less compressed. More spacious. Not because the objective minutes changed, but because the informational density per moment increased.

## The Repeated Wheel

Brackett's Mood Meter was designed for school-age children and general populations. Its genius is its simplicity — two axes, four colors, a granularity gradient within each quadrant. But for clinical performance, the power comes from repetition.

A CRNA who checks the color wheel once per shift gets a snapshot. A CRNA who checks it at every gap moment — between cases, during holds, at handoffs — gets a time series. And the time series reveals what a snapshot cannot: drift. The slow migration from yellow (energized, positive) at 7 AM to green (calm, depleted) at 11 AM to blue (low energy, negative) at 3 PM to red (frustrated, reactive) at 6 PM. This is not a personality trait. It is an autonomic trajectory — the predictable arc of a nervous system under sustained load.

The repeated check transforms the color wheel from an assessment into a **biofeedback loop without the device**. The clinician becomes their own instrument. The amplitude tracking across a shift reveals patterns that no wearable captures: when does the color shift? What triggers the migration? Which cases push the palette toward red, and which pull it back toward green? When does the amplitude spike, and what preceded it?

Brené Brown catalogued eighty-seven distinct emotions in *Atlas of the Heart* — a taxonomy of human feeling at a resolution most people never access. The color wheel is the entry point to that taxonomy. Over time, with practice, the four quadrants differentiate. The reds separate into alarm, indignation, exasperation, dread. The greens separate into relief, equanimity, groundedness, satisfaction. The vocabulary becomes the instrument. And the instrument, wielded repeatedly across a shift, generates the experiential diversity that Hoemann's research showed feeds back into greater granularity. The practice builds the skill that makes the practice more precise. A virtuous loop.

## Slowing Time on Purpose

The literature converges on a claim that sounds impossible until you understand the mechanism: a clinician can learn to slow time down.

Not metaphorically. Not through "being present" in the vague mindfulness sense. Through two specific, trainable pathways that share a neural substrate in the insular cortex:

**Pathway 1: Parasympathetic temporal dilation.** Slow breathing at resonant frequency (~5.5 breaths per minute) activates the parasympathetic nervous system, modulates dopaminergic tone via the mesolimbic pathway, and slows the fronto-striatal pacemaker. Sixty objective seconds are experienced as longer. The evidence base includes Lehrer, Vaschillo, and Vaschillo (2000) on resonant frequency breathing, Six Dijkstra and colleagues (2019) on the one-minute HRV protocol, and the Craig (2009) insular model of temporal snapshot generation.

**Pathway 2: Granularity-driven informational dilation.** Fine-grained emotional categorization increases prediction error density per unit time, which the predictive processing model (Fountas et al., 2022) demonstrates produces the subjective experience of longer duration. Each differentiated emotion is a data point. Each data point generates a prediction requiring an update. More updates per second means more subjective seconds per objective second.

The two pathways are additive. A clinician practicing slow breathing while naming their emotional state with granularity is engaging both the autonomic and cognitive temporal dilation mechanisms simultaneously. The gap moment that objectively lasts thirty seconds expands — not infinitely, but meaningfully — into a subjective space where assessment, naming, amplitude checking, and state regulation all become possible.

This is what it means to engineer time with the body. The Teghil and Wittmann (2025) meta-analysis of meta-analyses — the highest level of evidence available — confirms that subjective time is processed in the same insular cortex that processes interoception. Training interoception is training time perception. Adding emotional granularity adds informational richness to each interoceptive snapshot. The result is a clinician who has more time — not in any mystical sense, but in the measurable sense that their brain generates more experiential content per objective second than a clinician operating at Level 3 with shallow breathing and an undifferentiated "stressed."

## The Closing Image

[RANDY: A moment where this came together for you — a case where you noticed the color, named the shade, felt time open up, and made a clinical decision in the space that opened. Or the inverse: a moment where the lack of granularity compressed time and the seconds weren't enough. The body is the evidence. The color is the data. The seconds are the canvas.]

The seconds are not fixed. They never were. They are constructed by the same neural machinery that constructs how you feel — the anterior insula integrating interoceptive signals, emotional predictions, and temporal snapshots into a single stream of conscious experience. The precision of your emotional vocabulary is not a soft skill. It is a chronometric instrument. Each shade of feeling you can name is another tick of the internal clock, another snapshot in the sequence, another fraction of a second gained.

The CRNA between cases has thirty seconds on the wall clock. How many seconds they actually experience depends on what they do with their breath and what they can name about their own state. The color wheel is a clock. The breath is a dial. And the gap moment is where both become operational.

Five breaths. One color. One shade within the color. One amplitude. Sixty seconds that feel like ninety.

That is enough.
