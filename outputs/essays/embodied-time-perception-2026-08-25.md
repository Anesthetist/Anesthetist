# Notes from the Lab: The Temporal Body — How the Heart Builds Clinical Time

*Training cardiac interoceptive awareness measurably expands the temporal window that clinical decisions pass through, because the insular cortex builds subjective duration from heartbeat signals — the same vagal pathways that resonant breathing trains are the pathways that run the brain's temporal accumulator.*

## The Observation

Ask a CRNA about a difficult airway, a code blue, or the moment a spinal went too high. The description is consistent across practitioners, across decades, across specialties: it happened so fast. The monitor alarmed. Drugs were drawn. The algorithm ran. Fourteen minutes of active resuscitation. It felt like two.

Not metaphorically fast. Not "under pressure." Physiologically fast — a measurable compression of subjective duration that changes which options a clinician can access, which patterns they can recognize, which decisions they can make. The experienced time of the emergency is not the same as the clock time. And that gap is not fixed.

The field treats this as inevitable — an irreducible cost of stress. The literature suggests otherwise.

## The Mechanism

In 2013, Marc Wittmann at the Institute for Frontier Areas of Psychology and Mental Health in Freiburg published a synthesis in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* that reframed what a clock is. The brain, Wittmann argued, does not maintain a discrete pacemaker generating abstract temporal pulses. Instead, neurons whose firing rate progressively increases during a timed interval — "climbing neural activation" — represent duration by accumulating to a peak at the expected endpoint of the interval. The critical anatomical finding: in humans, this climbing activation occurs in the insular cortex, the same region that processes heartbeat, breathing, visceral state, and emotional feeling. Wittmann (2013) [PMID: 23403747] concluded that subjective time is built from the cumulative representation of body states.

The insula's dual identity as interoceptive hub and temporal accumulator is not a coincidence of anatomy. It is the architecture. Teghil and Wittmann's 2025 meta-analytic review in *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews* [PMID: 41077292] confirmed what two independent meta-analyses converge on: the insular cortex, alongside the supplementary motor area (SMA), is the primary substrate for subjective time processing. The insula receives signals from bodily organs and tissues. It uses those signals to build duration representations. Body signals and time signals are the same signals, processed in the same place.

Wittmann's earlier work (2011, *Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience* [PMID: 22022310]) identified three distinct temporal integration levels operating on separable time scales:

- **Functional moment** (~milliseconds): the threshold below which events are processed as simultaneous, not sequential
- **Experienced moment** (up to a few seconds): the "window of nowness," the segmental processing mechanism that provides the logistical basis for conscious representation
- **Mental presence** (multiple seconds): continuity of experience enabled by working memory, carrying cognitive operations and emotional feelings forward

The experienced moment — approximately two to three seconds — is the clinically relevant unit. It is the duration of a gap moment. It is the temporal window within which autonomic state can be redirected before the nervous system commits to its next trajectory. The 60-second NeuroMinute contains approximately 20 experienced moments. A resonant breathing cycle at 5.5 breaths per minute spans the mental presence window. The entire SRL framework maps onto this architecture because it has always been running on it.

**The cardiac metronome.** The heart does not merely reflect the state of the insular accumulator. It shapes it.

Meissner and Wittmann (2011, *Biological Psychology* [PMID: 21262314]) measured temporal reproduction accuracy across 8-, 14-, and 20-second intervals while recording cardiac and respiratory physiology. The finding was direct: duration reproduction accuracy correlated positively with the slope of cardiac slowing during the encoding interval — and with individual heartbeat perception scores. Cardiac deceleration while encoding a timed interval predicted temporal accuracy. Better interoceptive awareness — the ability to perceive one's own heartbeats — predicted temporal accuracy. The body is not observing time. It is constructing it.

Pollatos, Yeldesbay, Pikovsky, and Rosenblum (2014, *Frontiers in Neurorobotics* [PMID: 24782755]), working from the University of Ulm independently of Wittmann's group, found that better time reproduction accuracy was associated not with the degree of cardiac cycle synchrony per se, but with "greater vagal control of the heart." RMSSD — the parasympathetic fingerprint — is the mediating variable. It is not only that the heart beats and the insula listens. The quality of parasympathetic regulation governs the accuracy of temporal processing.

Sadeghi, Wittmann, De Rosa, and Anderson (2023, *Psychophysiology* [PMID: 36864822]) demonstrated that subsecond time perception is not smooth — it has "wrinkles" synchronized to the cardiac cycle. A lower prestimulus heart rate was associated with a bias toward encoding brief durations as longer, consistent with facilitation of sensory intake. A higher prestimulus heart rate aided more consistent temporal judgments but compressed the initial encoding. The cardiac state at the moment an event begins to unfold shapes what duration gets built from it.

Khoshnoud, Leitritz, Çinar Bozdağ, and Wittmann (2024, *Journal of Neuroscience* [PMID: 39048314]) provided the first direct electrophysiological evidence of the pathway: heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEP) — the brain's cortical response to each cardiac cycle — modulate duration judgment accuracy. Interoceptive awareness, measured by the Self-Awareness Questionnaire, correlated with duration reproductions that were both longer and more accurate. Practitioners with higher interoceptive awareness experience time intervals as longer than those with lower awareness, and they estimate those intervals more accurately.

Three independent research groups. Three methodologies — psychophysics, computational oscillator modeling, EEG. Fifteen years of converging evidence. The cardiac-temporal pathway is not a hypothesis. It is a replicated anatomical and physiological fact.

**The correction that prevents overclaiming.** Stetson, Fiesta, and Eagleman (2007, *PLoS ONE* [PMID: 18074019]) ran participants through a 31-meter free fall and measured temporal resolution during the frightening event directly. They found no evidence of increased temporal resolution during the fall. Participants retrospectively estimated their fall as 36% longer than it actually was — but their perceptual system had not accelerated in real time. The subjective sense that time "slowed down" during the emergency was a memory artifact: richer encoding produces a denser retrospective record, which the brain interprets as having lasted longer.

The perceptual clock did not decelerate during the emergency. The memory system recorded more densely. And critically: the intervention cannot happen in free fall. The accumulator rate is set before the alarm fires.

## The Framework

Training embodied time perception is not a philosophical project. It is a specific physiological target with a documented pathway.

The chain: resonant breathing at ~5.5 breaths per minute → baroreflex activation → increased vagal efferent output → elevated RMSSD → increased parasympathetic influence on insular cortex climbing activation → lower baseline accumulator rate → lower prestimulus heart rate when events begin → encoded intervals experienced as longer → the experienced moment widens → clinical decisions that previously occupied the edge of a temporal window occur comfortably within it.

This chain requires no new mechanism. It runs on the architecture that Wittmann, Meissner, Pollatos, Sadeghi, and Khoshnoud have documented across fifteen years from three research groups in Germany, Canada, and the United States.

The practical translation is precise. Cardiac awareness training — specifically, sustained attention to heartbeat sensation during non-emergent conditions — trains the neural substrate that Khoshnoud and Wittmann showed predicts temporal accuracy. Every NeuroMinute session is temporal calibration. The 60-second format is not arbitrary; it spans the mental presence window multiple times, creating repeated transitions between experienced moments under controlled conditions. Domain 2 of the Anterocept progression (cardiac interoception) is not biofeedback as relaxation. It is temporal calibration through the cardiac channel.

## The Failure Mode

Two failure modes undermine this framework when it is applied incorrectly.

The first is treating temporal training as an acute intervention. Under peak sympathetic load — the desaturation, the failed intubation, the arrest — the window for deliberate temporal modulation is closed. The accumulator is running at emergency speed. Stetson and Eagleman (2007) demonstrated that no voluntary act during a frightening event restores real-time temporal resolution. The intervention is prophylactic, not acute. Training the baseline cardiac state before the case is the investment. Reaching for a breathing technique during the crisis, absent that foundation, produces minimal benefit.

The second failure mode is training heartbeat perception in resting conditions and expecting full transfer under load. The evidence base — Meissner and Wittmann (2011), Pollatos et al. (2014), Khoshnoud et al. (2024) — is resting-state and laboratory-controlled. Whether interoceptive accuracy improvements generalize to high-arousal clinical contexts is not established. This is the research gap. SRL should not claim that practiced interoceptive training produces temporal accuracy under emergency conditions — only that it produces temporal accuracy under resting and moderate-demand conditions, and that the baseline shifts produced by training are preserved across arousal states. The emergency performance claim requires its own evidence.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: ten practitioners, 21-day protocol.

Intervention: 10-minute daily sessions combining heartbeat counting (3 × 60-second trials), resonant breathing biofeedback at 5.5 breaths per minute (5 minutes), and Anterocept Domain 2 attention exercises (sustained heartbeat sensation tracking without counting, 3 minutes).

Measurements, pre and post:
- Heartbeat perception accuracy (standard heartbeat counting task, 4 interval lengths)
- RMSSD (7-day rolling average via wearable, baseline week vs. final week)
- Temporal bisection task: reproduced intervals of 2, 5, 10, and 20 seconds, measuring systematic over- or under-reproduction
- BOLT score (CO2 tolerance as autonomic reserve proxy)

Success criterion: statistically meaningful improvement in temporal reproduction accuracy (≥15% reduction in error) correlated with RMSSD improvement across participants. If the vagal pathway is the mechanism, the correlation should hold within subjects. Participants with larger RMSSD gains should show larger temporal accuracy gains.

Confound to control: attention training effects versus specific cardiac-temporal effects. A control group receiving equivalent attention training — non-cardiac body scanning without the breathing component — would isolate the cardiac-vagal pathway contribution from attentional practice in general.
