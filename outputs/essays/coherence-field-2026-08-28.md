# Notes from the Lab: The Field You Cannot See — Coherence as Team Infrastructure in the Operating Room

*A surgical team's aggregate autonomic coherence is a measurable environmental variable — not a metaphor — that governs case duration and communication quality; one regulated team member elevates the field for everyone, one dysregulated member collapses it, and no current clinical training system deliberately targets this.*

---

## The Observation

The surgical timeout ends. Eight people in a room. The anesthesiologist adjusts a line. The scrub tech counts instruments. The circulator marks the board. The surgeon stares at the ceiling.

Then something happens that is not in the safety checklist: one of them takes a slow, full breath. Not on purpose. Not as a protocol. Just — a breath. And the room changes. The scrub tech's shoulders drop. The surgeon's eyes come down from the ceiling. The pace slows by three seconds and simultaneously sharpens. Anyone who has worked enough cases knows this moment. It is not mystical. It is not imagination. It is the coherence field arriving.

Randy Graybeal, 28 years in high-stakes anesthesia, put it plainly: "When the CRNA is regulated, the whole room changes." That observation is the seed of the coherence-field concept — the hypothesis that the collective autonomic state of a team is not merely an aggregate of individuals but an emergent environmental property that every member operates inside.

This essay is the step from dyadic to collective. The previous work on interpersonal physiological synchrony established that two nervous systems in proximity entrain to each other — a measurable, bidirectional, clinically consequential phenomenon. The coherence-field concept asks: what happens when you have eight nervous systems in the same room, every day, under life-or-death conditions?

---

## The Mechanism

The individual coherence story is well established. Elbers and McCraty (2025) [PMID: 41445965] reviewed a decade of HeartMath research showing that slow, rhythmic breathing combined with positive emotional engagement produces a specific HRV pattern — high-amplitude, low-frequency oscillations at approximately 0.1 Hz — that HeartMath terms "coherence." This coherent state is associated with increased vagal activity, synchronization across cardiac, respiratory, and brain rhythms, reduced perceived stress, improved cognitive performance, and a more resilient autonomic baseline.

The coherence state is individual. The coherence field is what happens when multiple individuals are in that state simultaneously.

McCraty (2017) [PMID: 29075623] proposed that social coherence — "a network of relationships in which individuals share common interests and objectives" — can be measured and augmented through group-level HRV synchronization. His review of infant-caregiver, couple, and small-group synchrony studies led to three hypotheses: that providing feedback of individual and collective HRV coherence will increase group coherence; that training in group coherence techniques will increase prosocial behavior, cooperation, and communication; and that the heart's biomagnetic field may be a primary mechanism for HRV synchronization among group members.

These are hypotheses, not established causal chains. The controlled trials in clinical teams do not yet exist. What does exist is the clearest proxy data the field has produced.

Powezka et al. (2022) [PMID: 36433593] measured HRV continuously in ENT and vascular surgical teams at Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust. They identified four HRV synchrony metrics and tested their correlation with surgical outcomes. The finding: length of procedure was inversely correlated with all four HRV synchrony metrics (*p* values ranging from 0.002 to <0.0001). Teams with higher HRV synchrony during the case completed the procedure faster. Intraoperative stress, measured by STAI questionnaire, was positively correlated with longer procedures (*p* = 0.007, *R* = 0.493). Team familiarity enhanced synchrony.

This is not a causal study. But it is the clearest available signal: the physiological climate of a surgical team is not background noise. It is correlated with how long that team takes to complete a procedure, and by extension, with patient exposure time, resource utilization, and the cumulative stress load on every person in the room.

McCraty (2022) [PMID: 35731454] described the mechanism pathway: individual HRV coherence biofeedback training builds the capacity for self-regulation under stress. That trained capacity, when present in multiple members of a team, creates conditions for elevated group physiological synchrony — not because team members are consciously coordinating, but because regulated nervous systems are more responsive to social entrainment cues: vocal prosody, respiratory rate, postural signals, and possibly the cardiac electromagnetic field, which extends approximately three to four feet from the body.

The working model is this: individual coherence is a skill. Group coherence is an emergent property of that skill applied in proximity. You cannot train the coherence field directly. You train the people who generate it.

The confound to state explicitly: in Powezka's data, team familiarity also predicts synchrony, and familiarity is not trainable by a breathing protocol. Multiple variables changed in every case. The synchrony-to-performance link is real, but the specific contribution of autonomic coherence versus procedural familiarity has not been isolated.

## The Protocol

The minimum viable coherence-field intervention already exists in clinical workflow. The surgical timeout.

The safety timeout is, physiologically, a natural synchrony event: eight people pause simultaneously, attend to a shared object, speak in sequence, and stop moving. This is the exact conditions under which physiological coupling research consistently finds that synchrony increases. The timeout is already a coherence priming opportunity. No one is using it that way.

The protocol: after the standard timeout completes and before the first incision, the most regulated person in the room takes one visible, audible, slow breath — specifically, a five-second inhale and five-second exhale. Not announced. Not mandatory. Just done, clearly enough that three people near them mirror it.

This is the "Team Breath Before First Case" — the application of Gap Moment Training at the team level. It requires nothing of the team except one trained clinician with the capacity to broadcast regulated state. The CRNA, positioned at the head of the table, in continuous contact with the most sensitive monitoring in the room, facing the team, is the natural anchor.

The enterprise extension: team-level HRV wearable monitoring during cases. Not for surveillance — for feedback. An aggregate team coherence score available at shift's end would give clinical leadership an objective signal they have never had: is this team's physiological climate improving over training, degrading under staffing pressure, or holding flat?

## The Failure Mode

The authority gradient is a physiological hierarchy.

In any group, physiological synchrony is not symmetric. One person leads; others follow. In the interpersonal synchrony literature, the leader is typically the person with higher social status, stronger emotional intensity, or more dominant vocal pattern. In an OR, that is almost always the attending surgeon.

This creates the central failure mode: a regulated CRNA cannot overcome a dysregulated surgeon. If the senior surgeon enters the room with elevated sympathetic arousal — post-conflict, late start, previous case complication — their state floods the field. The CRNA's coherent baseline becomes a boat in rough water rather than a regulating force.

McCraty (2017) reviewed evidence suggesting that the heart's electromagnetic field can influence others' neural oscillations, but also noted that this influence operates across short distances and with highly variable individual sensitivity. The regulated person is not guaranteed to win the entrainment contest. Status, emotional intensity, and proximity all modulate who sets the field's tone.

The second failure mode is cumulative: coherence debt. A CRNA who runs three complex cases without between-case recovery no longer has reserve to broadcast. They are not dysregulated — they are depleted. A depleted coherence anchor provides no lift to the field.

This is why individual regulation — the CRNA's own HRV recovery between cases — is not self-care. It is team infrastructure maintenance.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 16 OR staff at a single facility, two consecutive four-week periods.

**Period 1 (control):** Standard OR operations. All staff wear continuous HRV wearables (Polar H10 or equivalent). Collect team HRV synchrony metrics per case using Powezka's four-metric battery. Collect: case length, NOTECHS non-technical skills scores, team self-report (3-item debrief), staff end-of-shift HRV.

**Period 2 (intervention):** CRNA cohort completes two weeks of Gap Moment Training (individual HRV biofeedback, minimum-effective-dose protocol). Introduce Team Breath as optional post-timeout protocol. Continue identical wearable collection.

**Primary hypothesis:** Team HRV synchrony in Period 2 will be higher, and case lengths will be shorter, in cases led by the trained CRNAs versus Period 1 controls. Secondary: trained CRNAs will show better end-of-shift HRV recovery than in Period 1.

This is a feasibility study. It doesn't prove causation. It tests whether the signal is large enough to detect, and whether the intervention is acceptable enough to the team to be delivered. Both questions are unanswered.
