# Notes from the Lab: Five Sheaths, One Clinician

*The pancha kosha model is not a wellness overlay on somnistics training — it is the precise architectural sequence the nervous system follows when moving from reactive to regulated, and every measurable clinical intervention in the literature respects the order of its layers.*

---

## The Observation

Two CRNAs. Same program. Same clinical hours. Same preceptors. One evening, a difficult airway — the kind that escalates faster than any protocol anticipates. One clinician moves with precision: breath controlled, hands steady, situational awareness intact, the correct decision available before the monitors demand it. The other is technically competent but one half-step behind. Reaching for information the body didn't prepare. Making decisions from the edge of the nervous system rather than its center.

The difference is not knowledge. It is not even skill in the conventional sense. It is the degree to which each clinician has trained the system *beneath* their skill — the biological substrate that determines whether clinical judgment is available under pressure.

That substrate has a structure. The ancient Vedic framework called it pancha kosha: five sheaths, nested from gross to subtle, each providing the foundation for the next. The literature now gives us the biomarkers to see it.

---

## The Mechanism

In Vedantic philosophy, a human being is understood to exist across five interpenetrating layers — the pancha koshas. Each sheath (kosha) represents a distinct dimension of experience, from the physical body outward to pure awareness. The model is approximately 3,000 years old. What is new is the neurophysiology that validates its architecture.

**Annamaya Kosha — the physical body.** The first layer is the most literal: muscle, bone, fascia, posture, tissue. This is where SRL training begins not because it is most important but because it is unavoidably first. Research by Vinay, Venkatesh, and Ambarish (2016) in the *International Journal of Yoga* [PMID: 26865773] demonstrated that a one-month yoga practice combining physical postures (asanas), breathing techniques (pranayama), and meditation produced significant improvements in heart rate variability across all domains. The integrated practice began with the body. SDNN increased from 33.60 to 42.11 ms. RMSSD shifted from 22.00 to 25.6 ms. You cannot train breath regulation on a body that carries chronic postural tension and unprocessed muscle guard — Annamaya must be addressed first, or the upper layers have no stable platform.

**Pranamaya Kosha — the breath/energy body.** The second layer is the primary training surface of somnistics. Prana is vital energy; pranayama is its cultivation through breath. The literature on pranayama and autonomic regulation is now substantial. Tyagi and Cohen (2016) [PMID: 27512317] conducted a systematic review of 59 yoga-and-HRV studies (2,358 participants) and found that yoga practices increase HRV and vagal dominance during practice, and that regular practitioners have elevated vagal tone at rest compared to non-practitioners. This is not a transient effect. The nervous system shifts its resting set point.

Kuppusamy et al. (2020) [PMID: 32025489] ran a six-month randomized controlled trial of Bhramari pranayama (humming-bee breath) in 520 healthy adolescents. After six months, HRV time-domain parameters shifted significantly toward parasympathetic predominance; the control group drifted in the opposite direction, toward sympathetic dominance. Six months. Three times the exposure of most clinical wellness interventions — and it produced a measurable, directional shift in the nervous system's default state.

Telles, Singh, Sharma, and Balkrishna (2024) [PMID: 39959514] showed that nostril-regulated yoga breathing (Suryabhedana and Chandrabhedana Pranayama) significantly increased LF power and SD of NN intervals while reducing respiration rate — confirming that the mechanism is respiratory: slower, regulated breathing drives the autonomic shift at the Pranamaya layer.

Catela et al. (2024) [PMID: 39449478] further demonstrated that abdominal and complete yoga breathing techniques — without any retention, simply conscious breath — enhanced parasympathetic activity and raised oxygen saturation in practitioners. The critical phrase in their conclusion: "simple, inexpensive and non-intrusive." The gate into the nervous system is breath. It is always available.

**Manomaya Kosha — the mental and emotional body.** The third layer is where thoughts, emotions, and reactive patterns live. In clinical training, this is the layer most poorly addressed. Medical education offers cognitive tools — decision trees, checklists, Bayesian reasoning — but almost nothing for the emotional-affective dimension that determines whether those cognitive tools are available under acute stress.

Steinberg, Klatt, and Duchemin (2017) [PMID: 27965223] tested an eight-week mindfulness-based intervention in a surgical ICU. The intervention combined meditation, mild yoga, and music for one hour per week. Work satisfaction measured by the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale increased significantly in the intervention group. The most notable finding: participants identified "recognizing their stress response" as the primary benefit. Not relaxation. Recognition. The capacity to notice the Manomaya layer activating — and to not be consumed by it. That is the training.

**Vijnanamaya Kosha — the wisdom/intuitive body.** The fourth layer is clinical judgment itself: embodied intuition, pattern recognition, the pre-monitor knowing that differentiates expert from competent practice. Lopez Blanco and Tyler (2025) [PMID: 40718569] reviewed the role of the vagus nerve in human performance and described a precise layered architecture: vagal tone governs cardio-respiratory activity, then emotional responses, then cognitive control, then stress resilience — in sequence. Their language names what the kosha model maps. "Executive functioning under pressure" — Vijnanamaya — is downstream of breath (Pranamaya) and emotional regulation (Manomaya). The clinician who reaches for clinical judgment before training the two layers beneath it is building on an unstable substrate.

This is the confound that most clinical education ignores. In this context, four other variables also change when a clinician's performance degrades under stress: their respiration rate, their HRV, their emotional reactivity, and their postural tension. Most training protocols address only one — the cognitive layer — while the biological substrate beneath it deteriorates. The kosha model says: address all five, in sequence.

**Anandamaya Kosha — the bliss/purpose body.** The deepest layer is meaning, coherence, and existential resilience. In clinical practice, this is what separates the clinician who sustains thirty years of high-stakes work from one who burns out at twelve. Cohen, Pignata, Bezak, Tie, and Childs (2023) [PMID: 37385740] conducted a systematic review of workplace interventions for burnout in nurses, physicians, and allied healthcare professionals. Thirty-three studies met inclusion criteria. Mindfulness-based practices — adopted in twenty of the thirty-three studies — produced significant improvements in well-being, work engagement, quality of life, and resilience, with reductions in burnout, perceived stress, anxiety, and depression. The Anandamaya layer is not metaphysical. It is measurable as work engagement, burnout resistance, and sustained professional identity.

## The Protocol

The Moongate Training Paradigm moves practitioners through five stages, each corresponding to a kosha layer. The progression is not arbitrary — it reflects the biological constraint that you cannot reliably access upper layers without stabilizing the lower.

1. **Annamaya stage:** Postural awareness, diaphragmatic engagement, release of chronic muscle guard. Duration: Weeks 1-2.
2. **Pranamaya stage:** Pranayama practice (nasal breathing, resonant frequency, Bhramari). Duration: Weeks 3-8. Biomarker: RMSSD baseline shift.
3. **Manomaya stage:** Emotional labeling, interoceptive body scan, gap moment recognition. Duration: Weeks 9-14. Biomarker: MAIA-2 Emotional Awareness subscale.
4. **Vijnanamaya stage:** Clinical pattern integration, embodied debriefs, multi-anchor attention under simulated load. Duration: Weeks 15-21. Biomarker: Decision latency under standardized stress.
5. **Anandamaya stage:** Purpose alignment, team co-regulation, clinical meaning-making. Sustained practice. Biomarker: Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, burnout inventory.

## The Failure Mode

The common failure is entry at the wrong layer. Corporate wellness programs that open with purpose and meaning — Anandamaya — without addressing the breath layer beneath it produce short-term engagement and long-term erosion. The clinician who meditates on purpose while their breath is uncontrolled and their emotional reactivity is unaddressed is building coherence on an unstable floor.

The second failure is layer-skipping: assuming that because a clinician is cognitively capable (Vijnanamaya), their Pranamaya layer is stable. It is not. RMSSD data from clinicians in active practice shows widespread autonomic dysregulation that has no cognitive correlate — the clinician does not know their breath is dysregulated because their training never taught them to notice it.

A kosha intervention that addresses only one layer is not a somnistics intervention. It is a single-modality practice. Useful, but structurally incomplete.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: Twenty CRNAs, a 21-day progressive protocol tracking one biomarker per kosha layer — RMSSD (Pranamaya), MAIA-2 Emotional Awareness subscale (Manomaya), simulated airway decision latency (Vijnanamaya), and work engagement score (Anandamaya). Pre and post, with a validated structured progression through each layer in sequence.

Prediction: Improvements at each layer correlate with baseline stability at the layer beneath it. Clinicians with higher baseline RMSSD show greater Manomaya responsiveness. Clinicians with higher Manomaya scores show greater Vijnanamaya access under load. The cascade is measurable.

This is not a clinical trial. It is a 21-day proof test with a wearable, a structured protocol, and validated instruments. The kosha model predicts the correlation structure of the data.
