# Notes from the Lab: The Problem the Monitor Cannot Solve

*The two leading scientific theories of consciousness were substantially challenged in a landmark 2025 Nature adversarial collaboration (n=256), and both failed key predictions; a CRNA who modulates this unresolved mechanism 1,000+ times per year holds more empirical contact with the hard problem of consciousness than any laboratory neuroscientist, and that clinical experience constitutes a form of evidence science has not yet learned to collect.*

---

## The Observation

There is a moment in every induction.

The propofol enters the vein. A breath slows. Eyes lose their tracking. The patient — who was speaking, who was afraid, who had a name and a life outside this room — is no longer there. You know it before the monitor confirms it. Something in the room changes. The monitors register it after you do.

Twenty-eight years of this. More than twenty thousand transitions, if the case count holds. The experienced CRNA does not think about consciousness. They manage it. They produce it, suspend it, and restore it with the precision of a craftsperson who has internalized a process they could not verbally describe if you asked them.

Here is the problem: neither can neuroscience.

The question of what consciousness is — where it lives in the brain, what physical process generates the experience of being awake and aware — has no scientific consensus. This is not a pop-science overstatement. In 2025, the field's two most rigorous competing theories were put to a direct test, in a collaboration designed specifically to settle the question, and both failed key predictions.

A CRNA reading that sentence should feel something shift. The mechanism you modulate every day is, by current scientific standards, unexplained.

---

## The Mechanism

Consciousness science has been organized, for the past three decades, around two dominant theoretical frameworks.

The first is Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT), developed by Bernard Baars and formalized in its neural architecture by Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux. Mashour, Roelfsema, Changeux, and Dehaene reviewed its central tenets in *Neuron* in 2020 [PMID: 32135090]: conscious experience arises when a stimulus triggers nonlinear "ignition" — a cascade of recurrent processing that amplifies a neural representation and broadcasts it to a distributed network of specialized processors. In the conscious state, information becomes globally accessible. Under anesthesia, that ignition fails. The working hypothesis has substantial empirical support: EEG patterns under propofol show the signature disruption of global workspace dynamics, and the theory maps cleanly onto what anesthesiologists observe in practice.

The second is Integrated Information Theory (IIT), developed by Giulio Tononi. IIT proposes that consciousness corresponds to integrated information — a quantity called phi (Φ) — generated by neural systems with a specific architecture of causal interdependence. Unlike GNWT, IIT locates consciousness in the posterior cortex and predicts sustained synchronization within that region as the hallmark of conscious experience. IIT also makes the philosophically uncomfortable prediction that systems with arbitrarily high integrated information — including, in principle, non-biological systems — could be conscious.

Both theories have independently accrued evidence over decades. Both have influential proponents. Both cannot be simultaneously correct at their key predictions.

In 2025, Melloni and colleagues published an adversarial collaboration in *Nature* [PMID: 40307561] designed to directly juxtapose IIT and GNWT. This was not a typical experiment. The theory proponents themselves (Tononi's team for IIT; Dehaene's team for GNWT) co-developed the experimental design and preregistered their divergent predictions. A theory-neutral consortium — 256 human participants, functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG — then ran the experiment.

The results challenged both theories substantially. For IIT, the consortium found a lack of sustained synchronization within the posterior cortex — directly contradicting the claim that network connectivity in that region specifies consciousness. For GNWT, the experiment found a general lack of the predicted ignition at stimulus offset, and limited representation of certain conscious dimensions in the prefrontal cortex — two predictions the theory requires. Melloni and colleagues noted that the challenges extend to other theories that share these predictions.

Neither framework survived intact.

What survives is a more modest anatomical account. Qureshi and Stevens, writing in the *Handbook of Clinical Neurology* in 2025 [PMID: 39986726], describe consciousness as organized around two linked systems: arousal (generated by mesopontine tegmentum, augmented by lateral hypothalamus and basal forebrain, relayed through thalamic intralaminar nuclei) and awareness (a stream of consciousness running as large-scale distributed cortical networks, holding singular representations moment-by-moment). These systems can dissociate. Arousal without awareness is documented; awareness without behavioral responsiveness is documented. The monitors in use today measure proxies for one or the other — not the relationship between them.

Lee, Sanz, Tononi, Gosseries, and colleagues, publishing in *Nature Communications* in 2022 [PMID: 35217645], used deep learning to build an Explainable Consciousness Indicator (ECI) that simultaneously quantifies arousal and awareness from EEG under sleep, general anesthesia, and severe brain injury. The ECI successfully distinguished states that behavioral and pharmacological markers cannot separate — notably, ketamine-induced anesthesia (low arousal, high awareness) from other anesthetic states. The finding is clinically urgent: ketamine produces measurable awareness even at doses that eliminate responsiveness. The key anatomical finding from the ECI work: parietal regions appear most relevant for quantifying awareness, with or without arousal.

McCulloch and Sanders, writing in the *British Journal of Anaesthesia* in 2023 [PMID: 37198033], stated the clinical failure plainly: commercial depth-of-anesthesia monitors rely on the frontal EEG, which does not arise from a neural correlate of consciousness. Different commercial monitors, analyzing identical frontal EEG data, produce "highly discordant" results. Their recommendation: anesthesiologists could benefit from assessing the raw EEG spectrogram directly rather than trusting an index whose theoretical basis is contested.

The implication is uncomfortable. The monitors measure something. They do not reliably measure the presence or absence of conscious experience.

## The Framework

What does this mean for a CRNA practicing in 2026?

Three things.

First, the confidence that comes from watching a number fall is misplaced — not because the numbers are meaningless, but because the relationship between the number and the thing it purports to measure is theoretically unresolved. A BIS score of 40 is associated with unconsciousness in most patients in most conditions. It is not a definition of unconsciousness. That distinction matters every time the conditions deviate from "most."

Second, the clinical intuition that experienced CRNAs report — the sense that something has shifted before the monitors register it — is not mysticism. Coppola, Owen, Menon, Naci, and Stamatakis, publishing in *Communications Biology* in 2026 [PMID: 41565999], showed that the Default Mode Network (DMN) is more individually variable during consciousness than during unconsciousness: during aware states, neural dynamics become more person-specific, less predictable from population averages. What an experienced clinician detects in the "feel" of a case may be genuine signal — the emergence of individual neural dynamics that population-averaged monitoring cannot resolve.

Third, and most directly relevant to SRL's work: the body has more access to the problem than the monitor does. Anterocept™ training — the progressive cultivation of interoceptive awareness from breath to heartbeat to whole-body state — trains exactly the sensory channels that may be detecting what instrumentation misses. The CRNA who can read their own state transitions has a model for reading the patient's. This is not metaphor. It is a hypothesis about parallel interoceptive architecture: a clinician who cannot detect the state transitions in their own nervous system is working with one channel offline.

The "nonlocal" framing from the Adam Curry lineage — consciousness operating beyond the skull, interacting with systems at a distance — is speculative and contested. The PEAR Lab, IONS, and the Global Consciousness Project constitute a research tradition that the mainstream has not validated and that peer review has not resolved. These claims should not be presented as established science.

What is established: the boundary of consciousness is not where the standard model puts it. The monitor cannot solve the problem it purports to solve. And a clinician who has crossed that boundary 20,000 times holds a form of empirical knowledge that the laboratory has not yet found a way to collect.

---

## The Failure Mode

The failure mode here is certainty — in either direction.

The practitioner who dismisses consciousness science as irrelevant to clinical practice misses the live question embedded in every induction: how do you know they're not aware? The answer is probabilistic, not categorical. Understanding that the underlying theory is unresolved keeps that probability appropriately live in the clinician's mind.

The practitioner who adopts nonlocal consciousness framing wholesale — treating intention as a clinically measurable force, or GCP data as practice-relevant — has moved from frontier science into speculation without acknowledging the transition. The PEAR lab and GCP findings have not survived the adversarial collaboration treatment that IIT and GNWT underwent. They may be real. They are not established.

The productive position is in between: epistemic humility about the limits of current monitoring, heightened respect for embodied clinical signal, and honest acknowledgment that the question is open.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 20 CRNAs, stratified by years of experience (0-5, 6-15, 16+). Measure interoceptive accuracy at baseline using the heartbeat counting task and MAIA-2. Measure a second time after a standardized 4-week Anterocept™ training protocol.

Primary outcome: correlation between interoceptive accuracy score and clinician-reported confidence in assessing patient state transitions (validated self-report instrument), compared pre/post training.

Secondary outcome: correlation between interoceptive accuracy and documented instances of clinical intuition preceding monitor confirmation during transition states — as logged in structured case notes.

Falsification criterion: if higher interoceptive accuracy shows no correlation with transition-state assessment confidence at either timepoint, the proposed embodied-detection mechanism is unsupported and requires a different framing.

The assumption being tested: the clinician who has trained their own interoceptive channels has expanded their detection bandwidth for state transitions in others. This assumption has not been tested. It should be.

## The Connection

This essay links [[nonlocal-consciousness]] to [[anterocept]], [[neurogating]], [[state-transition]], and [[gamma-state-binding]] — the vault's network around consciousness transitions and the clinical instruments for detecting them. The nonlocal framing is kept speculative here, as the evidence requires. What is not speculative: the limits of current monitoring, the failure of both leading theories under adversarial conditions, and the untested value of embodied interoceptive training as a supplementary channel for clinical detection.

Somnistics does not require consciousness to be nonlocal to be worth building. It requires only that the embodied clinician has more bandwidth than the frontal EEG index — a claim the 2023 McCulloch-Sanders editorial and the 2022 Lee et al. ECI study both support.
