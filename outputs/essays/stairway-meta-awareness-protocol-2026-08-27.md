# Notes from the Lab: Five Steps Up — The Stairway Meta-Awareness Protocol as Clinical Architecture

*The Stairway Meta-Awareness Protocol layers five ascending attentional targets — body, emotion, environment, collective field, unified awareness — into a breath-scale sequence that interrupts sympathetic tunneling without adding cognitive load, because each step uses a neural system already active in the clinical moment.*

---

## The Observation

Ninety minutes into a four-hour liver resection, something happens that most CRNAs cannot name until it has already happened. The visual field narrows to the monitor array. The surgeon's commentary becomes signal noise. The scrub tech moves at the edge of peripheral vision without registering. Internal state — tension in the shoulders, the subtle catch in breathing, the faint hum of low-level activation — has gone completely invisible.

This is not inattention. The clinician is intensely focused. That is precisely the problem. Under sustained task load, the attentional system defaults to a progressively smaller aperture. The interoceptive channel closes first. The exteroceptive field narrows second. The team field — what Porges would call the co-regulatory social engagement substrate — goes last.

By the time the surgeon says "something's wrong," the CRNA's awareness architecture has already narrowed to a single thread. Recovery to full situational awareness from this state takes not seconds but minutes — long enough for an undetected event to propagate.

The body knew before the monitors did. But no one was listening to the body.

---

## The Mechanism

Tara Springett described a five-level developmental model of ascending consciousness — each level building on the previous, each engaging a broader relational field. Antonio Damasio established that the feeling of what happens — what he called the somatic marker system — operates as the biological substrate beneath conscious knowledge, giving the self its temporal continuity. George Gurdjieff's self-remembering practice required simultaneous awareness of self and world: the internal witness and the external task held in undivided attention.

The Stairway Meta-Awareness Protocol (SMAP) is a synthesis of all three, mapped onto the [[kosha-architecture]] and executed in under one breath cycle during a [[gap-moment-training]] gap. Each step targets a distinct attentional layer that has a verifiable neural substrate. This is the novel claim: the protocol's steps are not arbitrary — they are ordered by neurobiological dependency.

**Layer 1: Orient to Body (Annamaya/Pranamaya)**

The entry point is interoceptive grounding. The MAIA-2 Attention Regulation subscale — which Rogowska, Tataruch, and Klimowska (2023) validated across athletes and non-athletes — measures precisely this capacity: the ability to regulate attention to bodily sensations and shift between internal and external stimuli [PMID: 38042880]. Athletes score significantly higher in this dimension, suggesting it is trainable through deliberate practice. In CRNA terms: notice the breath. Notice the physical tension. Place awareness at one body anchor before anything else.

This is not relaxation. It is calibration. The body signal is where the state lives.

**Layer 2: Name the Current Emotion (Manomaya)**

Once the body is in the frame, one word follows — not an analysis, just a label. *Tense. Alert. Flat. Rushed.*

The mechanism here is among the most replicable findings in affective neuroscience. Lieberman, Eisenberger, Crockett, Tom, Pfeifer, and Way (2007) demonstrated using fMRI that affect labeling — placing a verbal label on an emotional state — diminishes amygdala reactivity to emotional stimuli and increases activity in right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex [PMID: 17576282]. The RVLPFC and amygdala showed inverse correlation, mediated by medial prefrontal cortex. The pathway runs: RVLPFC → MPFC → amygdala. Language reaches the limbic system through a specific circuit.

What makes this clinically tractable is what Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia, and Crockett (2011) established in a follow-up study: affect labeling is *incidental* emotion regulation [PMID: 21534661]. It requires no deliberate intent to regulate. The downregulation occurs as a byproduct of naming. The CRNA does not need to *try* to feel calmer. They need only to apply one word. Kircanski, Lieberman, and Craske (2012) extended this to a real-world clinical exposure context, where affect labeling reduced physiological arousal (skin conductance response) relative to distraction and reappraisal conditions [PMID: 22902568].

The three variables that changed in Kircanski's study were the labeling condition, the exposure context, and the measurement window. They isolated the labeling effect. This is the confound acknowledged: other regulatory processes were simultaneously active. But labeling's contribution was specific and measurable.

**Layer 3: Sense the OR Soundscape (Manomaya → Vijnanamaya)**

Step 2 named the internal state. Step 3 expands outward without abandoning the body. The instruction: listen. Not to the monitors — those are being processed continuously. Listen to the room. The HVAC hum. The surgeon's breathing. The quality of silence from the perfusionist corner.

This is not vigilance scanning. It is deliberate exteroceptive opening. The attending system widens from body to environment while holding the body signal. In [[anterocept]] terms, this is Domain 2 expanding toward Domain 3 — interoceptive awareness beginning to couple with the perceptual field.

The mechanism here draws on what the MAIA-2 Attention Regulation scale describes as the "ability to shift attention between internal and external stimuli" — a trainable capacity, not a fixed trait.

**Layer 4: Widen to the Collective Field (Vijnanamaya)**

Step 4 is the most operationally significant and the least clinically recognized. It asks: what is the team's state right now?

Hemakom, Powezka, Goverdovsky, Jaffer, and Mandic (2017) demonstrated that respiratory and cardiac synchronization can be quantified in surgical teams — and that this synchronization, like that observed in choir singers, tracks with degrees of team cooperation [PMID: 29308229]. The physiology is coupled. What the primary surgeon carries in their nervous system propagates through the operating field. What the CRNA carries propagates in the other direction.

Step 4 is not empathy. It is co-regulatory sensing: noticing the team's physiological texture without analysis. Is the surgeon moving fast or slow? Is the scrub tech's breathing audible? Is the anesthesia tech calm or tight? These signals are already present in the environment. Step 4 is the act of receiving them.

This is [[co-regulation]] in its most practical form — not as a concept but as a 2-second attentional gesture.

**Layer 5: Hold the Full Stack in Awareness for One Breath Cycle (Anandamaya)**

The final step does not add a new target. It holds all four simultaneously — body, emotion, environment, team — for one complete breath cycle. This is [[self-remembering]] in its most applied form: the observer and the observed in the same frame.

Lao, Kissane, and Meadows (2016) systematically reviewed neuropsychological outcomes from standardized mindfulness training programs and found preliminary evidence for improvements in meta-awareness — defined as the capacity to know what one's attentional system is doing [PMID: 27580462]. The same review found that short-term training did not consistently improve attention or executive function, suggesting that meta-awareness may be a distinct cognitive capacity with its own training curve.

Step 5 is where meta-awareness lives. Not knowing more, but knowing *where attention is* — the observer watching the observer, one breath at a time.

## The Protocol

During any gap moment — a case transition, a waiting period, a lull in surgical activity:

1. **Body** (5 seconds): One hand on the chest or abdomen. Notice the breath. Notice one area of physical tension. Label the sensation (tight, heavy, open, clear).

2. **Emotion** (3 seconds): One word for the current emotional state. Not a story — a single label. *Tense. Alert. Flat. Bored. Present.*

3. **Environment** (5 seconds): Eyes open. Expand attention to the room's acoustic texture. What do you hear that isn't the monitors?

4. **Team** (5 seconds): Scan the team's state without speaking. What is the physical tone of each person in the room?

5. **Hold** (one full breath): Maintain awareness of body, emotion, environment, and team simultaneously. Let the breath complete.

Total elapsed time: approximately 18–20 seconds.

Success is not calm. Success is distributed awareness restored across all four channels simultaneously.

Failure is completing Steps 1–3 while Step 2 drives the wrong direction — when the emotion label activates rather than attenuates. This is the only failure mode addressed below.

---

## The Failure Mode

The protocol breaks under three conditions.

First, if Layer 1 is inaccessible — when sympathetic activation has already crossed into acute crisis — the body signal is too loud to serve as an anchor. The SMAP is a maintenance and pre-escalation tool, not a crisis intervention. Using it inside an acute airway emergency reverses its benefit; attention directed inward during a critical event is attention withdrawn from the critical event.

Second, Layer 2 fails when the affect label activates rather than attenuates. Lieberman, Inagaki et al. (2011) noted that affect labeling *dampens affective responses in general* — but this means it can also reduce appropriate arousal during moments that require high activation. Labeling "calm" when an alarm is firing may briefly interrupt the arousal needed to respond. The timing matters: Layer 2 is for state-check moments, not acute alerts.

Third, Layer 4 fails when the team is in overt interpersonal conflict. The co-regulatory sensing in Step 4 reads the team's physiological state. In a room where the surgical and anesthesia teams are in conflict, what propagates is activation, not coherence. The CRNA who uses Step 4 in a conflicted environment may receive a strong co-regulatory pull toward sympathetic escalation rather than distributed calm.

## The Test

Minimum viable proof test:

- **Participants**: 10 CRNAs at one facility
- **Duration**: 20 shifts per participant
- **Intervention**: One deliberate SMAP practice per shift, timed to a natural gap moment (case turnover or post-induction lull)
- **Measurement**: RMSSD via Polar H10 for 5 minutes before and after practice; self-reported state clarity (0–10 Likert) immediately after
- **Primary question**: Does RMSSD increase from pre to post? Does subjective state clarity improve?
- **Secondary question**: Do CRNAs who practice SMAP show higher MAIA-2 Attention Regulation scores at 20-shift endpoint vs. baseline?

Success criterion: RMSSD increase ≥ 5 ms and subjective clarity increase ≥ 2 points in ≥ 60% of practice instances.

This does not require a randomized controlled trial. It requires 200 measured gap moments and honest data recording.

## The Connection

The SMAP is the operational layer beneath [[kosha-architecture]]: where the five-sheath model stops being a framework and becomes a 20-second practice. It is the clinical instantiation of [[self-remembering]] — Gurdjieff's divided attention applied with a breath clock. It deploys into [[gap-moment-training]] as its most structured variant, requiring no special equipment and no step away from the patient.

The five layers correspond to the five sheaths not by coincidence but by design: the contemplative tradition and the neuroscientific evidence converged on the same sequence independently. Body first. Emotion named. Environment expanded. Team felt. Unified held.

What the SMAP adds that neither Springett, Damasio, nor Gurdjieff articulated: the pathway is bidirectional. A CRNA who practices the ascending sequence repeatedly builds the descending reflex — the capacity for distributed awareness to fire as a unit under load, without the protocol prompt.

*In the OR, the body is always in the room. The question is only whether the clinician is.*
