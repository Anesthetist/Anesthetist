# Notes from the Lab: Vagal Tone Is Not Relaxation — What RMSSD Actually Measures

*Vagal tone is not a measure of calm — it is a measure of autonomic flexibility, the nervous system's capacity to shift between states on demand, and this flexibility predicts cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and clinical durability more reliably than any stress biomarker.*

## The Observation

The CRNA with the highest resting HRV on the team is not the most relaxed person in the room. She is the most responsive. When the patient desaturates, her heart rate spikes faster than anyone else's. When the crisis resolves, her heart rate drops back to baseline faster than anyone else's. She can be at 120 during the emergency and back to 68 two minutes later. The CRNA with the lowest resting HRV takes twenty minutes to come down. Same stimulus, same training, same credentials — different nervous systems.

The difference is not calm versus anxious. It is flexible versus rigid. The high-HRV clinician's nervous system has a wider operating range and faster return-to-baseline. The low-HRV clinician's system is locked in a narrower band — functional, but less adaptive. Over a 12-hour shift, this difference compounds. By case eight, the flexible system is still shifting fluidly. The rigid system is running on diminishing reserves it cannot detect — [[subconscious-performance-degradation]] accumulating in the gaps between cases.

The wellness industry has reduced vagal tone to "relaxation." The literature says something more precise and more useful: vagal tone is the bandwidth of your nervous system.

## The Mechanism

Julian Thayer and Richard Lane formalized this insight in their neurovisceral integration model. In a landmark 2009 paper, Thayer, Hansen, Saus-Rose, and Johnsen demonstrated that a set of neural structures involved in cognitive, affective, and autonomic regulation are functionally linked — and that heart rate variability indexes this network's output [PMID: 19424767]. The finding is specific: individual differences in resting HRV predict performance on executive function tasks, particularly those requiring inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility. High HRV = better prefrontal-subcortical circuit function. Not "more relaxed" — more capable of top-down regulation when it matters.

The neural architecture makes this clear. The central autonomic network — anterior cingulate cortex, insula, ventromedial prefrontal cortex — sends inhibitory signals to subcortical threat circuits via the vagus nerve. RMSSD (root mean square of successive differences between heartbeats) captures the moment-to-moment output of this inhibitory network. When RMSSD is high, the prefrontal cortex is actively modulating the heart's response to changing conditions. When RMSSD is low, that modulation has weakened — the subcortical circuits are running with less oversight.

Arakaki et al. (2023) synthesized this evidence in a literature review connecting HRV to neurological health and cognition, confirming that HRV is associated with executive functions, decision-making, and emotional regulation across multiple clinical populations [DOI: 10.3389/fnins.2023.1055445]. The relationship is not correlational noise — it reflects the shared neural substrate that governs both cardiac regulation and cognitive control. The vagus is the wire. HRV is the signal quality on that wire.

Laborde et al. (2022) established the intervention side in a 223-study meta-analysis: voluntary slow breathing increases vagally-mediated HRV during sessions, immediately after single sessions, and following multi-session interventions [PMID: 35623448]. The mechanism is baroreflex stimulation — slow diaphragmatic breathing stretches thoracic baroreceptors, which trigger reflex bradycardia and parasympathetic activation via the vagus. At the individual's [[resonant-breathing-frequency]] (typically 4.5-6.5 breaths per minute), this oscillation locks into resonance, producing maximal HRV amplitude.

**The confound that must be stated:** RMSSD is influenced by respiration. During slow breathing, RMSSD increases partly because of the mechanical effect of respiratory sinus arrhythmia — not purely because of increased vagal "tone." Grossman (2023) argued this point forcefully, contending that RSA is "neither an invariably reliable index of cardiac vagal tone nor of central vagal outflow" and that polyvagal theory's five basic premises face "fundamental challenges" [PMID: 37230290]. Grossman (2024) extended this critique, proposing that RSA functions as an evolutionary adaptation for metabolic efficiency rather than a parasympathetic indicator [PMID: 38151156].

This critique is scientifically important and SRL should not ignore it. The respiratory confound is real — a person breathing at 6 breaths per minute will show higher RMSSD than the same person breathing at 15, even if their underlying vagal tone hasn't changed. SRL's response must be methodologically honest: RMSSD during paced breathing reflects the combined effect of vagal activation AND respiratory mechanical coupling. The change in resting RMSSD (measured during normal, uncontrolled breathing, ideally upon morning waking) is the cleaner index of training-induced vagal tone improvement. The distinction matters for clinical credibility.

**What survives Grossman's critique:** Even if RSA is an imperfect vagal tone proxy, the Thayer-Lane neurovisceral integration findings stand on firmer ground — they use resting HRV (not breathing-manipulated HRV) to predict cognitive performance. The association between resting RMSSD and executive function is replicated across dozens of studies and is not explained by respiratory confounds. The vagus may be more complex than Porges' three-tier hierarchy suggests, but the empirical link between resting HRV and cognitive flexibility remains robust.

## The Framework

Vagal tone in the SRL system operates across three measurement contexts, each answering a different question:

**1. Resting vagal tone (morning RMSSD)**
- Measured: 5-minute supine recording upon waking, natural breathing
- What it answers: "What is the baseline capacity of this nervous system today?"
- Clinical use: Daily trend tracking. Declining morning RMSSD across a work week signals [[clinician-durability]] erosion before subjective fatigue arrives. The Apple Watch captures this automatically.

**2. Reactive vagal tone (stress response + recovery)**
- Measured: HRV during and after a standardized stressor (the squat-sit test, the Massimo protocol)
- What it answers: "How fast can this nervous system shift from activation to recovery?"
- Clinical use: The heart rate recovery (HRR) metric. Cole et al. (1999) demonstrated that failure to decrease HR by >12 bpm at 1 minute post-exercise predicts all-cause mortality. HRR is vagal tone in action — the speed of the parasympathetic rebound.

**3. Trained vagal tone (within-session RMSSD delta)**
- Measured: RMSSD before and after a 60-second [[neurominute]] at personal [[resonant-breathing-frequency]]
- What it answers: "Can this person produce a measurable autonomic shift in 60 seconds?"
- Clinical use: SRB-60 Domain 4 (Measured State Delta). The trainability question — not just "do you have vagal tone?" but "can you deploy it on demand?"

The three contexts form a hierarchy. Resting tone is the foundation. Reactive tone is the test. Trained tone is the skill. SRL trains all three — morning tracking establishes the baseline, the stress protocol reveals the capacity, and the NeuroMinute builds the on-demand deployment skill.

## The Failure Mode

Vagal tone as a concept breaks under three conditions:

**1. Reifying the number.** RMSSD is a proxy, not the thing itself. A clinician who chases the number — "my RMSSD was only 38 this morning" — has turned a biomarker into a source of anxiety. The number serves the clinician; the clinician does not serve the number. If tracking morning HRV increases performance anxiety, the tracking is iatrogenic. SRL protocols must include guidance on when to stop looking at the data.

**2. The low-HRV starting point.** Some individuals have constitutionally low resting HRV due to age, fitness level, medication (beta-blockers), or cardiac history. A CRNA on metoprolol for hypertension will have suppressed RMSSD regardless of their autonomic flexibility. The relevant metric for these individuals is not absolute RMSSD but delta — the change from their personal baseline. The system must adapt its thresholds to the individual, not impose normative ranges that pathologize medication-appropriate physiology.

**3. Vagal tone without vagal skill.** High resting HRV does not guarantee the ability to regulate under pressure. The naturally high-HRV individual who has never trained gap moments may have excellent passive tone but no active deployment capacity. Conversely, a trained practitioner with modest resting HRV may produce larger within-session deltas because they've practiced the skill. Tone is the reservoir. Skill is the pipeline. Both matter. Measuring only resting HRV misses half the picture.

## The Test

**14-day proof test with 10 CRNAs:**

- **Days 1-7 (baseline):** Morning RMSSD upon waking (Apple Watch, 1-minute recording, natural breathing). End-of-shift subjective fatigue VAS. No interventions.
- **Days 8-14 (intervention):** Same morning RMSSD tracking. Add five 60-second resonant frequency breathing sessions distributed across each shift ([[gap-moment-training]]). Capture within-session RMSSD delta for each gap moment.

**Primary outcome:** Mean morning RMSSD trend during intervention week vs. baseline week (within-subject).

**Secondary outcomes:** End-of-shift fatigue VAS, average within-session RMSSD delta, correlation between morning RMSSD and same-day shift fatigue.

**Positive result:** Morning RMSSD trend ≥ 10% higher during intervention week, AND within-session deltas ≥ 5ms RMSSD improvement per gap moment. The dual requirement tests both the training effect (resting tone improves) and the skill effect (on-demand deployment works).

**What would falsify the model:** If within-session deltas are consistently positive (the breathing works in the moment) but morning RMSSD does not improve (the training doesn't compound), then gap moment interventions are acute tools, not autonomic training. That would require repositioning GMT from "build vagal tone over time" to "use as needed for immediate regulation" — a meaningful but less ambitious claim.

## The Connection

Vagal tone is the primary outcome measure for everything SRL builds. [[Gap-moment-training]] is how it's trained. [[Resonant-breathing-frequency]] is the mechanism that stimulates it. [[Cognitive-variability-analysis]] uses it as input for state classification. [[Neurogating]] monitors its fluctuations in real time. [[Clinician-durability]] is what it predicts over decades. The entire system — every concept, every protocol, every measurement — ultimately resolves to a question about vagal tone: is the nervous system flexible enough to meet the demands placed on it?

But the word must be understood correctly. Not calm. Not relaxation. Flexibility. The capacity to shift. The bandwidth of the nervous system. That is what RMSSD measures — when you measure it right.

*Place two fingers on your radial pulse. Feel the rhythm. Now take one slow breath — five seconds in, seven seconds out. Feel the rhythm change. That change — the gap between what your heart was doing and what it's doing now — is vagal tone made tangible. Not a number on a screen. A rhythm under your fingertips.*
