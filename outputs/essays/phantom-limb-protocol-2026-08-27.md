# Notes from the Lab: The Phantom Limb Protocol — How the Nervous System Carries the People Who Steady It

*A clinician can deliberately pre-load the nervous system with the co-regulatory signature of a stabilizing relationship — so the regulatory effect operates as an internal resource during high-stakes stress even when the person is absent — and the interpersonal physiological synchrony literature suggests this is not metaphor but trainable physiology.*

---

## The Observation

Two CRNAs, twelve years working together. When they're both on the floor, something is different. Not louder, not faster — steadier. Families notice. The circulator notices. Even the attending, who has never once said anything about it, starts turning toward the room with less urgency. Nobody can name it, but the regulation is real.

Now she's on leave. He goes into the next case with the same hands, the same chart, the same drugs. The hands know their work. But something is differently tuned. He catches himself at 0247 in a long thoracic case, heart rate ticking up at minute thirty-seven, noticing the absence the way you notice a chair that's been moved three inches.

The nervous system remembers her.

The question is whether it can do more than remember — whether it can carry her, deliberately, as a physiological resource. Whether the brain's tendency to maintain detailed neural maps of important relationships can be recruited as a regulation tool, not just experienced as a loss when those relationships are unavailable.

That is the Phantom Limb Protocol: not a metaphor, not mindfulness. A somatic installation practice for pre-loading the regulatory signature of a stabilizing relationship before entering high-stakes stress.

---

## The Mechanism

The name comes from a phenomenon that has fascinated neuroscientists for decades: after amputation, the brain continues to maintain a vivid, detailed cortical map of the missing limb. The representation does not fade gracefully. It persists, reorganizes, and in many patients, continues to generate sensation — phantom pain, phantom movement, phantom pressure. The brain models what is absent because the neural architecture was built around its presence.

The argument of the Phantom Limb Protocol is that the same organizing principle operates in the autonomic nervous system's representation of key relationships. The body builds detailed regulatory templates — what Porges calls "neuroceptive maps" of social safety — and these templates actively shape autonomic tone whether or not the person is present. The brain maintains a phantom of the people who regulate it.

The interpersonal physiological synchrony (IPS) literature, now two decades deep, has documented the mechanism in progressively finer detail. Koul and Novembre (2026) demonstrated that mere visual contact between familiar dyads is sufficient to synchronize all four measured autonomic signals simultaneously: heart rate, skin conductance, respiration rate, and pupil diameter. No shared task required. No verbal exchange. The investigators measured heart rate and pupil diameter as particularly vision-dependent — interrupting visual contact produced significant reductions in synchrony for these signals [PMID: 42301140]. The nervous system is continuously reading the regulatory state of familiar others and adjusting its own state in response. This is not attention. It is automatic.

He and colleagues (2026) extended this finding into naturalistic, multi-day settings using continuous GPS and cardiac monitoring across 72 participants. Synchrony was reliably higher among socially familiar peers and was modulated by physical proximity, joint attention, and acoustic environment — emerging during close-proximity interactions and shared focus, collapsing in excessive noise. Familiarity amplified the effect, suggesting that the regulatory template becomes more robust, more precise, and more rapidly activated with accumulated shared experience [PMID: 42345043].

This is the installation mechanism. Repeated co-regulatory interactions build a progressively refined autonomic template of the other person's nervous system. The clinician pair who have worked together for twelve years have built each other's regulatory signatures into their own autonomic architecture. What looks like "steadiness" in the room is partly the activation of those templates.

The critical question for the Phantom Limb Protocol is whether this template can be activated without the person being present. Goodyke and colleagues (2021), in an integrative review of eight studies on perceived social support and HRV, found that perceived social support — not actual contact, not confirmed presence — was positively associated with vagal HRV during rest, stress induction, and recovery from an acute stressor [PMID: 34238084]. The mechanism is representation, not presence. What the nervous system responds to is the internalized regulatory model of the relationship, not the person.

Gallistl and colleagues (2025) sharpened this finding in a subtle direction. In their study of 79 couples, the observer's attachment state measurably shaped the target's heart rate reactivity during stress. When the observer showed insecure-avoidant attachment, the target's heart rate reactivity was elevated. The regulatory influence traveled from observer to subject without any intervention from the subject — shaped entirely by the observer's invisible autonomic state and its interaction with the target's learned model of that relationship [PMID: 40517647]. The nervous system carries the relational template even when reading it only through learned expectation.

Three more findings round out the mechanism:

Boukarras and colleagues (2025) found that physiological synchrony between dyads intensifies precisely when they navigate novel task challenges together — "switch blocks" where the task structure changed produced higher IPS than stable, familiar task repetition [PMID: 40097345]. High-stakes OR environments — the novel case, the unexpected complication, the case that takes a turn at minute thirty-seven — are not the worst conditions for co-regulation. They are the conditions under which co-regulation has been most repeatedly reinforced. The novel-stress template is often the strongest one.

Bello and colleagues (2026) demonstrated in high-stakes manufacturing teams that HRV synchrony was a distinguishing feature between high- and low-performing teams, and that triadic teams showed stronger IPS and more robust shared mental models than dyads [PMID: 41891848]. Performance and synchrony co-vary. The regulatory field and the cognitive field move together.

Mostafa and colleagues (2024) found that IPS — measured as electrodermal synchrony — predicted the social transmission of nocebo hyperalgesia across three generations of dyads, including a third generation who never witnessed the original pain event [PMID: 39242740]. The regulatory template transferred through the social chain, mediated by synchrony between intermediate dyads. What physiological state a relationship installs can propagate beyond immediate contact.

Taken together, these findings establish: the nervous system automatically builds regulatory templates of significant others; these templates activate in proximity but persist and influence autonomic tone through representation alone; perceived presence of the relationship — not confirmed physical presence — is sufficient to produce measurable HRV effects; and the templates built under novel, high-stakes stress are among the most robust.

The Phantom Limb Protocol is the deliberate recruitment of this architecture.

## The Protocol

**Step 1 — Identify the regulatory anchor.** Select a relationship with documented co-regulatory effect. Not someone you care about abstractly — someone whose physical presence you have experienced as steadying. The test: when you think of them standing next to you in a difficult case, does something in the chest or belly settle? That settling is the template activating. If it doesn't settle, the template isn't there, or the relationship has mixed valence that complicates the signal.

**Step 2 — Installation.** Three minutes, eyes closed, before entering the high-stakes case. The instruction is somatic, not narrative: do not think *about* the person. Find where in the body their co-regulatory presence lives. For most people it is in the chest or the abdomen — a quality of expansion, steadiness, or warmth. Stay with the felt sense, not the story. Name the location. Breathe into it. The nervous system is activating its representation of this regulatory template.

**Step 3 — Calibration.** Track resting HRV before and after the installation practice over ten sessions. If the template is activating cleanly, you should see a measurable shift — an increase in vagal tone during the installation period. If no shift is detectable, the anchor is either insufficiently loaded (needs more shared high-stakes experience with that person) or the anchor selection criteria weren't met (mixed valence is blocking the signal).

**Step 4 — Portability practice.** Intentionally activate the installation practice in progressively challenging conditions — starting with familiar low-stakes situations, then progressively higher-stakes contexts. The goal is to strengthen the neural pathway between the sensory cue (the felt sense in the body) and the regulatory state, until the anchor becomes accessible even under maximum cognitive load.

What this is not: visualization of scenarios, affirmations, or thinking about what the person would say. The target is not the narrative layer. It is the autonomic layer — the same layer that Koul and Novembre demonstrated activates automatically from visual contact alone. The protocol is training a body-based access route to a template that is already there.

---

## The Failure Mode

There are three ways this breaks.

**Toxic dependency.** If the anchor relationship is the only regulatory resource, and the installation practice has been used to compensate for underdeveloped personal regulation skills, the clinician becomes more dysregulated when the relationship is damaged, unavailable, or ends. The protocol strengthens one access route; it cannot substitute for developing a full regulation portfolio.

**Wrong anchor selection.** A relationship with significant mixed valence — care and fear together, admiration and performance anxiety together — can produce physiological noise rather than a clean signal. The body can't easily separate the stabilizing component from the activating component. Insecure-avoidant attachment dynamics (documented by Gallistl et al.) can invert the effect. If the person represents evaluation as much as safety, the template activates threat pathways as readily as safety pathways.

**Novel-stress override.** In extreme threat states — the patient losing pressure fast, the unexpected airway crisis — the dorsal vagal system may override the regulatory template entirely. The phantom limb protocol is a ventral vagal installation practice; it does not function in freeze states. It is designed for the case under load, not the case in freefall.

## The Test

Fourteen CRNAs, two teaching hospitals, four weeks. Pair them according to existing working relationships (at least three years, high familiarity). Random assignment: protocol group installs the practice in the twenty minutes before each shift for weeks one and two; control group follows their usual pre-shift routine. Primary measure: continuous HRV logging during first case of each shift (wrist-worn device; RMSSD as primary metric). Secondary measures: SART attentional lapses, self-reported readiness, same-shift colleague-rated regulation quality. Weeks three and four: washout period, followed by reversal. Primary hypothesis: RMSSD during first case is higher in protocol condition, with effect size increasing in cases with higher complexity ratings.

The confounds to control: relationship quality (not all pairs are equally loaded), shift start time (circadian effects on HRV), and case complexity (complex cases may elevate or suppress HRV depending on arousal level and skill match).

## The Connection

Every SRL training layer assumes a regulated clinician. The NeuroMinute works better when the clinician is already close to ventral vagal. Gap Moment Training has more impact when the regulatory baseline is higher. The Phantom Limb Protocol is an upstream intervention: it installs regulatory infrastructure before the shift begins, at the level of the nervous system's relational architecture, not at the level of technique.

It also points toward the enterprise value proposition. When institutions train co-regulatory pairs — deliberately building high-familiarity, high-IPS relationships across their clinical workforce — they are installing a system-level regulatory infrastructure that functions through the personnel, not despite them. One calm clinician is a team resource. [[co-regulation]] A team with high IPS is a safety system.

---

*Somewhere between the last handoff and the first incision, a nervous system is running its calculations — who is here, who is known, what their presence predicts about the safety of this moment. The Phantom Limb Protocol is the recognition that those calculations can be deliberately stocked before the door opens.*
