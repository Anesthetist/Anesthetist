# Notes from the Lab: Neither Wanton Nor Hamlet — The Reflective Gap as Clinical Agency

*The two failure modes of clinical agency — the Wanton who acts without reflection and is captured by cognitive bias, and Hamlet who reflects without acting and freezes in paralysis — define the performance collapse poles in anesthesia, and the reflective gap is the measurable, trainable interval that holds the practitioner between them.*

---

## The Observation

It is 0330. The OR has that particular stillness — monitors humming, surgeon's music low, the patient's chest rising on the ventilator. The CRNA has been doing anesthesia for eleven years. She knows this case the way she knows her own kitchen in the dark.

That knowing is the hazard.

She notices the end-tidal CO₂ is trending upward. Her brain, running on eleven years of pattern library and four hours of unbroken vigilance, generates an answer before she has consciously asked a question: *circuit issue, recheck connections.* She acts. The connections are fine. The CO₂ keeps climbing. She rechecks. Still fine. The answer her nervous system provided is wrong — but her hands keep checking the circuit because that's what the answer says to do.

Somewhere across town, another CRNA faces a similar deviation. He is in his third year of practice, running his first complex cardiac case. He notices the same trend and stops. He begins running through differentials mentally. Pulmonary embolism? Malignant hyperthermia? Ventilator failure? Equipment error? Each possibility spawns three more. He is still reviewing the list when the surgeon asks why the ventilator isn't being adjusted.

Two clinicians. Two failures. Neither one is incompetent. Both are caught in a failure of agency that the philosophical and clinical literature has been circling for decades without quite naming it together.

---

## The Mechanism

Harry Frankfurt, in his 1971 paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (*Journal of Philosophy*), introduced a distinction that clinical training has never used and desperately needs.

The **Wanton** is not a bad person. The Wanton is a person — or a system — that acts directly from first-order desires without any higher-order evaluation of those desires. The Wanton does what salience demands. Whatever pulls hardest in the environment gets executed. There is no pause between trigger and response.

**Hamlet** is the inverse failure. Hamlet has rich higher-order desires — he cares deeply about acting rightly, about knowing his own motivations, about not making a mistake. That caring is so active it prevents him from closing the loop into action. He reflects but does not act. He is paralyzed by his own meta-cognition.

Both are failures of agency. Real agency — what Frankfurt calls freedom of will — is the capacity to have second-order desires about your first-order desires, *and* to act from that reflective capacity in a timely, contextually calibrated way.

Clinical medicine, particularly anesthesia, instantiates this polarity in high-stakes real time.

The Croskerry model of clinical decision-making provides the neurological machinery underneath Frankfurt's philosophical frame. In his foundational 2009 review, Croskerry described two systems governing clinical judgment: **System 1**, which is fast, heuristic, intuitive, pattern-based — and **System 2**, which is slow, analytical, deliberate, effortful. Croskerry argued that diagnostic error rates remain unacceptably high because practitioners rely too heavily on System 1 while underestimating its susceptibility to bias, and that developing metacognitive strategies to toggle between systems is the central educational task. The pure System 1 operator is the Wanton. The System 2-locked operator is Hamlet. [Croskerry P (2009). Academic Medicine. PMID: 19638766]

Stiegler, Neelankavil, Canales, and Dhillon made this concrete in anesthesia in their 2012 pilot study, which remains one of the most important pieces of cognitive science in anesthesiology practice. They used a modified Delphi method to identify the 10 cognitive errors most prevalent in anesthesia practice — anchoring, availability bias, premature closure, feedback bias, framing effect, confirmation bias, omission bias, commission bias, overconfidence, and sunk costs — and then observed residents managing simulated emergencies. Seven of nine error types occurred in more than 50% of observed scenarios. These are Wanton-mode errors: the practitioner's System 1 generated a confident answer and the practitioner acted on it, bypassing the reflective check that would have caught the error. Stiegler's group concluded that metacognitive training and de-biasing strategies are the corrective — which is another way of saying the reflective gap needs to be trained. [Stiegler MP et al. (2012). British Journal of Anaesthesia. PMID: 22157846]

The Hamlet failure has its own clinical literature, though it goes by different names. Frankenthal and colleagues described "frozen thinking" — a phenomenon in which clinicians distrust a medical device's output and instead fixate on a prior clinical script despite data contradicting it. What looks like excessive skepticism is actually a paralysis: the clinician cannot integrate contradicting signals into a revised action because they are stuck in recursive evaluation of the reliability of the evidence itself. The monitor says one thing; their prior experience says another; they are in the loop, not through it. [Frankenthal H et al. (2022). International Journal for Quality in Health Care. PMID: 35166351]

Ramaswamy and colleagues, in their 2024 review of decision-making misconceptions in critical care, documented the full complexity: patient severity, illness complexity, time pressure, and clinician-side factors — fatigue, cognitive overload, inexperience — all compound the error rate. Critically, they named both failure poles: errors of premature action (Wanton) and errors of incomplete action (Hamlet). The corrective they propose is metacognitive training: teaching clinicians to recognize which mode they're in and shift accordingly. [Ramaswamy T et al. (2024). World Journal of Critical Care Medicine. PMID: 38855268]

Cabrera and colleagues tested the accuracy of System 1 judgment empirically at Mayo Clinic Emergency. Emergency physicians were given minimal patient data and asked to predict acuity, disposition, and diagnosis. System 1 reached ~80% accuracy for acuity and disposition — but performed significantly worse for ICU admission prediction and diagnosis accuracy. Their conclusion was precise: System 1 provides a necessary cognitive scaffold, but is insufficient for final clinical decisions in high-stakes domains. In other words, you cannot be the Wanton and survive complex cases. You need the reflective layer. [Cabrera D et al. (2015). Western Journal of Emergency Medicine. PMID: 26587086]

The mechanism, integrated: the practitioner who has no reflective gap — who acts directly from System 1's first answer — becomes error-prone in proportion to the complexity and novelty of the clinical situation. The practitioner who has too large a reflective gap — who cannot commit System 2's analysis to an action — introduces delays that are themselves a form of care failure. Between these poles is a narrow operating zone where agency functions: the reflective gap that is sized to the clinical moment.

This is not a new clinical observation. What is new is the training claim: that gap size is not fixed. It is a trainable parameter of clinical performance.

## The Protocol

Gap Moment Training™ operationalizes the reflective gap as a physiologically anchored micro-intervention. The target is not "pause more" or "think less" — the target is **calibrated gap insertion** that matches the complexity of the clinical moment.

**The Gap Sizing Framework:**

| Clinical Complexity | Appropriate Gap | Function |
|--------------------|-----------------|----------|
| Routine (stable patient, familiar case) | 0–3 seconds | Confirm System 1 read, proceed |
| Moderate (trend deviations, unclear etiology) | 5–15 seconds | One differentials check, name your assumption |
| High (rapid decompensation, novel presentation) | 15–30 seconds | Full System 2 pass; verbalize reasoning to team |
| Crisis (code-equivalent) | Protocol-driven | Crisis framework overrides individual gap sizing |

The measurable marker of gap sizing failure: **when the hands are moving before the question has been asked**. In the Wanton mode, the body responds before the mind has evaluated the response. In the Hamlet mode, the mind is still evaluating after the body should have moved.

Gap Moment Training builds the proprioceptive sensitivity to notice both: the kinesthetic sense of premature action (hands already adjusting before explicit reasoning), and the parasympathetic brake of over-thinking (the feeling of recursive loop, of generating options without closing toward any of them).

---

## The Failure Mode

This framework breaks at the extremes and under load.

**When it breaks toward Wanton:** High-speed clinical environments (rapid inductions, trauma, cardiac decompensation) apply genuine time pressure that can make a gap feel like a luxury. In these contexts, practitioners trained to pause may pause when they should not — introducing a reflective delay into a situation that requires immediate procedural execution. The corrective: crisis frameworks (pre-learned protocols) should override individual gap sizing in code-equivalent situations. The gap training is for the 95% of cases; the crisis protocol is for the 5%.

**When it breaks toward Hamlet:** Clinicians in burnout or compassion fatigue often develop pathological Hamlet patterns — exhausted meta-cognition that can no longer resolve into action. This isn't thoughtfulness; it is a failure of autonomic regulation masquerading as careful reasoning. Decision fatigue, moral injury, and chronic underrecovery all shift the practitioner toward the Hamlet pole. The gap gets too large and becomes a liability.

**The misapplication:** Training people to "take a breath before acting" without physiological anchoring and without size-calibration produces neither outcome reliably. It adds a behavioral beat without changing the underlying cognitive-autonomic state. The reflective gap only works when the nervous system is in a state capable of using it — which is why autonomic regulation training must precede and accompany gap sizing practice.

## The Test

**Who:** 10–15 CRNAs, 14-day protocol.

**How:** Participants wear HRV-capable wearable during clinical shifts. They log — via voice note at post-case review — three items: (1) a situation where they acted before explicitly checking their reasoning ("hands moved first"); (2) a situation where they stayed in deliberation longer than the clinical situation warranted ("loop, not through"); and (3) a situation where they felt the gap was sized correctly. After 14 days, correlate logged patterns with HRV data (RMSSD time-series) to identify whether autonomic state predicts gap-sizing failure direction.

**Success criterion:** Participants can reliably identify their gap-sizing failure mode (Wanton vs. Hamlet) within the session, and HRV data shows a measurable difference in autonomic profile between the two failure modes.

**Failure criterion:** No observable autonomic signature distinguishes the two failure modes — which would mean gap-sizing is a purely cognitive variable, not an autonomic one. If this is what the data shows, it changes the training model significantly.

**Honest caveat:** This has not been tested. The Wanton-Hamlet polarity applied to anesthesia practice is a framework derived from philosophy and cognitive science, applied to a clinical domain that has not yet measured it directly. The 14-day test above is the minimum viable experiment to determine whether it generates actionable data.

## The Connection

The Wanton-Hamlet polarity deepens every other SRL concept that depends on the practitioner being able to observe their own state. [[attentional-sovereignty]] requires the meta-capacity to place attention; you cannot place it if you are being placed by it (Wanton) or paralyzed in the attempt (Hamlet). [[state-transition]] happens inside the gap — the transition is the gap. [[autonomic-diary]] is the record of which mode you were in and when, making the invisible pattern visible over time. [[gap-moment-training]] is the operational translation of this entire framework into a clinical micro-practice.

What the Wanton-Hamlet frame adds: it names the failure modes, which is the precondition for training away from them. You cannot train a practitioner out of a failure they cannot name.

*In the 0330 OR, the CO₂ is still climbing. The eleven-year CRNA finally stops checking the circuit and asks the question she skipped: what if the assumption is wrong? The answer arrives in six seconds. She adjusts. The monitor begins its descent. The gap — that six-second pause she had been trained not to skip — is the whole story.*
