# Notes from the Lab: The Sensor Is the Clinician

*When the anesthesia provider is understood as a closed-loop physiological controller whose primary instrument is their own nervous system, interoceptive training exits the wellness column and enters patient safety — the same category as equipment calibration.*

---

## The Observation

The operating room is instrumented in one direction.

The patient is wired: arterial line, pulse oximetry, capnography, BIS monitor, temperature probe. Every variable that matters — SpO₂, MAP, ETCO₂, heart rate, temperature — is displayed, alarmed, trended. The OR is one of the most densely monitored environments in medicine.

The CRNA is not monitored at all.

This asymmetry would be unremarkable if the CRNA were simply a technician operating equipment. But that is not what a CRNA does. A CRNA is the integrating controller — the entity whose nervous system receives all those patient signals, processes them against a 28-year pattern library of pharmacokinetics, surgical field cues, ambient sounds, team dynamics, and their own gut sense, and outputs the micro-decisions that keep a human being alive under general anesthesia. The CRNA's anterior insula is running closed-loop control on someone else's body. Sub-minute. Continuous. Without interruption.

If the sensor running that control loop is miscalibrated — dysregulated, noisy, saturated from the previous case, carrying unprocessed moral injury from last week — the downstream decisions inherit that noise. Not metaphorically. Mechanistically.

The OR monitors everything except the thing doing the monitoring.

---

## The Mechanism

Craig established the anatomical fact in 2009. The anterior insular cortex (AIC), he demonstrated, functions as the neural substrate for interoceptive re-representation — the convergence point where signals from the body's internal state are integrated into subjective feelings that inform decision-making, awareness, and what he called "the sentient self." The AIC is implicated in a remarkable range of conditions and behaviors: bowel distension and orgasm, cigarette craving and maternal love, decision-making and sudden insight. What unifies them, Craig argued, is that the AIC provides the common neural substrate for all subjective feelings — the layer at which raw bodily signals become the felt sense that drives action. Craig et al. (2009) [PMID: 19096369]

The implication for anesthesia providers is specific and consequential. A CRNA's clinical judgment does not arise from a disembodied algorithm. It arises from a nervous system that is simultaneously monitoring the patient and experiencing its own internal state — hunger, anxiety, fatigue, residual activation from a prior critical event, the background hum of chronic overload. The AIC cannot segregate those signals cleanly. When the provider's own interoceptive state is dysregulated, clinical signal processing degrades. Not because the CRNA is incompetent, but because the sensor is noisy.

This is where the closed-loop-controller frame crystallizes into a patient-safety claim.

In control systems, sensor calibration is not a maintenance luxury — it is a prerequisite for loop stability. A temperature controller with a drifting thermistor doesn't give bad temperature readings because the controller is broken. It gives bad readings because the measurement is corrupted upstream of everything else. Every downstream output — valve position, heating element state, safety cutoffs — inherits the measurement error. The same logic applies to the human controller in the OR. If the CRNA's interoceptive apparatus is the primary sensor through which patient signals are processed, then the calibration state of that apparatus is a patient-safety variable by definition.

A finding from Zafiriou and colleagues published in 2026 is instructive here. In a simulation study of 34 anesthesia professionals — including anesthesiologists, anesthesiology residents, nurse anesthetists, and nurse anesthetist students — resilience scores (Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale) showed no significant association with physiological stress responses (HRV) during a high-fidelity pediatric laryngospasm simulation. Zafiriou et al. (2026) [PMID: 42228700] The trait measure did not predict the physiological response. The authors conclude that "direct measurement of resilience may therefore be more appropriate than relying on observable stress responses to identify professionals at psychosocial risk." In other words: you cannot infer calibration state from a questionnaire administered at rest. You have to measure the body during the work.

## The Framework

The wellness/patient-safety distinction is not semantic. It has structural consequences.

When interoceptive training is categorized as **wellness**, it occupies the same political and budgetary space as gym membership subsidies and mindfulness apps. It is discretionary. It competes with revenue-generating priorities. It is framed as something a clinician does for themselves, on their own time, to feel better. The decision-maker is HR. The evidence standard is employee satisfaction.

When interoceptive training is categorized as **patient safety**, the architecture changes entirely. Budget allocations are mandatory in patient-safety frameworks. The evidence standard shifts to measurable clinical outcomes. The political weight changes from "optional wellness initiative" to "professional competency." The decision-maker moves from HR to clinical operations.

Congdon, Boyd, and Alexander's 2025 integrative review of CRNA burnout illustrates the cost of the current miscategorization. Burnout prevalence across 15 studies ranged from 12.5% to 72%. Contributing factors included moral distress, lack of autonomy, and poor physician relations. Outcomes included intention to leave one's primary position, decreased job satisfaction, and fatigue. Congdon et al. (2025) [PMID: 40440194] None of these outcomes are categorized in the literature as patient-safety variables. They are workforce and HR variables. The same phenomenon — a dysregulated clinician — lands in a different policy apparatus depending on which label is applied.

The sensor-calibration frame is not a marketing trick. It is a correct re-categorization that follows mechanistically from the closed-loop-controller premise. If the clinician's nervous system is the instrument, then the instrument's calibration state is a clinical operations issue. That is what the evidence says.

---

## The Failure Mode

The reframe fails in two specific conditions.

**Condition one: sensor calibration deployed as a substitute for system reform.** Dean and Talbot identified this trap in their 2018 STAT essay on moral injury in healthcare — an argument that has accumulated substantial empirical backing since. Moral injury is structurally generated: by systems that ask clinicians to act against their professional values, by workloads that preclude the care patients deserve, by administrative machinery that obstructs clinical judgment. Calibrating the sensor in a morally injurious environment does not eliminate the injurious signal. It makes the clinician more capable of surviving the injury, which is genuinely valuable — but it must not be deployed as a reason to defer system-level reform. The framing explicitly requires both: structural change AND sensor calibration. Not one as a substitute for the other.

**Condition two: training effects that don't persist.** Rueda and colleagues (2026) studied a multimodal digital health intervention for 99 frontline nurses, combining VR stress simulation, HRV biofeedback, and psychoeducation. Anxiety and stress decreased at 2 weeks post-intervention. By 12 weeks, those gains were not sustained. Rueda et al. (2026) [PMID: 42275639] This is not a failure of the mechanism — it is a failure of dose. Sensor calibration is not a one-time procedure. It is a maintenance protocol. A single workshop or 6-week program followed by nothing is the equivalent of recalibrating a thermistor once and then running it unattended for the remainder of its operational life. The Andersen protocol for police officers with posttraumatic stress injuries (2023) makes this explicit: ANS dysregulation requires ongoing Autonomic Modulation Training, not a single-course intervention. Andersen et al. (2023) [PMID: 37223981]

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment. This is not a clinical trial — it is a 12-week proof test.

**Population:** 12 CRNAs at a single hospital department. Target departments with moderate-high case volume and documented burnout concern.

**Instrument:** Wearable RMSSD monitoring, worn for 30 minutes before and after each case shift for 12 weeks. No intervention for weeks 1–4 (baseline). NeuroMinute protocol deployed weeks 5–12 (two per shift, one pre-induction and one end-of-case).

**Measures:** RMSSD delta (pre-shift vs. post-shift, week-by-week trend). MAIA-2 administered at weeks 1, 4, 8, 12. Optional: CRNA self-report of case confidence at end of each shift (1–10 scale, non-validated but trend-informative).

**Success criteria:** Attenuated RMSSD decline across shift (clinicians finishing cases with less autonomic depletion than baseline). MAIA-2 "Noticing" and "Self-Regulation" subscale improvement. Feasibility criteria: protocol adherence >70%.

**What this cannot prove:** That improved RMSSD → better patient outcomes. That link requires a separate, larger, IRB-approved study with clinical outcome linkage. This test only establishes that the intervention moves the physiological variable in the right direction in the right population.

---

## The Connection

The vault's closed-loop-controller frame was always doing two things: describing what CRNAs are, and arguing that this description has structural implications for training, research, and professional norms. This essay is the second move — taking the structural implication and translating it into a budget argument, an evidence argument, and a political argument.

What changes if this frame lands: interoceptive training for CRNAs is no longer a wellness elective. It is instrument maintenance. The people who fund it, approve it, and hold CRNAs accountable for doing it are clinical operations leaders and patient-safety committees — not HR. The evidence required to justify it is HRV data linked to case outcomes, not employee satisfaction surveys. The research pipeline that SRL is building — wearable monitoring, MAIA-2 validated assessment, NeuroMinute as a titrated intervention — is precisely the evidence apparatus this category requires.

The sensor is the clinician. Calibrating it is not self-care. It is occupational necessity.

---

*Before the next induction, somewhere in an OR, a CRNA's anterior insula is integrating a morning's worth of signals — the MAP trending down, the surgeon's body language, the metallic taste of anxiety left over from the difficult airway two cases ago — and producing a decision. Whether that decision emerges from a clean signal or a noisy one is a patient-safety question. The field just hasn't named it that yet.*
