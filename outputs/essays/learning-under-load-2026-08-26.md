# Notes from the Lab: Learn Under Load — The Case Is the Curriculum

*Knowledge encoded under low stress and retrieved under high stress degrades at exactly the moment it's needed — and the fix is a structured learning architecture woven inside each clinical case, not studied between them.*

---

## The Observation

A CRNA has studied the difficult airway algorithm. They reviewed it this morning — slides, decision tree, the blade angles. Maybe mannequin practice last month. Then the patient desaturates faster than expected, the anatomy is unusual, and the algorithm — the one reviewed this morning — has gone somewhere inaccessible. Not absent entirely. Somewhere unreachable.

The hands move. The decisions happen. But the structured knowledge that was supposed to scaffold this moment isn't loading. This is not a knowledge deficit. It is an architecture deficit.

The clinician built the knowledge in a low-arousal context and is attempting to retrieve it from a high-arousal context. These are neurologically different states. What encodes under quiet study does not necessarily fire under acute stress. The result is a specific paradox: the more conscientiously a clinician studies outside of pressure, the more confident they become in knowledge that pressure can make unavailable. They feel prepared. They were not preparing for the right retrieval context.

---

## The Mechanism

Beilock documented two choking mechanisms that converge during clinical crises. The first: **explicit monitoring** — pressure redirects attention to step-by-step components of procedures that should run automatically, breaking the fluid execution that expertise requires. The second: **working memory depletion** — anxiety consumes cognitive resources, leaving insufficient capacity for clinical reasoning. The CRNA who rehearsed the difficult airway algorithm in a quiet room is specifically vulnerable to the first: under genuine pressure, self-focused attention fragments the automated procedure into attended steps, and attended steps lose their timing and fluency.

Staal, in a comprehensive synthesis of military and aerospace research for NASA (2004), identified a convergent finding from three decades of stress-cognition literature: attentional narrowing under stress filters relevant cues at the same threshold as irrelevant ones. At moderate arousal, this narrowing is adaptive — Easterbrook's cue-utilization theory established this in 1959. At high arousal, the narrowing becomes pathological. The complete algorithm, with its branching conditional logic, is exactly the kind of multi-threaded information that stress narrows away.

A third mechanism is state-dependent encoding. Retrieval is enhanced when the retrieval context matches the encoding context — a principle established in cognitive psychology over decades of laboratory research. Knowledge encoded in a quiet conference room carries the physiological and attentional signature of that room. The high-acuity OR is a different encoding context. The mismatch is not metaphorical; it is neurological.

Fawzi and colleagues (2026) tested whether deliberately structured learning strategies embedded within clinical education could overcome this mismatch in medical interns. A curriculum integrating retrieval practice, spaced repetition, interleaved problem sets, metacognitive coaching, and mindfulness produced significant improvements in clinical competency (p < 0.001 on faculty-assessed mini-CEX scores) and a drop in perceived stress from 48% to 32% (p = 0.008). The key finding: resilience increase was the strongest independent predictor of clinical competency improvement (R² = 0.35). The mechanism is not just cognitive — it is autonomic. The curriculum changed how the body responds to performance conditions, not just how much content could be recalled.

Tung and colleagues (2023), reviewing evidence-based learning strategies for radiology professionals, confirmed that three techniques consistently outperform passive restudying: **retrieval practice** (testing rather than rereading), **spaced repetition** (distribution over time rather than massed review), and **interleaving** (mixing problem types rather than blocking by category). All three improve *transfer* — the capacity to apply knowledge to novel problems, including high-pressure novel problems. This is the learning-science fingerprint of robust encoding: not faster recall in the same context, but available recall across unfamiliar contexts.

Marinelli and colleagues (2022) documented the application of these principles for surgical trainees in otolaryngology, finding that spaced repetition combined with active recall produced superior long-term retention compared to conventional passive review — with the greatest advantage appearing at the six-month retention test, the test that matters in clinical practice.

One confound must be stated explicitly. These studies primarily examine knowledge retention and recall, not motor-procedural execution under acute physiological stress. The evidence that retrieval practice improves *performance under genuine clinical crisis* — as opposed to knowledge recall under examination conditions — is thinner. What can be said with confidence: the retrieval architecture matters for what can be recalled under pressure. Whether recall translates to fluid execution under extremis requires the additional mechanism of pressure-matched practice.

Sadideen and colleagues (2013) resolved this for procedural skills in their review of surgical expertise development. Ericsson's deliberate practice — specifically, practice structured to challenge the learner at the edge of current competence, with immediate feedback, in conditions approximating target performance — was the primary mechanism for expertise acquisition across surgical specialties. The key parameter is not cumulative hours but specificity of the challenge relative to the context of use.

This is Randy's synthesis: the CRNA who wants knowledge that survives pressure must train in pressure-matched contexts, extract learning while the case is still warm, and then drive that extraction into long-term memory through structured retrieval cycles. The four-phase learning loop is the operationalization.

---

## The Protocol

**Four phases. Every case. No exceptions.**

1. **Pre-case intention** (2–3 minutes, before the patient is present): Name one specific thing to notice during this case — a clinical judgment call, a team communication moment, a physiological response. "I'm going to track how I handle the transition from induction to maintenance today." The intention creates a directed attentional filter that transforms lived experience into learnable material.

2. **In-case micro-registration** (throughout): When a surprising event occurs — an unexpected hemodynamic response, an airway finding that doesn't match the chart, a teammate's unusually effective cue — register it mentally. One-word note. Not documentation, not analysis. Registration: "flag." This prevents the episodic trace from being overwritten by the next event.

3. **Post-case harvest** (3–5 minutes, immediately after): Convert the registered moments into declarative sentences while episodic memory is still high-resolution. Three sentences or one voice memo. What happened. What surprised you. What you'd do differently. This is the encoding act that matters. Beveril and colleagues (2019) showed that structured post-simulation reflection produced significantly better knowledge retention in perioperative nurses compared to simulation alone — the reflection, not the simulation, was the mechanism.

4. **Spaced retrieval** (that evening, next morning, one week later): Retrieve the harvest note. Don't reread it passively — attempt to reconstruct it from memory first, then check. Each retrieval strengthens the memory trace. After three retrievals spaced across a week, the case knowledge is no longer episodic. It has become part of the procedural substrate that survives acute stress.

---

## The Failure Mode

The protocol breaks when the pre-case intention creates a learning-performance conflict. Beilock's explicit monitoring mechanism applies in reverse: a CRNA simultaneously trying to perform and consciously observing themselves as a learner has split working memory. The observer and the performer draw on overlapping attentional resources. The fix: set the intention before the case begins, then release it. The intention should run in background, not foreground.

The protocol also fails under high case volume. Seven cases in a shift. The pre-case intention and post-case harvest feel unjustifiable. This reasoning inverts the logic: high-volume days produce the most learning material and the most reliable forgetting. A two-sentence harvest is enough. The harvest must scale down, not be skipped.

A subtler failure: the harvest becomes an error log only. Noticing what went wrong is valuable; noticing what worked and *why* is more valuable. The expertise-building mechanism requires encoding of success as explicitly as failure. Sadideen and colleagues (2013) found that adaptive experts — those who excel not just in routine but in novel high-stakes situations — build their edge from accurate models of what generates good outcomes, not just inventories of mistakes.

---

## The Test

Fourteen CRNAs. Fourteen working days. Days 1–2: baseline assessment — written response to five clinical scenarios, timed. Days 3–12: structured pre-case intention logged in a shared note-taking tool; post-case harvest submitted within 15 minutes of case close; retrieval prompts sent at day + 1 and day + 7. Days 13–14: same five baseline scenarios plus five new scenarios not previously encountered. Primary outcome: retrieval accuracy on the new scenarios (transfer). Secondary: self-reported clinical confidence, perceived preparedness, shift-end cognitive fatigue.

Optional instrumentation: wearable HRV during post-case harvest windows to confirm low-arousal encoding state. If HRV during harvest is below resting baseline, the harvest is occurring in residual sympathetic activation — and the encoding quality is compromised. This becomes a biofeedback trigger for a 60-second regulation protocol before harvesting.

## The Connection

[[learning-under-load]] is the cognitive infrastructure that [[gap-moment-training]] creates the time for. The post-case harvest is a gap moment. The pre-case intention is a gap moment. Without the structural habit of catching those transitions — treating them as trainable windows rather than dead time — the four-phase loop has no entry point. The [[embodied-clinical-intelligence]] that defines expert clinical judgment accumulates exactly through this kind of deliberate experiential extraction. [[clinician-durability]] depends on it: clinicians who extract and consolidate learning from each case compound expertise; those who perform without extraction repeat experience without accruing wisdom. The 28-year CRNA career is either a compounding asset or a repeated present tense. The learning architecture determines which.

---

*The next case begins. Two minutes before induction, the room is quiet. One intention is named. The case runs — it always does. Afterward, three sentences while the memory is still warm. Tonight, a retrieval attempt. Next week, another. What was once a case becomes a rung. The career climbs.*
