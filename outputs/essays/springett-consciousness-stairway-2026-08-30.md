# Notes from the Lab: The Phase Diagram of Awareness — Why Consciousness Is Not a Staircase

*The Springett consciousness framework describes not a staircase of permanent developmental stages but a phase diagram of awareness — where consciousness can move between ice (rigid, identity-locked) and steam (expansive, field-aware) states within a single clinical shift — and the clinical implication is that Gap Moment Training is not a stage accelerator but a phase regulator: a method to melt frozen awareness back into fluid presence when the case demands it.*

---

## The Observation

Two CRNAs, same years of experience, same residency program, same case complexity. Watch them at minute forty of a laparoscopic Whipple. The first is competent — tracking numbers with precision, hands on the right things. But something has closed. Eyes stay on the monitor array. The surgeon's commentary becomes background radiation. The scrub tech's small signal — a slight change in posture, a barely audible intake of breath — goes completely unregistered. The clinician is in the case. They are also somehow not in it.

The second seems to occupy more space. They are watching the same monitors, but peripheral vision is active. A hand rests loosely on the drape, receiving haptic information that isn't being consciously processed but is informing something. When the surgeon pauses a beat longer than usual between sutures, this clinician has already shifted awareness toward the field before any verbal signal arrives. They are not managing the case. They are receiving it.

These two clinicians have the same stage. They are in different phases.

## The Mechanism

Tara Springett, the British-Tibetan Buddhist teacher and psychotherapist, describes consciousness development using the metaphor of water in its three physical states. Ice: rigid, fixed, identity-fused — the self as a separate, boundaried object in a world of other objects. Melted ice: the transitional state, structure beginning to give way, edges softening. Water: fluid, adaptive, moving according to the terrain without losing integrity. Steam: expansive, diffuse, no longer contained by any single vessel — awareness extending into the field itself.

Springett presents this as a developmental model, and in the long arc, it is one. But the framework has a property that conventional stage models don't: **phase reversibility**. Water freezes when the temperature drops. Steam condenses back to water under pressure. A clinician who has touched steam in contemplative practice can still freeze under surgical stress. The phase and the stage are different variables.

The neuroscience confirms this distinction.

Lindström, Goldin, Mårtensson, and Cardeña investigated what they called *trait self-boundarylessness* — a measurable personality-like disposition toward the fluid, de-identified quality of the steam state — using fMRI (2023) [PMID: 37114163]. They found that trait boundarylessness correlates with greater self-endorsement of words describing fluidity, and with reduced activity in the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus during mind-wandering. The PCC/precuneus complex is the hub of the default mode network's self-referential architecture — the neural engine of the "ice" state's constant identity-maintenance narrative. In higher-boundarylessness individuals, this engine quiets. Not through suppression from the outside, but from within.

The more clinically urgent finding was what Lindström and colleagues found when they gave both non-meditators and experienced meditators a simple instruction: *direct attention to the centre of experience*. This single instruction elicited brain activation equivalent to meditation onset in both groups — increases in anterior precentral gyrus and anterior insula, decreases in default mode network areas. Non-meditators, with no prior training, shifted phase when given the right instruction.

This is the clinical fact that changes the training architecture. The phase transition is not locked to developmental stage. It is accessible to instruction — which means it is accessible to a structured sixty-second protocol.

Osaka, Minamoto, Yaoi, Azuma, and Osaka (2026) examined Zen monks with an average of 7.53 years of meditation practice using fMRI during a Stroop cognitive conflict task followed by meditation [PMID: 42039022]. The standard finding in non-meditators is that task engagement activates the Central Executive Network (CEN) while suppressing the Default Mode Network — the classic antagonistic seesaw. The monks showed something different: during conflict trials, connectivity analyses revealed *suppression within DMN-related regions* rather than DMN-CEN antagonism. The meditators weren't suppressing self-referential activity by cranking up task focus. The DMN had reorganized internally.

In Springett's terms: ice state is characterized by high DMN activity (self-referential spinning) with constant suppression by CEN (task pressure). The DMN and CEN fight for resources. Steam state is characterized by a DMN that has quieted its self-maintenance function — not because something external shut it down, but because the self-model it maintains has become more fluid. There is nothing to defend. The CEN can run clean.

This is not a small theoretical distinction. In the OR, a frozen DMN (ice state) produces the clinical picture of state drift: the implicit self-monitoring that should be background becomes foregrounded, competing with case monitoring for the same attentional resources. The clinician who feels the need to perform competence — whose awareness is partially occupied by the self-model of being-a-good-CRNA — has fewer resources available to receive the case.

Payne's 2015 synthesis of Benner's novice-to-expert model with Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis provides the clinical-practice bridge [PMID: 26154676]. The somatic marker system — the pre-cognitive, body-based signal processing that generates what experienced clinicians call "something feels off" — operates on inputs from the viscera, the periphery, and the musculoskeletal state. These signals are received by the insula. But the insula's signal-to-noise ratio depends on the state of the DMN's self-referential activity. In ice state, the cortical narrative (am I doing this correctly? what did the attending say about this case?) competes with the somatic signal channel. The expert's body knows something. The ice-state brain overrides it with rumination.

Payne's framework predicts what Miller and Hill (2017) confirmed in a sample of 98 practicing nurses across three acuity levels [PMID: 28831858]: nurses who self-rated at higher Benner proficiency levels scored significantly higher on the Rew Intuitive Judgment Scale. Years of experience correlated with higher proficiency ratings. And proficiency correlated with intuition. What the data doesn't show — but the phase model predicts — is that the expert's intuition is state-dependent. The same expert, in ice phase during a stressful case, may score like a competent-stage clinician. Not because their stage regressed, but because their current phase is suppressing somatic marker access.

Ruther, Jackson, and vanSonnenberg's 2025 narrative review of 27 neuroimaging studies on Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction found that MBSR consistently produced functional changes — enhanced coupling between medial prefrontal cortex and amygdala, increased activity in superior parietal lobule and posterior cingulate cortex — while structural MRI changes were inconsistent across studies [PMID: 41422646]. The functional-not-structural pattern is consistent with a phase model rather than a stage model: contemplative practice produces preferential activation of water/steam-state circuitry without necessarily rebuilding the hardware. The phase becomes more accessible. It does not become the permanent default.

## The Protocol

If consciousness has phases, the training target shifts from *who you become* to *what you can access under load*.

The five steps of the Stairway Meta-Awareness Protocol map precisely onto the melting sequence. Step one — somatic check-in — interrupts the ice-state self-referential loop by redirecting attention to the body. This is the initial melting: the rigid identity-model (ice) begins to soften as the clinician's attention moves from cortical narrative to visceral reality. Step two — naming an emotion — continues the melt: the emotion is no longer the clinician, it is information the clinician is receiving. Step three — peripheral environmental scan — marks the shift to water: awareness now includes context rather than being defined against it. Step four — team field check — is steam beginning to rise: the boundary of "me" extends into the dyadic or group nervous system. Step five — unified presence — is the steam state: alert, diffuse, fully available.

The sequence works not because it is elegant but because it follows the neurobiological dependencies. Somatic signal must be received before emotional information can be parsed. Environmental context must be included before relational field can be sensed. Each step prepares the substrate for the next.

## The Failure Mode

The phase model has two misapplication risks.

The first is **forced steam**. A clinician who skips steps — who tries to enter unified field awareness without first grounding in somatic signal — is generating a cognitive simulation of the steam state, not a phase transition. The DMN has not reorganized. The body has not been consulted. The "expanded awareness" is cortical performance. Genuine steam state leaves a physical trace: reduced tension in the shoulders, slight deepening of breath, HRV coherence beginning to emerge. Performed steam leaves a different trace: activation, effortfulness, the subtle hum of trying.

The second is **misjudging regression as failure**. Under extreme operative stress — unexpected hemorrhage, equipment failure, cardiovascular event — consciousness refreezes. This is not pathology. Ice is the appropriate phase for a narrow-aperture crisis response. The failure mode is staying in ice after the crisis has resolved. Water is the resting operational phase. Steam is the signature of the gap moment. Knowing the difference — and knowing how to melt deliberately — is the trainable skill.

## The Test

Ten CRNAs, two clinical sites, fourteen days.

**Measurement:**
- RMSSD via Polar H10, captured pre-shift and at end of each gap moment
- Self-reported phase rating at case start (ice/water/steam, 3-point scale)
- SMAP use log with timestamps

**Primary question:** Does pre-shift RMSSD predict self-reported phase at case start?

**Secondary question:** Does deliberate SMAP practice during the pre-shift period shift baseline phase by day fourteen compared to day one?

**Success criterion:** A statistically significant correlation between RMSSD and self-reported phase, and a minimum 0.5-point improvement on the 3-point scale between week one and week two means.

This is not a clinical trial. It is a calibration study: does the measurement instrument (RMSSD) track the phenomenon it is supposed to track (awareness phase)?

## The Connection

The vault contains a staircase of concepts ascending from [[interoception]] to [[embodied-metacognition]] to [[vertical-development]]. The Springett model adds the dimension the staircase metaphor cannot hold: the same step looks different at different temperatures. A clinician at Kegan Stage 4 in ice phase is clinically indistinguishable from a clinician at Stage 3 in water phase. Stage predicts access probability. Phase predicts access now.

[[gap-moment-training]] is the heating element. The [[stairway-meta-awareness-protocol]] is the melting sequence. The Springett consciousness stairway is the physics that explains why both of them work.
