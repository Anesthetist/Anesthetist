# Notes from the Lab: Polyanchora — Multiple Anchors, One Nervous System

*Single-anchor meditation practices fail under clinical load because they depend on a single attentional channel that saturates when competing demands arrive — Polyanchora trains the nervous system to maintain regulatory access through multiple simultaneous sensory anchors, creating redundancy that holds when any one channel is overwhelmed.*

## The Observation

The instruction is simple: "Focus on your breath." The CRNA tries it between cases. For about eight seconds, it works. Then the OR door opens. A monitor beeps. The circulator asks a question. The surgeon's tone shifts. Each interruption hijacks the single attentional channel that was holding the breath anchor. By the third interruption, the breath is gone. Not forgotten — displaced. The channel was occupied.

This is the fundamental problem with single-anchor meditation in clinical environments: the environments are designed to interrupt. The OR generates 50-70 auditory events per hour. The CRNA manages 6-12 simultaneous data streams. A practice that requires exclusive attentional access to one sensory channel is asking for a resource the clinician cannot spare.

But watch what happens when the instruction changes. "Notice your breath. Now also notice the weight of your feet on the floor. Now also notice the temperature of the air on your skin." Three anchors. When the door opens and displaces the breath anchor, the feet are still there. When the monitor beeps and captures auditory attention, the skin temperature remains. The clinician doesn't lose regulation — they lose one anchor and still have two.

This is redundancy engineering applied to attention. The same principle that puts three flight computers in an aircraft. Not because any single computer is unreliable, but because the environment guarantees that any single system will be disrupted.

## The Mechanism

The neurophysiology of multi-anchor attention operates through the fronto-parietal network — the same neural infrastructure that CRNAs rely on during complex procedures.

Yordanova et al. (2021) demonstrated that experienced meditators who practice multiple meditation styles (focused attention, open monitoring, loving-kindness) exhibit strong theta synchronization of both fronto-parietal and medial-frontal brain networks, with connectivity patterns varying by meditation type and expertise level [PMID: 33649378]. The finding is specific: expert meditators don't use a single attentional mode. They have multiple modes available and switch between them fluidly. The theta synchronization across fronto-parietal hubs is the neural signature of this switching capacity — the same neural real estate that governs multi-task clinical performance.

Tanaka et al. (2022) extended this finding to open monitoring meditation specifically, showing that expert meditators demonstrate enhanced frontoparietal gamma coherence — the right frontoparietal network acting as a "biomarker of enhanced open monitoring meditation training" [PMID: 35636352]. Open monitoring is the meditation style closest to Polyanchora: rather than fixating on a single object, the practitioner holds simultaneous awareness of multiple sensory streams. The gamma coherence signature suggests that training this capacity produces measurable, lasting changes in how the fronto-parietal network coordinates cross-modal attention.

Ferrari and Noppeney (2021) provided the mechanistic detail: attention controls multisensory perception through two distinct pathways at different levels of the cortical hierarchy [PMID: 34793436]. Pre-stimulus attention enhances the reliability of sensory inputs in modality-specific and posterior parietal cortices. Post-stimulus task demands enable flexible Bayesian combination of sensory estimates at higher cortical levels. This dual-mechanism architecture means that Polyanchora training could work at both levels: pre-loading attention across multiple sensory channels (enhancing reliability of each anchor) AND training the higher-order integration that flexibly combines them.

Farb, Segal, and Anderson (2013) showed that mindfulness training alters cortical representations of interoceptive attention — specifically, increasing anterior insular activation during body-focused attention while decreasing dorsomedial prefrontal recruitment [PMID: 22689216]. The finding indicates that interoceptive attention training produces neuroplasticity in the very circuits that process body signals. Polyanchora extends this: if single-anchor interoceptive training changes insula function, multi-anchor training that recruits insula, somatosensory cortex, auditory cortex, and parietal cortex simultaneously should produce broader cross-modal plasticity.

**Confounds to state explicitly:** The leap from "expert meditators show fronto-parietal changes" to "60-second multi-anchor practice produces the same changes" is an extrapolation. Yordanova's and Tanaka's meditators had thousands of hours of practice. Polyanchora claims efficacy in 60-second windows for novices. The time-scale gap is enormous. The honest claim is that Polyanchora trains the same attentional architecture that expert meditators develop — not that it produces expert-level neural changes in 60 seconds. What it may produce is functional multi-anchor access at a basic level, sufficient to maintain one regulatory anchor when others are displaced. Testing this specific claim requires its own data.

Additionally, multi-anchor attention divides the finite attentional resource across more targets. Kahneman's capacity model predicts that adding anchors should *reduce* performance on each. Polyanchora's counter-claim is that the anchors create redundancy, not competition — because the goal isn't deep processing of each anchor but threshold-level maintenance of access. The distinction between "deeply attend to seven things" (impossible) and "maintain background awareness of seven things" (trainable) is the design boundary.

## The Protocol

Polyanchora operates as a progressive stacking protocol within the 60-second [[neurominute]] window:

**Level 1: Dual Anchor (Weeks 1-2)**
Breath + one additional anchor (heartbeat, feet on floor, or hand contact). The instruction: "Breathe at your resonant frequency. Also notice [second anchor]. When you lose one, the other is still there." The training surface is noticing the loss and recovery, not maintaining perfect dual awareness.

**Level 2: Tri-Anchor (Weeks 3-4)**
Breath + heartbeat + one exteroceptive anchor (ambient sound, peripheral vision, or skin temperature). The shift from two interoceptive anchors to mixed interoceptive-exteroceptive is the [[anterocept]] Domain 2 → Domain 3 transition. The exteroceptive anchor prevents the "eyes-closed cave" failure mode — staying connected to the environment while regulating.

**Level 3: Full Stack (Weeks 5+)**
Up to five simultaneous anchors: breath, heartbeat, proprioception (muscle tension), one exteroceptive (visual field OR ambient sound), and emotional valence. The seventh anchor (haptic metronome from Apple Watch) is optional and provides the external pacing cue that stabilizes the breath anchor against displacement.

**Progression criteria:** The clinician advances when they can maintain awareness of the current level's anchor count through at least one external interruption (door opening, monitor alarm, verbal question) without losing all anchors. Losing one anchor and recovering is success. Losing all anchors is the signal to stay at the current level.

## The Failure Mode

Polyanchora breaks under three conditions:

**1. Cognitive overload from premature stacking.** Adding anchors too fast overwhelms rather than scaffolds. A clinician who has not stabilized single-anchor breath awareness and attempts five-anchor Polyanchora will experience the practice as chaos, not regulation. The [[titration-to-effect]] principle applies: start with two, prove stability, add one. The protocol must enforce progression gates rather than letting eager practitioners skip levels.

**2. Anchor competition instead of anchor redundancy.** If the clinician tries to actively process all anchors simultaneously — deeply attending to heartbeat AND breath AND visual field — the result is divided attention degradation, not attentional redundancy. The design distinction is critical: Polyanchora anchors operate at threshold awareness ("I can tell my heartbeat is there"), not focal attention ("I am counting my heartbeats"). Training must explicitly distinguish background-level monitoring from focal-level processing. The instruction "notice, don't study" captures this.

**3. Environmental anchor unavailability.** Some clinical environments eliminate specific anchors. Wearing a lead apron eliminates proprioceptive sensitivity in the torso. A loud OR eliminates ambient sound as a useful anchor. Wearing gloves eliminates skin temperature. The system must train flexibility about which anchors are available rather than dependence on a fixed set. The question is not "can you hold five specific anchors?" but "can you find three available anchors in any environment?"

## The Test

**14-day proof test with 10 CRNAs:**

- **Days 1-3 (baseline):** Single-anchor breath focus during gap moments. Measure: number of seconds anchor is maintained before first displacement, number of anchor recoveries per 60-second window, subjective difficulty rating (0-10).
- **Days 4-7 (dual anchor):** Breath + heartbeat. Same measures plus: when one anchor is lost, is the other still accessible? Binary yes/no per interruption event.
- **Days 8-10 (tri-anchor):** Breath + heartbeat + feet-on-floor. Same measures.
- **Days 11-14 (tri-anchor in clinical environment):** Practice during actual shifts, not just between cases. Measure: anchor maintenance through real clinical interruptions.

**Primary outcome:** Anchor survival rate — percentage of external interruptions where at least one anchor remains accessible. Single-anchor baseline vs. tri-anchor intervention.

**Secondary outcomes:** Subjective regulation capacity (VAS 0-10) after interrupted gap moments, HRV delta after interrupted vs. uninterrupted gap moments.

**Positive result:** Anchor survival rate ≥ 80% with tri-anchor protocol vs. ≤ 40% with single-anchor, during real clinical interruptions. The 80% threshold means that in 4 out of 5 interruptions, the clinician still has regulatory access through at least one anchor.

**What would falsify the concept:** If tri-anchor practice shows no improvement in anchor survival over single-anchor — meaning adding anchors doesn't create redundancy but merely dilutes attention — then the multi-anchor model is wrong. The redesign would shift toward a single robust anchor (cardiac-anchored breathing via Apple Watch haptics, which can't be displaced by environmental noise) rather than a distributed anchor portfolio.

## The Connection

Polyanchora sits at the intersection of [[anterocept]] (which provides the progressive sensory focus spectrum), [[multi-phase-interoceptive-coupling]] (which provides the intra-body signal coupling), and [[neurominute]] (which provides the 60-second delivery format). It is the training methodology that turns the interoceptive capacity built in [[interoception]] training into a clinically deployable skill that survives the OR environment.

The metaphor is aviation, not meditation. Redundant systems, not deeper concentration. The question Polyanchora answers is not "can you focus harder?" but "when your primary system is disrupted, does a backup come online?" Every CRNA understands this principle — they apply it to patient monitoring equipment every day. Polyanchora applies it to the monitoring equipment between their ears.

---

*Notice your breath. Now also notice the weight of your body in the chair. Now the ambient sound in the room. Three anchors, running in parallel. Let one go — deliberately, just to see. The other two are still there. That's the skill. Not holding on tighter. Having more to hold.*
