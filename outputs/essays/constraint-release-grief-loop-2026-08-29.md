# Notes from the Lab: The Incomplete Cycle — Grief in the Operating Room and the Protocol That Isn't There

*In the operating room, grief accumulates as disenfranchised load — recognized nowhere, processed never — and this suppression measurably attenuates vagal tone and consumes prefrontal resources; the Gap Moment is where micro-completion becomes physiologically possible without abandoning task readiness.*

---

## The Observation

Fifteen minutes after a pediatric patient dies on the table, the CRNA is masked up for the next induction. No protocol governs what happens in those fifteen minutes. No institutional language acknowledges what just occurred. The chart is closed, the room is cleaned, the next family is in the waiting area. The body is not cleaned. The body is carrying something the system has no container for.

Lathrop (2017) named this pattern in physicians: disenfranchised grief — grief the workplace does not recognize as legitimate, grief that therefore has no sanctioned form of expression or processing. The losses are real, cumulative, and ambiguous: the patient who didn't make it, the near-miss that cost a week of sleep, the pediatric case that broke the sequence of competence. These losses accumulate without acknowledgment, without ritual, without any designated window for physiological completion.

Udayakumaran (2026) catalogued five dimensions of surgeon grief specific to clinical futility: grief of unexpressed mastery, grief of identity disruption, grief of anticipatory knowledge, grief of perceived failure, and disenfranchised grief — each feeding the others, each contributing to what the literature now measures as occupational burnout and career attrition. Liang and colleagues (2026) found occupational grief at high levels among pediatric emergency and ICU nurses, with disenfranchised grief as the single strongest predictor of total grief burden.

The CRNA carries all of this. The system offers nothing.

---

## The Mechanism

O'Connor and colleagues (2008) produced a finding that reframes what grief actually is inside the body. Using fMRI in bereaved women, they found that reminders of the deceased activated the nucleus accumbens — the brain's primary reward center — in those with complicated grief, but not in those with non-complicated grief. The implication is physiologically precise: the grieving brain is not only registering pain. It is seeking reward. Attachment creates reward prediction; the attached person became a predictor of positive outcomes. Loss disrupts the prediction, but not the seeking. The nucleus accumbens continues to fire in response to reminders of the deceased because the brain is still running the old loop.

This finding explains the quality of grief that experienced clinicians will recognize: it is not simply sad. It pulls. It recurrs unprompted. It activates in the middle of the next case when a patient sounds like the patient who died. That pull is dopaminergic, reward-seeking, and physiologically real.

Kakarala and colleagues (2020) extended this in a systematic review, finding evidence for differential activation across the amygdala, orbitofrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and nucleus accumbens in prolonged grief disorder. The network does not turn off between reminders. It maintains a kind of standing vigilance — yearning as a background physiological state, consuming resources across every subsequent task.

The autonomic cost of suppressing this was documented by Yoshiike and colleagues (2025) in a prospective Japanese study of 35 bereaved adults. They found a nonlinear relationship between grief avoidance and HF-HRV — the high-frequency component of heart rate variability that directly reflects parasympathetic tone. The critical finding: in the low-avoidance group, HF-HRV and grief improved together longitudinally. In the high-avoidance group, neither moved. Avoidance was associated with attenuated vagal tone and the persistence of grief over time. The body maintains the grief load precisely by refusing to contact it.

The prefrontal cost was quantified by Freed and colleagues (2009) at Columbia. Using fMRI in twenty recently bereaved subjects, they found that grief regulation works through functional connectivity between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the amygdala. Higher DLPFC-amygdala connectivity correlated with reduced attentional bias toward grief cues. The prefrontal cortex is doing active suppression work — screening reminders before they reach consciousness. For a CRNA managing a complex case, this is not hypothetical cognitive load. It is real-time prefrontal consumption that competes directly with the attentional resources required for clinical monitoring, intraoperative adjustment, and situational awareness.

O'Connor (2019) synthesized the broader picture in a review that covers five decades of bereavement research: complicated grief is associated with rumination, cortisol dysregulation, and measurable immune function changes — including altered leukocyte gene expression patterns in inflammatory and antiviral pathways. Grief is not a psychological event that happens to have emotional consequences. It is a physiological event that happens to have psychological correlates. The body is in this.

Three confounds require explicit acknowledgment. First, most of this literature studies spousal bereavement — a different loss topology than occupational patient death. Whether the nucleus accumbens pattern O'Connor identified applies to patient attachment specifically remains unstudied. Second, individual differences in attachment style and grief regulation style vary widely; the protocol described below will not produce uniform effects. Third, this essay isolates the autonomic and prefrontal cost of grief suppression from the broader burnout literature — many variables change simultaneously in burned-out clinicians, and grief suppression is one of several contributing mechanisms, not the sole explanation.

## The Protocol

Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, applied here: the constraint is not the grief. The constraint is the blocked completion. When the physiological cycle of grief cannot run — because the culture doesn't permit it, because the next case is on the table, because there is no language for it — the system optimizes around the blocked cycle rather than through it. Optimization-around-constraint is expensive. The cycle draws resources indefinitely.

The Constraint-Release Grief Loop is designed for 60-second deployment in a claimed Gap Moment. It is not therapy. It is scheduled partial completion — one movement of the cycle, not resolution.

**Five steps:**

1. **Locate.** Where in the body is the charge? Not the story — the sensation. Throat, thorax, behind the eyes, jaw. Name the physical location.

2. **Label.** One word. What is this? Sadness. Helplessness. Anger. Unfinished. Not a narrative. The label interrupts the prefrontal suppression loop and routes the sensation to a different processing channel.

3. **Contact.** Three resonant breaths directed toward the located sensation. Not suppression; not analysis. Directional attention to the physical location of the charge.

4. **Allow one movement.** Notice whether the sensation shifts, softens, intensifies, or moves. One cycle. The loop is designed around the recognition that grief does not complete in sixty seconds. It is designed to allow one step of a multi-session physiological process.

5. **Return.** Deliberate attentional shift outward. Next patient's airway. Back to task. The return is part of the protocol — not an interruption of it.

The five steps map onto the Stairway Meta-Awareness Protocol: Steps 1-2 of the grief loop parallel Steps 1-2 of the SMAP (interoceptive grounding + affective labeling). Both protocols use the same attentional infrastructure. A clinician who has practiced SMAP has the substrate already.

## The Failure Mode

**Activation during active patient management.** This protocol is designed exclusively for claimed Gap Moments — the transitions between cases, the post-induction pre-incision window, the recovery room handoff interval. It is contraindicated during active patient monitoring. A clinician who attempts Step 3 (contact) during a critical phase of a case has misapplied the protocol. The Gap Moment is the container; without it, the protocol becomes a competing attentional demand.

**Skipping the location step.** Clinicians trained in suppression will attempt to run Steps 3-5 without Steps 1-2. This is breathwork without a target — it may produce transient autonomic calming, but it leaves the specific grief charge uncontacted. The somatic location is the key that distinguishes this protocol from generic breath intervention.

**Expecting resolution in one cycle.** Grief loops across months, not minutes. The failure mode is expecting relief after one protocol execution and abandoning the protocol when it doesn't arrive. The experimental evidence from Yoshiike and colleagues (2025) suggests that the direction of movement — toward contact rather than away from it — is the variable, not the speed.

**Confusing grief with appropriate clinical vigilance.** Not every charged state in the OR is grief requiring completion. A clinician whose RMSSD is low because of a high-acuity case is not necessarily carrying unprocessed grief. The prerequisite skill is interoceptive literacy sufficient to distinguish between state-appropriate arousal and suppressed emotional load. These feel different. The protocol is for the latter.

## The Test

Fourteen CRNAs in a department with documented exposure to patient mortality. Wearable HRV monitoring across a 14-day observation period. Daily grief event notation: patient death, significant adverse event, or near-miss, timestamped. Gap moment claims logged via app button press, with protocol step completion noted.

**Hypothesis:** CRNAs who claim Constraint-Release Grief Loop protocols after documented grief events will show attenuated RMSSD degradation at 24 and 48 hours post-event compared to CRNAs who continue without the protocol. Effect size target: ≥8% RMSSD difference at the 48-hour post-event mark.

Secondary measure: self-reported intrusive thoughts using a modified Grief Intrusion scale administered at morning check-in. Hypothesis: lower intrusion scores in the protocol group at 72 hours.

This is not a clinical trial. It is a minimum viable proof-of-concept: 14 CRNAs, 14 days, one wearable device per clinician, one app button.

## The Connection

The Constraint-Release Grief Loop is where gap-moment-training meets the second-victim phenomenon. Gap Moment Training provides the container and the format (60 seconds, claimed window, single protocol). The second victim literature provides the clinical context — the scope of the problem, the absence of institutional response, the link to burnout and career attrition.

The Gap Moment, in this application, is not a wellness optimization tool. It is the only available window for physiological completion of a cycle the institution has no other provision for. This is SRL's operating principle: the minimum effective dose, applied to a problem the system pretends does not exist.

The autonomic evidence — Yoshiike and colleagues' demonstration that avoidance attenuates vagal tone — converts what might otherwise appear to be a philosophical position about grief into a measurable physiological prediction. The research suggests that contact, not suppression, is the autonomic move. The protocol structures contact for the specific environment — high stakes, short windows, institutional prohibition — where contact has historically been impossible.

---

*The body does not wait for the appropriate moment. It carries. The fifteen minutes between cases are already happening. The question is only whether anything useful happens in them.*
