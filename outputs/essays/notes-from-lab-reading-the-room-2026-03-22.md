# Notes from the Lab: Reading the Room

*An experienced clinician may estimate the autonomic states of others in the OR through at least five sensory channels — and the accuracy of this estimation likely correlates with interoceptive training.*

## The Observation

Somewhere around year fifteen, I started playing a game with myself in the OR.

I would look at the patient's heart rate on the monitor. Then I would try to guess my own. Not by taking my pulse — by feeling it. By noticing the throb in my fingertips against the laryngoscope, the rhythm in my chest, the tempo of something I couldn't quite name.

Then I got curious about the surgeon.

Could I estimate the surgeon's heart rate without touching them? Could I read the scrub nurse? The circulator? Not the number — the state. Amped or settled. Sympathetically charged or vagally anchored. Running hot or running cool.

I started noticing that I could. Not with precision. Not to the beat. But directionally — I could sense when the surgeon's autonomic state shifted before their behavior changed. Before the voice got tighter. Before the movements got faster. Something in the room told me, and I didn't know which sense was doing the telling.

## The Mechanism

There are at least five channels through which this could work. Each has evidence. None is sufficient alone. The interesting question is whether they operate as a stack.

### Channel 1: Visible Carotid Pulsation

The simplest explanation. The carotid artery runs superficially in the neck. In thin-skinned or stressed individuals, the pulse is visible — a rhythmic distension that an observant clinician could detect peripherally.

CRNAs are trained to assess carotid pulsation in patients. That same perceptual skill, directed laterally toward a colleague's neck, could provide a rough heart rate estimate. Under stress (elevated HR, increased stroke volume), the carotid pulse becomes more prominent. The signal gets louder when it matters most.

**Confound:** This works only when you're looking at the person's neck, in adequate lighting, and they have visible anatomy. In a full surgical cap and mask, you might see only the temporal artery above the ear. Limited channel, but real.

### Channel 2: Mirror Neuron Activation

Giacomo Rizzolatti's discovery of mirror neurons demonstrated that observing another person's actions activates corresponding motor representations in the observer's brain. Jean Decety and Philip Jackson extended this to emotional states: observing someone in pain activates the observer's insula and anterior cingulate cortex — the same regions involved in processing one's own pain.

The implication for the OR: observing a surgeon under stress may activate sympathetic arousal in the CRNA — not as empathy in the emotional sense, but as autonomic mirroring. Your heart rate nudges up because theirs did. Not because you're scared. Because your mirror system coupled.

**Confound:** Mirror neuron activation is a response, not a measurement. You feel something shift in your own body, but you're reading YOUR mirror response, not their actual state. The signal is indirect. The accuracy depends on the fidelity of the mirroring — which varies between individuals and may be trainable.

### Channel 3: Respiratory Entrainment

People in shared spaces unconsciously synchronize breathing rates. This is well-documented in dyadic interactions — therapist-client, mother-infant, romantic partners — and has been observed in small groups.

In an OR, the ambient rhythm of the ventilator provides one respiratory anchor (the patient's mechanical breath rate). But the human occupants of the room may develop their own unconscious synchrony, particularly during sustained focus on a shared task. If breathing rates synchronize, cardiac rhythms partially follow — because respiratory sinus arrhythmia links breath cycle to heart rate oscillation.

Stephen Porges' work on the social engagement system suggests this synchrony is mediated by the ventral vagal complex — the same branch of the parasympathetic system that supports calm, coordinated social behavior. When the team is functioning well, their autonomic systems may literally be oscillating together.

**Confound:** Respiratory entrainment produces synchrony, not measurement. You can't extract another person's heart rate from shared breathing rhythm. But you CAN detect when the synchrony breaks — when one person's breathing suddenly desynchronizes from the group. That break IS the signal. The room felt different because someone's rhythm left the ensemble.

### Channel 4: Cardiac Electromagnetic Field Coupling

Rollin McCraty and the HeartMath Institute published research demonstrating that the heart's electromagnetic field is detectable in another person's EEG when they are in close proximity. The heart generates an electrical field measurable by ECG and a magnetic field measurable by magnetocardiography. This field extends beyond the body.

McCraty's finding: when two people sit near each other, one person's cardiac signal appears in the other person's brainwave recording — particularly when the two individuals are in a state of emotional coherence. The signal is faint. It is real. It has been replicated.

**Confound:** This is the most provocative channel and the least established. The signal is detectable by sensitive instruments but the question of whether the human brain consciously or unconsciously processes this electromagnetic input remains open. McCraty's work is peer-reviewed but not universally accepted. The mechanism — how the brain would transduce a weak magnetic field into a perceptual signal — is unresolved. I state the evidence and the gap.

### Channel 5: Interoceptive Self-Referencing

This is the channel I think is actually doing most of the work.

You don't read the surgeon's heart rate. You read YOUR OWN heart rate — and you notice that it shifted when the surgeon's state changed. Your body is the antenna. The room's collective autonomic state produces micro-perturbations in your own physiology — through mirror neurons, through respiratory entrainment, through visual cues, through channels you can't name. Your interoceptive system detects those perturbations and reports them as a felt shift: *something changed*.

A clinician with high interoceptive accuracy (Garfinkel's dimension 1) can detect that shift precisely. A clinician with high interoceptive awareness (Garfinkel's dimension 3) can correctly attribute it — "that shift isn't mine, it's the room's." A clinician with high MAIA-2 Body Listening scores actively monitors for these shifts as a source of clinical intelligence.

This is not empathy. It is **somatic inference** — using your own autonomic perturbation as a proxy measurement of the environment's autonomic state.

**Confound:** The accuracy of this channel depends entirely on the clinician's interoceptive calibration. A clinician who can't feel their own heart rate can't use their heart rate as an instrument for reading the room. Diaphragmatic blindness blocks this channel. Interoceptive training opens it.

## The Protocol

**Interpersonal Autonomic Reading Training (IART)**

This is speculative. Untested. But here's the minimum version:

**Step 1:** Establish your own interoceptive baseline. Can you estimate your heart rate within 5 bpm without checking? Practice until you can. (This IS Anterocept Domain 1.)

**Step 2:** In a low-stakes environment (break room, handoff), silently estimate a colleague's autonomic state: amped or settled? Don't check. Just notice what your body tells you.

**Step 3:** If possible, verify. Ask: "How are you doing — honestly?" Or observe their behavior over the next few minutes. Did your estimate match?

**Step 4:** Track accuracy over weeks. Does it improve with interoceptive training? Does it correlate with your MAIA-2 Body Listening score?

**Duration:** Ongoing. Not a protocol with an endpoint — a perceptual skill that sharpens with practice. Like auscultation. Like airway assessment by feel. A clinical skill trained in the body.

## The Failure Mode

**Projection.** You feel anxious and attribute it to the surgeon. Your own state contaminates the reading. The signal-to-noise ratio between "my perturbation" and "the room's perturbation" is the hardest discrimination to make. Without strong interoceptive calibration, you are projecting, not perceiving.

**Overconfidence.** Believing you can read the room when you can't. Making clinical decisions based on perceived autonomic reads that are actually your own unprocessed state. This is the Garfinkel gap: high sensibility ("I'm good at reading people"), low accuracy (you're not, you're reading yourself).

## The Test

**Hypothesis:** Clinicians with higher MAIA-2 scores estimate colleagues' autonomic states more accurately than clinicians with lower MAIA-2 scores.

**Test:** 20 CRNAs. Administer MAIA-2. Place each in a simulated OR with a confederate whose autonomic state is monitored by concealed wearable. At random intervals, the confederate undergoes a stress induction (mental arithmetic, cold pressor) or relaxation protocol. The CRNA reports when they sense a state change. Compare detection accuracy with MAIA-2 scores.

**What this tests:** Whether "reading the room" is a trainable interoceptive skill or an unverifiable folk belief.

If it correlates: interoceptive training IS team awareness training. The coherence field concept is validated at the perceptual level.

If it doesn't: the perception is noise, and we stop claiming it.

## The Connection

This is the bridge between individual regulation (what Pausality trains) and collective coherence (what Peter Merry describes as the field). The mechanism is: train your own interoception → your body becomes an instrument for reading the room → the room becomes a sensible field.

Martha Rogers called it energy field perception. Porges called it neuroception. Merry calls it field sensitivity. This essay calls it somatic inference — using your own autonomic perturbation as a proxy for the environment's state.

It only works if you can feel your own heart rate first. Everything starts with the diaphragm.
