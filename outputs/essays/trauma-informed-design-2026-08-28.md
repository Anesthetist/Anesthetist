# Notes from the Lab: The Risk Inside the Remedy

*The systematic interoceptive exposure that somnistics prescribes is also its primary risk vector — breathing practices can reactivate somatic trauma in CRNAs who carry layered occupational trauma, and trauma-informed design is not a sensitivity layer but the engineering specification that determines whether training integrates or destabilizes.*

## The Observation

She had been a CRNA for fourteen years. She could read a capnography trace at a glance, manage a difficult airway in under ninety seconds, and hold a full patient picture across six simultaneous monitors without losing her thread. She could manage everyone else's physiology with precision.

She could not feel her own breath.

Not unusual. In Week 1 of the somnistics curriculum, this is the most common finding: clinicians who monitor patients' respiratory patterns with expert precision cannot accurately sense their own diaphragmatic movement. Diaphragmatic blindness — the specific inability to detect whether one is breathing diaphragmatically or thoracically — is the starting deficit that [[anterocept]] training addresses.

But when the same clinician tried a guided breathing exercise during a break — just five minutes, the classic "take three deep breaths" instruction — she stopped abruptly. Chest tightening. Slight dissociation. "I don't know why, it just felt wrong." She did not try again.

The exercise meant to bring her into her body activated the system she had spent years keeping quiet. This is not a failure of compliance. It is a failure of design.

## The Mechanism

CRNAs enter interoceptive training carrying three layers of somatic load, none of which the standard wellness-app interaction model accounts for.

**Layer 1: Trained suppression.** Medical and nursing education systems, explicitly and implicitly, reward clinicians for overriding somatic distress signals during patient care. Hunger, fatigue, bladder urgency, fear — the training environment systematically teaches clinicians to suppress these signals in service of task performance. The interoceptive suppression hypothesis (documented in SRL's prior research) describes this as adaptive in the short term and pathogenic in the long term. The clinician who learned to disappear from their body during training is not malingering when they cannot feel their breath. They learned that skill.

**Layer 2: Cumulative clinical trauma.** Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, Mitzman, Jones, McNamara, Stobart-Gallagher, and King (2019) [PMID: 31106086; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.4186)] curated the foundational literature on second victim syndrome — the psychological and emotional suffering of healthcare workers following adverse patient events, near-misses, or unanticipated deaths. The literature makes clear that SVS "can result from a much wider range of circumstances" than outright medical error, and that learners and residents are particularly susceptible. CRNAs manage high-acuity patients across entire careers; exposure to adverse events is near-certain over a fourteen-year arc.

Also according to PubMed, Munn, Liu, Swick, Rose, Broyhill, New, and Gibbs (2021) [PMID: 34255750; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.NAJ.0000767352.47699.0c)] studied 2,459 healthcare workers during COVID-19, including certified registered nurse anesthetists specifically. Lower resilience and lower psychological safety significantly predicted at-risk well-being. This is the baseline that interoceptive training enters — not a rested, untroubled nervous system primed for exploration, but one already carrying years of unprocessed load.

**Layer 3: The body's own alarm architecture.** Laurin, Bottemanne, Bulteau, El Haj, Sauvaget, and Deschamps (2026) [PMID: 41485717; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106546)] provide the mechanism in precise computational terms. Using a predictive processing framework, they show that in non-dissociative PTSD, interoceptive signals are hyper-weighted toward threat: the brain's prior expectations of danger amplify body signals rather than calibrate them, producing the hierarchy Prior > Interoception > Exteroception. In dissociative PTSD, interoception goes offline entirely. Either configuration means that directing attention INTO the body, when the body has been a source of threat, does not produce relaxation. It amplifies alarm or produces shutdown.

This is the physiological reason "take a deep breath" can activate a trauma response. The instruction is technically correct. The design is wrong.

Based on articles retrieved from PubMed, Goldberg, Lam, Britton, and Davidson (2021) [PMID: 34074221; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10503307.2021.1933646)] provide the population-level confirmation. In a US community sample of meditators, 32.3% reported at least one meditation-related adverse effect, with anxiety, traumatic re-experiencing, and emotional sensitivity as the most common. Childhood adversity was specifically associated with elevated risk. Among practitioners with occupational trauma histories — which CRNAs represent — this is not an edge case. It is the median condition.

## The Framework

The seven principles of trauma-informed somnistics design are not a sensitivity layer applied after the fact. They are the engineering specification.

**Invitation, never command.** The linguistic distinction matters at the physiological level. "Close your eyes" is a directive that removes visual safety cues and activates threat detection: when we cannot see the environment, neuroception of safety drops (see [[polyvagal-theory]]). "If comfortable, allow your eyes to soften" preserves agency. Agency is not a courtesy — it is the physiological condition for ventral vagal engagement, which is the target state.

**Multiple anchors, always.** The [[polyanchora]] architecture is not a curriculum preference; it is a trauma safety mechanism. When one anchor (breath) activates alarm, another (feet on floor, ambient sound, hands on thighs) provides an exit ramp. A single-anchor breathing protocol is not just less effective for trauma populations — it is more dangerous. The absence of options is itself the safety failure.

**Titrate intensity.** The [[anterocept]] domain progression — Domain 1 (peripheral, grounding), Domain 2 (breath rhythm, heartbeat), Domain 3 (integrated internal-external) — is a structured graduated exposure protocol. Price, Spinazzola, Emerson, van der Kolk, and colleagues (2017) [PMID: 28121466; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/acm.2015.0266)] demonstrated exactly this with trauma-sensitive yoga: 20 weeks of titrated, choice-based, embodied practice produced significant reductions in PTSD and dissociative symptomatology in women with chronic, treatment-resistant PTSD. The graduated approach is not slow — it is what makes depth eventually safe.

**Name sensation, not emotion.** "Tightness in my chest" is physiologically contained and external to identity. "Anxious" is identity-level, can trigger shame, and may overwhelm the system. For trauma survivors — which a meaningful fraction of any CRNA cohort represents — sensation labeling maintains the window of tolerance (Siegel and Ogden's framework: the zone between sympathetic hyperarousal and dorsal vagal hypoarousal) while building interoceptive vocabulary without activating self-concept threat.

**Exit ramps in every session.** Grounding is taught before depth — always. The ground (feet on floor, hands on solid surface, ambient sound) is always available. The [[neurominute]] 60-second format is, structurally, a window-of-tolerance container: brief enough to prevent overwhelm, long enough to generate a measurable autonomic response.

**Normalize difficulty.** Trauma survivors expect to fail at body-based practices because the body has been a source of distress. "If your attention drifts, that IS the practice — noticing is the skill" eliminates the shame loop that causes abandonment. There is no wrong experience in this practice.

**Understand that the body remembers.** Van der Kolk's central contribution — that trauma is stored somatically, not just cognitively — means that interoceptive training will surface stored somatic experiences in some practitioners. This is not a bug. It is the mechanism. Without a therapist present, the design must ensure surfacing stays within the window of tolerance: short sessions, structured progression, choice-based depth, explicit permission to stop.

## The Failure Mode

Trauma-informed design fails whenever any of its seven specifications is treated as optional.

The most common failure is linguistic: a trainer defaults to directive language — "close your eyes," "take a deep breath," "now feel your heartbeat" — because it sounds more authoritative. The practitioner cannot name what happens next. Just a diffuse sense of wrongness, a closed session, and no return.

The second failure is speed: a practitioner or app pushes into Domain 2 interoceptive work before Domain 1 is stable. The body has not been shown it can be trusted with its own signals. The depth activates alarm rather than curiosity.

The third failure is single-anchor design. An app that instructs "focus only on your breath" has no exit ramp for the practitioner whose breath carries threat associations. The literal absence of options is the safety failure.

A subtler failure, and the most dangerous: assuming the CRNA population is a standard wellness population. The literature does not support this assumption. Mitzman et al.'s second victim syndrome review, Munn et al.'s pandemic data, and Britton's adverse effects research converge on the same finding — the clinical population SRL serves carries trauma load that ordinary wellness app design has never been required to account for.

The paradox is sharp: the tools most needed by the most traumatized clinicians are also most likely to cause harm if the design does not know what it is entering.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: N=20 CRNAs, recruited from two hospital systems, screened with PTSD-PCL-5 at baseline. Randomized to: (A) standard breathing app protocol or (B) trauma-informed protocol with full seven-principle implementation.

Primary outcomes at 12 weeks: discontinuation rate; adverse experience frequency (using Goldberg et al.'s meditation-related adverse effects scale). Secondary outcomes: interoceptive accuracy (heartbeat detection task), resting HRV, PTSD-PCL-5 at 90 days.

Hypothesis: Group B shows lower discontinuation and lower adverse experience rates, with equivalent or superior autonomic outcomes. Baseline PTSD-PCL-5 score predicts adverse experience in Group A but not Group B.

Untestable at smaller scale: the within-subject effect size for trauma-related adverse experiences requires adequate statistical power. A 10-person pilot can establish protocol fidelity and detect gross differences. Full confirmation requires N=20 minimum.

## The Connection

This essay connects to [[anterocept]] as the graduated exposure architecture; to [[neurominute]] as a built-in window-of-tolerance container; to [[polyvagal-theory]] as the neuroceptive mechanism underlying safety; to [[co-regulation]] as the regulated instructor presence that creates safety signal; and to [[interoceptive-suppression-hypothesis]] as the specific somatic damage this training must navigate before it can build.

The vault insight: trauma-informed design is not about being gentle. It is about being accurate. The CRNA population has an interoceptive baseline systematically altered by training, cumulative exposure, and adverse events. A training that ignores this is not merely ineffective — it introduces risk into the body that came to heal.

*She tried again six weeks later — feet on the floor this time, eyes soft, one breath at a time — and felt, for the first time in years, the subtle lift of her own diaphragm. She didn't know why she was crying. She did know she would be back tomorrow.*
