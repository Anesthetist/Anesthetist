# Notes from the Lab: The Skill That Has to Come First

*Diaphragmatic literacy is not a technique — it is a precondition: the trained four-skill capacity to perceive, distinguish, control, and maintain diaphragmatic breathing that every downstream autonomic protocol requires, and whose absence is the most common and least-named entry-level deficit in high-performing clinicians.*

## The Observation

The finding is consistent. A CRNA in week one of the Somnistics curriculum can adjust a ventilator by feel, read a capnography waveform before looking at the number, and catch a subtle change in respiratory compliance before the monitor alarms. The same clinician, asked to simply feel their own diaphragm move, goes quiet.

Not metaphorically quiet — physiologically blank. The introspective channel to their own primary breathing muscle is not undertrained; in many cases it has been actively suppressed by years of externalized clinical attention. The paradox is that mastery of the patient's respiratory physiology has deepened the disconnection from their own.

This is [[diaphragmatic-blindness]], and it is the starting condition for nearly every clinician who begins Somnistics training. Correcting it requires something specific and prior: diaphragmatic literacy, the trained capacity to perceive and work with the breath that high-performance practice has rendered invisible.

## The Mechanism

The diaphragm is not just the body's primary inspiratory muscle. Russo, Santarelli, and O'Rourke (2017) mapped its role in the autonomic system with precision: diaphragmatic activation at slow breathing rates (six breaths per minute or below) substantially amplifies respiratory sinus arrhythmia, increases baroreflex sensitivity, and drives cardiorespiratory coupling — the coordinated entrainment of heart rate to breath that underlies the HRV coherence state. The diaphragm's mechanical excursion directly stretches vagal afferents and activates baroreceptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinus. It is not merely delivering oxygen; it is the primary actuator of beat-to-beat parasympathetic modulation [PMID: 29209423, DOI: 10.1183/20734735.009817].

When the diaphragm is functionally absent — replaced by thoracic and accessory muscle breathing — this cascade breaks. Boulding, Stacey, Niven, and Fowler (2016) classified the resulting patterns into five clinically distinct dysfunctional breathing phenotypes: hyperventilation syndrome, periodic deep sighing, thoracic dominant breathing, forced abdominal expiration, and thoraco-abdominal asynchrony. Thoracic dominant breathing — the pattern most prevalent in sustained cognitive workload — shifts respiratory mechanics upward, recruits the scalenes and sternocleidomastoid, and reduces the tidal volume available for baroreflex-mediated heart rate modulation [PMID: 27581828, DOI: 10.1183/16000617.0088-2015].

The downstream consequence is measurable. Courtney, Cohen, and van Dixhoorn (2011) showed that dysfunctional breathing patterns — specifically thoracic dominance — directly impair the ability to achieve HRV coherence even when the biofeedback protocol is correctly applied. In their study of 83 people assessed before beginning HRV biofeedback, thoracic-dominant breathing was significantly associated with decreased coherence scores (r = −.463, p = .0001), and participants with paradoxical breathing showed the lowest coherence of all. The protocol was not wrong. The prerequisite skill was absent [PMID: 22164811].

This is why diaphragmatic literacy precedes every other [[anterocept]] intervention. Biofeedback, resonant breathing training, and cardiac-anchored breathing protocols all assume the user can activate the diaphragm deliberately and feel it doing so. Without that baseline, the protocols reduce to mechanical behaviors with no interoceptive feedback loop.

Bordoni and Escher (2025) further clarify the anatomical stakes: the diaphragm has fascial continuity with the pericardium, the transversus abdominis, the psoas complex, and the thoracic spine. When it fails to generate its proper excursion, the entire accessory respiratory architecture compensates — shifting work upward to the neck and upper back. Clinicians who finish a twelve-hour case with thoracic tension they attribute to posture are often observing the end state of a breathing pattern that was suppressed at the diaphragm hours earlier [PMID: 40091907, DOI: 10.7759/cureus.80599].

The interoceptive gap is not merely inconvenient. It is a circuit break in the system that makes autonomic self-regulation possible.

## The Protocol

Diaphragmatic literacy is built as four trainable skills, in sequence. Each requires the prior one to be functional.

**Skill 1: Perceive.** The ability to detect diaphragmatic movement in real time — to feel the belly-first expansion and contraction that distinguishes diaphragmatic from thoracic breath. Starting position: supine or seated, dominant hand on the upper abdomen just below the xiphoid process. Three cue breaths. The training question is not "are you breathing correctly?" but "what do you feel?"

**Skill 2: Distinguish.** The ability to discriminate reliably between chest-dominant and diaphragm-dominant breathing under varying conditions — rest, mild cognitive load, and task. This is the perceptual calibration step. A clinician who can perceive their breath at rest but loses the signal under cognitive load has Skill 1, not Skill 2.

**Skill 3: Control.** The ability to select and maintain a specific breath depth, rate, and pattern through deliberate diaphragmatic engagement. This is where [[resonant-breathing-frequency]] training begins — only once the clinician can feel what the diaphragm is doing can they begin to consciously shape it.

**Skill 4: Maintain.** The ability to sustain diaphragmatic breathing patterns under stress without active conscious monitoring — the shift from effortful to automatic. This is the clinical-use tier. A CRNA in a deteriorating case cannot dedicate working memory to breath mechanics. The pattern must be installed at a level that survives cognitive load.

Brown and Gerbarg (2005) articulated the underlying reason this skill sequence matters in their neurophysiologic model of yogic breathing: pranayama traditions explicitly train breath awareness before breath technique precisely because perception is the precondition for control. A technique applied without interoceptive feedback is a behavior pattern, not a regulated skill. The traditions discovered this through practice over centuries; the neuroscience now explains why [PMID: 15750381, DOI: 10.1089/acm.2005.11.189].

Two additional tiers extend beyond the clinical use case. A fifth skill — **Recover** — addresses diaphragmatic rehabilitation after inhibition through pain-guarding, surgery, or chronic dysfunction: the capacity to restore functional breathing mechanics after the pattern has been structurally disrupted. A mastery tier — **Teach** — is the ability to transmit the four skills to others, adapted to context: the clinical instructor, the wellness coach, the CRNA preceptor who can now onboard new colleagues into a skill set they carry forward.

## The Failure Mode

The four-skill model assumes a stable acquisition environment. It breaks under two conditions.

The first is acute stress loading before Skill 4 is automated. Under high cognitive demand, proprioceptive attention narrows. The interoceptive channel that Skill 1 opens can close again in conditions exceeding the training load. This is not a protocol failure — it is a dosing error. Skills built at rest must be retrained under progressively higher load. The [[gap-moment-training]] architecture addresses this explicitly: each gap moment is a brief re-embodiment exercise embedded in real workflow, building Skill 4 in the actual load context rather than expecting rest-state training to transfer automatically.

The second failure mode is treating the four skills as a single acquired competency. Clinicians who can demonstrate Skill 3 (control) in a training session sometimes lose Skill 1 (perception) under sustained stress — the automatic thoracic compensation pattern resurfaces without awareness. The skills are not a single threshold crossed once; they are a four-channel system that requires periodic recalibration. The [[somnistics-readiness-battery]] SRB-60 tests all four skills at intake and reassessment precisely because regression is predictable and measurable.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: 14-day diaphragmatic literacy calibration.

- N = 10 practicing clinicians (CRNA or critical care RN)
- Pre-assessment: MAIA-2 Noticing subscale + video breath-pattern assessment (thoracic vs. diaphragmatic dominant, two-minute baseline)
- Intervention: 3-minute daily haptic biofeedback session (hand-on-abdomen + Pausality breath tracking, morning)
- Post-assessment Day 14: MAIA-2 Noticing subscale + dual-task breath discrimination (auditory Stroop task + simultaneous breath-pattern assessment)
- Primary outcome: Percent correct diaphragmatic vs. thoracic discrimination under dual-task condition
- Success criterion: >80% correct discrimination under dual-task by Day 14 for ≥7 of 10 participants

The test is deliberately small-scale. It is not designed to prove efficacy — it is designed to answer a narrower question: does a three-minute daily perception-based practice shift the MAIA-2 Noticing subscale and dual-task breath discrimination in a direction consistent with Skill 1 and Skill 2 acquisition? If it does, the next test escalates the load.

The SRB-60 already provides the assessment infrastructure. The gap moment log already provides the daily practice record. The test runs on existing tooling.

## The Connection

[[Diaphragmatic blindness]] is the named deficit. Diaphragmatic literacy is its named resolution — a structured four-skill acquisition sequence, now on the map.

Every downstream concept in the Somnistics vault assumes this baseline is installed: [[anterocept]], [[resonant-breathing-frequency]], [[cardiac-anchored-breathing]], [[gap-moment-training]], [[neurominute]] protocols. The curriculum has been building on a foundation it has not explicitly named or assessed at the first-session level. Naming the four skills, sequencing them, and measuring them at intake does not add complexity to the curriculum — it completes the foundation that was always assumed.

---

*A CRNA who can feel their diaphragm move — really feel it, under load, mid-case, in the thirty seconds before the next decision — has not learned a technique. They have recovered a sense that clinical training took away.*
