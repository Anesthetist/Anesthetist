# Notes from the Lab: The Vigilance You Can't Sustain

*Hypervigilance and sustainable clinical vigilance are physiologically distinct autonomic states — the first carries a measurable HRV signature that degrades performance and predicts burnout, the second is a vagally-anchored sustained attention that endures across a career — and training the capacity to inhabit the second is the physiological difference between James Carse's finite player and the infinite one.*

---

## The Observation

Hour six of a ten-hour day. Three cases in, one running now. The monitor still reads the same values. The hands still move with precision. Ask the clinician and they will tell you they are just as vigilant as they were at 7 a.m.

The heart disagrees.

Ambulatory HRV data collected during surgical cases by Amirian and colleagues shows what the clinician cannot feel: their LF/HF ratio has nearly doubled over a shift, from 2.7 to 4.9. Their resting pulse has climbed seventeen beats per minute. Their heart rate variability — the beat-to-beat flexibility that reflects parasympathetic reserve — has declined significantly. The autonomic nervous system is running a finite game: maximum output, narrowing aperture, declining reserve. Subjectively, the clinician calls it vigilance. Physiologically, it is something else.

Vigilance is not one thing. This essay argues that the clinical tradition of treating vigilance as a single intensity dial — turn it up when the case gets hard, maintain it as fatigue sets in — systematically misidentifies a depleting sympathetic state as the very resource it is consuming. The frame that corrects this is one that James Carse named in 1986, not in a clinical context, but in a philosophical one: the difference between finite games, played to win within fixed rules, and infinite games, played to keep the playing going.

The CRNA who survives twenty-eight years in practice is not the one with the best threat-scan. They are the one who learned — consciously or not — to inhabit the second kind of vigilance.

---

## The Mechanism

According to PubMed, research on autonomic physiology and clinical performance reveals a problem that the word "vigilance" conceals: the physiological states it names are not equivalent.

**State One: Finite-Game Vigilance.** Sympathetically dominant, characterized by elevated heart rate, reduced heart rate variability, narrow attentional focus, and phasic threat-scan arousal. This is the classical hypervigilance response — the body treating every clinical event as a potential emergency requiring full mobilization. Amirian et al. (2014) measured this directly in surgical clinicians: 29 surgeons wore ambulatory ECG monitors for 48 consecutive hours spanning a precall day and a 17-hour on-call night shift. On-call, their median pulse rose from 64 to 81 bpm, their high-frequency HRV (parasympathetic marker) dropped from 199 to 99 ms², and their LF/HF ratio rose from 2.7 to 4.9 — indicating a pronounced shift toward sympathetic dominance [PMID: 25265102, DOI: 10.1503/cjs.028813]. The body was running a finite game: total expenditure, diminishing reserve.

Kane and colleagues (2025) pooled this kind of data across seven studies of doctors wearing continuous HRV monitors, producing the first meta-analysis of its kind. They found a statistically significant difference between HRV during stress and recovery periods across all clinician samples — RMSSD was substantially lower during clinical work (standardized mean difference = -0.63, p=0.005), with similar patterns in low-frequency and LF/HF ratio measures [PMID: 41157926, DOI: 10.1093/occmed/kqaf101]. The trajectory matters: clinicians whose daytime stress periods show the deepest RMSSD suppression without adequate nighttime recovery are the ones whose autonomic baseline shifts downward over months and years. This is the burnout vector.

Wright et al. (2022) connected sympathetic dominance directly to attentional impairment in cognitively demanding contexts. In 163 participants completing sustained attention tasks under chronic academic stress, they found that heart rate variability moderated the relationship between stress and attentional performance — specifically, higher sympathetic dominance during cognitive testing was associated with slower reaction times in bottom-up (reflexive) attention [PMID: 35178679, DOI: 10.3758/s13414-022-02454-x]. The authors noted gender-moderating effects on different attentional subtypes, which is a confound: these were students, not clinicians, and the testing conditions differed from an operating room. I am isolating one signal: sympathetic dominance impairs the attentional processes clinicians rely on, and this appears in HRV data before the clinician reports it subjectively.

**State Two: Infinite-Game Vigilance.** Vagally-anchored, characterized by higher RMSSD, broader attentional aperture, tonic alertness rather than phasic threat-response. The person in this state is not less attentive — they are attending in a fundamentally different mode. They are not scanning for threats; they are holding an open, responsive field of awareness. In Carse's language: they are playing to keep the game going, not to win this case.

O'Connell and colleagues (2008) demonstrated that this second mode is trainable. In Self-Alert Training (SAT), participants combined a validated behavioral self-alerting technique with an autonomic arousal biofeedback protocol, learning to volitionally modulate their own arousal levels. On the Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART), SAT participants showed improved accuracy. The critical control finding: participants in the placebo condition showed "a gradual reduction in arousal over time and increased reaction time variability indicative of a vigilance decrement" — exactly the finite-game trajectory [PMID: 18249419, DOI: 10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2007.12.018]. The SAT participants had learned something the control group had not: the capacity to sustain attention through volitional autonomic modulation rather than sympathetic expenditure.

This is the mechanism. Finite-game vigilance depletes parasympathetic reserve while narrowing attentional aperture. Infinite-game vigilance maintains vagal flexibility while preserving the broad attentional field that catches what phasic threat-scan misses. The two states are not a spectrum of the same thing. They are different physiological games, with different outcomes over a career.

**Confounds to state explicitly:** The Amirian data comes from surgical clinicians during night shifts, which conflates clinical workload with circadian disruption and sleep loss. The Wright data is in students, not clinicians. O'Connell's SAT training was brief and laboratory-based, not tested in clinical settings. The mechanism I am proposing — that the vigilance distinction holds across a career — is plausible from this evidence but not directly demonstrated in CRNAs at this time.

## The Protocol

For clinicians, not as prescription but as structure:

**The Inter-Case Reset (Infinite-Game Vigilance Maintenance)**

1. At every case transition — between induction and maintenance, between cases, at meal breaks — pause for 60-90 seconds.
2. Check the autonomic signal: pulse awareness, breath quality, jaw tension, shoulder position. Not to judge, but to read.
3. If sympathetically elevated: three to five resonant-frequency breath cycles (approximately 5.5 breaths/min, or one's personal resonant frequency). The goal is not calm — it is autonomic flexibility.
4. Return to the case from the restored state, not the depleted one.
5. End-of-shift autonomic discharge: HRV measurement, 10-minute resonant breathing or body scan, tracking of the day's autonomic trajectory.

The frame underlying the protocol: a case does not require hypervigilance to be done well. It requires sustained attentive presence. These are different asks. The finite game demands maximum vigilance at maximum cost. The infinite game demands just enough arousal to maintain optimal function — what performance science calls the Yerkes-Dodson optimal zone — while preserving the reserve to play again tomorrow.

---

## The Failure Mode

The frame fails in three specific conditions.

**First: Genuine acute emergencies.** When a patient is actively decompensating — anaphylaxis, can't intubate can't oxygenate, malignant hyperthermia — finite-game vigilance is adaptive and appropriate. The problem is not the sympathetic activation; the problem is treating a routine eight-hour case as if it were ten consecutive anaphylaxes. The frame is about distinguishing the appropriate grain of arousal from the inappropriate baseline of hypervigilance.

**Second: Sleep deprivation.** Below a threshold of roughly six hours of sleep, the autonomic system lacks the substrate for infinite-game vigilance. Kane et al.'s meta-analysis notes that nighttime recovery windows (high nocturnal RMSSD) are the biological mechanism by which the autonomic reserve resets. Sleep debt eliminates this window. The protocol above is ineffective in a sleep-deprived clinician; the intervention needed is upstream.

**Third: Post-adverse-event dissociation.** After a second victim event, some clinicians develop a form of functional detachment that superficially resembles infinite-game vigilance — calm, broad, unhurried — but is physiologically dissociation, not vagal flexibility. The difference is measurable: dissociation is associated with HRV suppression across the board, not preserved RMSSD. A clinician experiencing post-event traumatic stress is not accessing sustainable presence; they need the constraint-release grief loop, not the vigilance reframe.

## The Test

**Minimum viable experiment:** 10 CRNAs in a single practice site. Wearable HRV device (Apple Watch, Garmin, or research-grade ambulatory ECG) for four weeks continuous.

**Baseline (weeks 1-2):** Passive monitoring. Record RMSSD during cases, RMSSD recovery rate (time to return to resting baseline post-shift), subjective vigilance self-report at end of each case, error self-report.

**Intervention (weeks 3-4):** Same monitoring. Add the Inter-Case Reset protocol above. No other changes. Clinicians are not told their HRV data in real time — they are told only the resonant frequency breathing target.

**Primary outcome:** Change in post-shift RMSSD recovery time (hours to restore resting baseline).

**Secondary outcomes:** Subjective vigilance consistency across a shift, self-reported error rate, end-of-day cortisol (salivary, if feasible).

**What constitutes failure:** No measurable difference in RMSSD recovery time. The protocol is then either inadequate, insufficiently dosed, or the mechanism does not transfer from laboratory to clinical context.

This test is not a clinical trial. It is a 14-day signal test with a wearable and a simple protocol. If the signal is present, it motivates the larger study.

## The Connection

This concept anchors the deepest question in SRL's clinical design: what is it that we are actually training? If the answer is "vigilance," we risk training the finite-game version — more intensity, more threat-scan, more expenditure — when what the clinician needs is the infinite-game version: attentive presence that compounds across cases and careers.

The [[infinite-game-vigilance-frame]] connects directly to [[clinician-durability]] (the autonomic substrate of career longevity), [[constraint-release-grief-loop]] (the failure mode when adverse events knock a clinician out of sustainable presence), [[stairway-meta-awareness-protocol]] (the five-step ascending practice that trains the broader attentional field), and [[gap-moment-training]] (the delivery mechanism for inter-case autonomic resets).

It also implicitly challenges how CRNA education frames vigilance. If students are trained to equate vigilance with sympathetic intensity, they graduate with a finite-game autonomic habit that the evidence suggests will degrade over a 28-year career. Teaching the distinction early may be the highest-leverage intervention in the SRL curriculum.
