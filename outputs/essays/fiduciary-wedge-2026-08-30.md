# Notes from the Lab: The Gap That Doesn't Close

*The fiduciary wedge — the structural accountability floor between AI technical competence and legal answerability — does not narrow as AI improves; it reorganizes, concentrating legal, ethical, and physiological exposure onto the clinician who remains, which means the CRNA in an AI-augmented OR carries a heavier accountability burden, not a lighter one.*

## The Observation

The propofol drip is on closed-loop. The bispectral index reads 47 — well within target. The machine learning algorithm, trained on hundreds of thousands of induction records, is doing precisely what it does: titrating infusion rate to real-time EEG signal, compensating for hemodynamic shifts, holding the patient at the edge of unconsciousness where it has been trained to hold them. Giri and colleagues, reviewing AI applications in anesthesia, document that closed-loop systems reduce intraoperative hypotension by up to 40% and demonstrate measurably greater accuracy in maintaining anesthetic depth than manual titration [PMID: 41095978].

The CRNA in the corner of the room is watching.

Not doing. Watching. The technical question — how much propofol, right now — has been delegated. The CRNA occupies a new role: auditor of a competent machine, monitoring outputs, verifying they match expectation, ready to intervene if the system errors.

Here is the question the algorithm cannot answer: if something goes wrong in this case, who is accountable?

Not who will fix it — the CRNA will fix it. But who is *answerable*? Who bears the legal, ethical, and professional weight of this patient's outcome? The algorithm cannot be deposed. The training dataset cannot hold a nursing license. The closed-loop system cannot appear before a licensing board.

The gap between what AI can technically do and who can legally answer for it has a name: the fiduciary wedge. And it does not narrow as AI capability increases.

## The Mechanism

The fiduciary wedge was described structurally in organizational theory before it surfaced as a clinical problem. The basic argument: even as AI absorbs the cognitive work of institutions, two costs remain irreducible — trust costs and accountability costs. The gap between AI technical competence and legal answerability is not a function of AI capability. It is a function of what accountability structurally *is*.

Accountability requires a subject — a person who can be held responsible in law, sanctioned by a licensing body, required to testify. Algorithms are not persons. Not because current AI is insufficiently capable, but because accountability is a relational structure between persons and institutions. That structure cannot be re-routed to a model, no matter how accurate the model becomes.

The legal literature has begun to map this gap. Shumway and Hartman, reviewing medical malpractice liability for large language model AI, note that while no US case has yet been adjudicated with LLM AI as a direct liability factor, existing precedent establishes clearly that "the algorithm decided" will never function as a complete defense [PMID: 38295300]. The clinician who relies on AI output retains full professional accountability for the clinical decision — regardless of whether the AI's recommendation was technically sound.

Giorgetti, Giorgetti, and Boscolo-Berto, examining AI as a clinical decision-maker under the EU AI Act, articulate the structural paradox with precision: clinical decision support systems function as "co-decision makers alongside physicians," yet under current regulatory frameworks cannot operate fully autonomously — physicians are assigned an "oversight role" [PMID: 40788001]. The authors question whether full liability is coherent when oversight consists of "reviewing outputs generated by the CDSS in a manner that leaves no real control in the hands of the physician." Their question names the structural problem: accountability without control — perhaps the most dangerous configuration in clinical practice.

Abràmoff, Tobey, and Char, analyzing the FDA's first authorization of an autonomous AI diagnostic system, derived evaluation rules for exactly this boundary. Their framework establishes that technical validation — even rigorous preregistered clinical trial validation — does not settle the accountability question [PMID: 32171769]. Autonomous AI can be demonstrated safe and efficacious. It cannot be made *liable*. The liability structure must be independently specified, and it always lands on a person.

In anesthesiology specifically, Duarte-Medrano and colleagues, reviewing hybrid AI-human models in the perioperative setting, describe the optimal configuration as one where AI "augments rather than replaces clinical judgment — combining computational efficiency with the irreplaceable contextual understanding and ethical reasoning of the anesthesiologist" [PMID: 41517028]. The word *irreplaceable* is not sentiment. It is a structural claim. The contextual understanding and ethical reasoning cannot be delegated because they are the substance of the fiduciary obligation — the irreducible core of what makes the clinician answerable.

There is a second mechanism inside the fiduciary wedge, less legal and more physiological: automation bias. Pan, Liu, and Liu, in their multilevel governance framework for AI integration in clinical settings, document that generative AI systems produce "increased verification burden on physicians" that can "divert attention from independent clinical reasoning" and contribute to "deskilling" — measurable atrophy in the cognitive capacities the fiduciary role requires [PMID: 42341297]. The paradox is precise: AI systems designed to reduce cognitive load create, through dependency, a progressive erosion of the independent judgment the clinician is still legally obligated to exercise. Six variables change simultaneously when AI enters a clinical workflow; this review isolates the deskilling mechanism as the one that poses the specific threat to the fiduciary role, while acknowledging that reduced cognitive load may carry real benefits in other clinical tasks. The fiduciary wedge does not shrink. The capacity to fill it may.

Croskerry's foundational review of cognitive biases in clinical emergency medicine established that decision quality degrades predictably under conditions of stress and cognitive overload — and that the conditions most likely to produce bias are also the conditions most likely to disguise themselves as routine [PMID: 12414468]. In an AI-augmented OR, the disguise is the algorithm's confidence display. When the system presents high certainty, the reviewing clinician's threshold for independent scrutiny drops. The fiduciary role remains. The cognitive engagement required to discharge it may have already diminished.

## The Framework

The fiduciary wedge operates at three levels simultaneously:

**1. Legal accountability.** The malpractice attorney, the state licensing board, the adverse event review committee — all are looking for a person. The CRNA who supervised the closed-loop system is that person. Not the algorithm's developers. Not the hospital's IT governance committee. The clinician who stood in the room.

**2. Ethical authority.** The obligation to *this* patient — their specific anatomy, their history, their pre-induction state — is non-transferable. No algorithm has met the patient. No training dataset contains their fear. The fiduciary relationship begins before the first drug, in the room, between two persons. The contextual understanding Duarte-Medrano and colleagues identify as irreplaceable is not a cognitive capability that AI could eventually match. It is a relational one that accountability requires.

**3. Physiological capacity.** Here is where the autonomic regulation literature intersects with the fiduciary frame. Independent clinical judgment under uncertainty is an active cognitive performance — not passive monitoring. It requires attentional regulation, state management under incomplete information, and decision capacity under time pressure. These capacities are funded by autonomic regulation infrastructure: the same prefrontal executive function, HRV coherence, and RMSSD values that predict performance under stress. The CRNA who has been supervising AI outputs for months without exercising independent judgment is not practicing the physiological skill the fiduciary role demands. Deskilling is not a hypothetical risk. Pan and colleagues frame it as a governance problem requiring "bounded delegation" and "structured interprofessional review" to prevent the atrophy of the capacities that liability still requires.

## The Failure Mode

The failure mode is the **oversight illusion**: the belief that reviewing AI-generated recommendations constitutes independent clinical judgment when it constitutes something closer to rubber-stamping with professional credentials.

Giorgetti and colleagues name the trap directly: "reviewing outputs generated by the CDSS in a manner that leaves no real control in the hands of the physician" is not oversight — it is accountability without control. The clinician bears liability for a decision they did not effectively make.

The automation bias data compounds this. Pan and colleagues document that AI systems' miscalibrated confidence — when the model expresses high certainty about a wrong recommendation — is particularly dangerous because clinicians defer not to better analysis but to expressed certainty. The deferral happens below the threshold of deliberate choice; it is a heuristic response to a confidence signal, the same cognitive shortcut Croskerry catalogued in 2002 now amplified by an adversarial new input: an algorithm that sounds certain.

The second failure mode is scope blindness. As AI capability increases, the apparent scope of the fiduciary wedge narrows — fewer decisions require human judgment. What remains, however, is not easier. The decisions that survive AI displacement are the high-sigma ones: ambiguous, high-stakes, value-laden, irreversible. The CRNA in an AI-augmented OR may handle fewer decisions per case. Each decision carries proportionally more clinical weight and all of the legal weight.

## The Test

Minimum viable experiment: ten CRNAs. Establish baseline by measuring independent decision-making accuracy and HRV (RMSSD) during a standardized high-fidelity clinical simulation — a multi-decision anesthesia case without AI decision support. Document RMSSD as a proxy for cognitive engagement.

Assign participants to eight weeks of AI-augmented clinical decision support in their regular practice setting — clinical decision support active during routine cases.

At week eight, repeat the standardized simulation without AI support.

Primary hypothesis: RMSSD during independent simulation will decrease from baseline, reflecting reduced autonomic engagement with clinical decision-making — deskilling made physiologically visible. Secondary hypothesis: decision accuracy variance will increase, with some clinicians maintaining independence while others show measurable drift.

No clinical trial regulatory pathway required. Wearable HRV monitoring plus existing simulation infrastructure. Fourteen days of data collection per phase.

## The Connection

The fiduciary wedge is the architectural reason SRL's work compounds rather than becomes obsolete as AI improves. Every advancement in AI clinical capability increases the cognitive weight of the decisions that remain human — and every increase in cognitive weight increases the demand on the autonomic regulation infrastructure that makes clear judgment under load possible. This is not a defense of human limitation. It is a map of where human capacity remains the irreplaceable load-bearing structure.

A concept note for *automation complacency* does not yet exist in the vault — the mechanism by which AI dependency produces cognitive atrophy warrants its own note, connected to [[state-drift]], [[neural-transition-failure]], and [[human-ai-decision-boundary]].

SRL does not build tools for replacing clinical judgment. SRL builds the autonomic substrate that keeps clinical judgment worth preserving — precisely because the fiduciary gap does not close.
