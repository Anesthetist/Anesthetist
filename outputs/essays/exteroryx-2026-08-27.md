# Notes from the Lab: ExterOryx — The Toggle No One Trained

*The movement between attending to your own body and attending to the environment is not a simple attentional choice but a distinct neurological switching function — trainable, measurable, and absent from every clinical curriculum.*

---

## The Observation

The moment is routine. The patient is stable. The O2 sat reads 98. The CRNA's interoceptive attention is anchored inside — breath, position, internal processing of the next drug interval. The surgeon says something quiet across the drape. Not urgent, just informative. Blood loss is climbing.

The toggle never fires.

This is not a failure of intelligence or training in any conventional sense. The CRNA is highly competent. The monitor numbers are fine. But the switch between inward monitoring and outward room-scanning — between body-as-instrument and environment-as-data — requires a discrete neural operation that no training program has ever systematically addressed.

ExterOryx is built around that gap. Not the absence of situational awareness as a concept (anesthesia educators have written extensively about it) but the absence of situational awareness as a trainable neurological skill, distinct from both interoceptive monitoring and purely external vigilance.

---

## The Mechanism

The brain does not experience internal and external attention as a single continuous dial. Two large-scale neural networks handle these orientations: a default mode-adjacent system for inwardly directed cognition (body state, self-referential processing, planning) and a dorsal attention network for externally directed task engagement. These networks are anti-correlated — when one activates, the other typically suppresses.

What sits between them is the switch.

Menon and Uddin (2010) proposed the decisive framing. The anterior insula, operating as the central node of what they called the "salience network," functions not primarily as an interoceptive monitor but as an integration and switching hub. Its core operation: detect a salient event (internal or external), then signal the appropriate large-scale network to activate while suppressing the other. The model's four mechanisms are precise — bottom-up detection of salience, switching between networks to route attention and working memory resources, modulation of autonomic reactivity via insula-ACC coupling, and rapid access to the motor system when a salient event requires action. In the salience network model, the insula is not where you feel things. It is where the brain decides which domain of experience is currently relevant, and routes processing accordingly [PMID: 20512370].

The boundary between internal and external attention is not invisible. Guterstam and colleagues (2021) used fMRI and multivoxel pattern analysis to demonstrate that the left temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) encodes a distinction that matters clinically: whether attention is being driven from inside (endogenous, self-generated) or from outside (exogenous, environmentally induced). The left TPJ decoded attention type — internal vs. external — significantly better than chance, independent of who was doing the attending. The TPJ is not just a social cognition structure. It is the neural interface that marks where self-referential processing ends and environmental processing begins [PMID: 33587038].

Below both structures, integrating the raw sensory signals that feed the switch, sits the superior colliculus (SC). Alvarado and colleagues (2009) established a principle that matters here: SC multisensory integration — the synthesis of visual, auditory, and somatosensory inputs into a unified environmental signal — requires cooperative top-down input from cortical association areas. Deactivate the relevant cortical afferents and SC multisensory response enhancement disappears. The SC is not autonomous. It amplifies cross-modal environmental signals precisely when cortical systems attend to them — and the enhancement is largest when individual sensory inputs are weak, a phenomenon called "inverse effectiveness." A faint sound paired with a peripheral visual cue generates greater SC response than either signal alone, but only when the system is tuned to integrate them [PMID: 19458228].

This is the architecture ExterOryx targets: the salience-switching insula, the internal/external boundary function of the TPJ, and the multisensory integration amplification of the SC. Three structures, one functional problem — dynamic toggling between self-monitoring and environmental scanning under clinical load.

The interoceptive side of this system is measurable. Stern and colleagues (2017) demonstrated that interoceptive focus — attending to heartbeat or skin temperature — activates a network including insula, sensorimotor regions, occipital, temporal, and prefrontal cortex. Individual differences in MAIA subscales, specifically the attention regulation dimensions, predicted neural activation during interoception. The clinicians who score lowest on attention control to body sensation show the greatest compensatory activation — working harder to access information the skilled interoceptor accesses with less effort [PMID: 28901713].

And what happens when the toggle is inadequately trained in real clinical environments? Weigl and colleagues (2020), studying 74 observation sessions across 110 hours in emergency departments, found that clinicians experienced a mean of 10.9 interruptions per hour. High interruption rates were significantly associated with degraded situational awareness (β = −0.27). The specific culprits — telephone alerts, technical malfunctions, interruptive task-switching — are precisely the stimuli that demand rapid internal-to-external attentional shifts. The clinicians who maintain SA under this load are, whether they know it or not, executing hundreds of insula-mediated network switches per shift [PMID: 32678775].

No program teaches them to do it well. ExterOryx is the attempt to change that.

## The Protocol

ExterOryx builds the toggle through three progressive phases, each delivered within 60-second NeuroMinute windows:

**Phase 1 — Internal Anchors.** Stabilize interoceptive awareness: breath rhythm, heartbeat, muscle tension, proprioceptive baseline. The anchor is established before the toggle is trained. You cannot switch from a channel you haven't opened.

**Phase 2 — Merged.** Pair an internal signal with an external one simultaneously. Breath rhythm synchronized to an ambient soundscape tone. Heartbeat awareness held while scanning peripheral visual field. This phase specifically engages the insula's salience-detection function across both domains — creating the condition where the network switch can occur organically.

**Phase 3 — Dynamic Toggling.** Guided, timed switching between internal and external channels under increasing time pressure. Short intervals (4–8 seconds per channel) train rapid switching. Longer merged holds (15–20 seconds) train sustained dual-channel awareness. The SC's inverse-effectiveness amplification is activated when practitioners are toggling at speed — weak environmental signals become detectible because the system is tuned to integrate them.

Success in Phase 3 is not measured by subjective sense of awareness. It is measurable: HRV stability during toggling intervals (disruption indicates the insula-ACC coupling is stressed), MAIA attention regulation subscale scores, and error rates on simulated OR situational awareness tasks.

---

## The Failure Mode

ExterOryx fails in two directions.

The first is over-training the internal channel. A practitioner who becomes highly adept at interoceptive anchoring but does not complete Phase 3 can develop a kind of skilled introversion under load — body awareness is high, but the toggle to external scanning is slow and cognitively expensive. The result is better body-state monitoring with no improvement in situational awareness for the environment. This is the risk of any interoception-only training approach applied in a high-demand external context.

The second failure is task-switching confusion rather than clean toggling. Under fatigue or cognitive overload, Phase 3 practice can collapse into rapid, undifferentiated attention fragmentation — not toggling between two distinct channels but losing coherence in both. This looks like vigilance from the outside but produces neither stable interoceptive monitoring nor reliable environmental scanning. The clinical presentation is a practitioner who seems engaged but misses both internal body signals and environmental information systematically.

The failure mode is the reason Phase 1 must be stable before Phase 3 is introduced. The toggle is only as clean as the channels being switched between.

## The Test

Fourteen days, ten CRNAs, HRV wearable worn during practice sessions.

**Pre/post measures:** MAIA-2 (specifically the attention regulation, body listening, and trusting subscales); SAGAT (Situation Awareness Global Assessment Technique) administered during simulated OR scenarios with embedded environmental cues; EEG alpha-wave asymmetry as a proxy for internal/external attention balance.

**Prediction:** MAIA attention regulation subscale improves. SAGAT scores on external cue detection improve. EEG shows reduction in left-frontal alpha suppression during Phase 3 intervals (indicating less effortful switching over time). HRV remains stable during toggling.

**What constitutes failure:** No SAGAT improvement despite MAIA improvement — this would indicate the training improved interoceptive skill without generalizing to environmental awareness, confirming the Phase 3 gap. This would be a clinically significant finding about the limits of interoception-only training.

---

## The Connection

ExterOryx fills the gap between Anterocept Domain 3 (integrated interoceptive-exteroceptive awareness as a stable state) and the clinical demand for dynamic, rapid switching between these channels under load. Polyanchora trains simultaneous multi-anchor holding. ExterOryx trains what happens between holds — the crossing. Hemispheric Rebalancing names the direction of the switch (left-hemisphere narrow to right-hemisphere broad), and ExterOryx provides the training protocol that builds the crossing speed.

The vault has good maps of what to hold. ExterOryx is about building the speed and cleanness of the move between them.

---

*The CRNA heard the surgeon's comment — eventually. The information arrived, was processed, was integrated. The patient was fine. But the half-second delay, the momentary lag in the toggle, was the window where errors live. That window is not fate. It is a trainable gap.*
