# Diaphragmatic Blindness

Put your hand on your belly. Right now, wherever you are reading this. Lay it flat just below the navel, fingers spread.

Now breathe.

Don't change anything. Don't perform. Just notice: does the hand rise on the inhale, or does the chest lift instead? Is the movement large or small? Can you feel the diaphragm — the dome-shaped muscle separating the thoracic and abdominal cavities, the muscle you have used every second of your life since the moment you were born — descend?

Most people cannot.

This is not a failure of intelligence or attention. It is a failure of training. You were never taught to feel the most important muscle in your body doing the most important thing it does. You learned to read, to drive, to calculate drug dosages in your head while maintaining a conversation about the weekend. You learned to intubate a patient's trachea by feel in under fifteen seconds. You learned to detect a change in the pitch of a pulse oximeter tone that indicates a two-percent drop in oxygen saturation.

You never learned to feel your own breath.

## I. The Pharmacological Vocabulary

Consider what a Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist knows about the diaphragm.

The CRNA knows that succinylcholine — a depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent — will paralyze the diaphragm within sixty seconds of intravenous administration, producing complete respiratory cessation that requires mechanical ventilation. The CRNA knows that rocuronium, a non-depolarizing agent, achieves the same paralysis through competitive antagonism at the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, and that sugammadex can reverse it by encapsulating the rocuronium molecule in a molecular cage. The CRNA knows that the phrenic nerve, originating at C3-C5 in the cervical spine, innervates the diaphragm, and that an interscalene nerve block placed too medially will paralyze the ipsilateral hemidiaphragm. The CRNA knows that positive-pressure ventilation reverses the normal mechanics of breathing — pushing air in rather than drawing it in — and that this reversal changes intrathoracic pressure dynamics, venous return, cardiac output, and cerebral perfusion.

This is not cursory knowledge. This is deep, operational expertise deployed under life-threatening time pressure multiple times per shift. The CRNA's pharmacological vocabulary for the diaphragm is among the most sophisticated in medicine.

Now ask the same CRNA: Are you breathing from your diaphragm or your chest right now?

The question lands in silence.

Not because the CRNA is incurious. Not because the CRNA is inattentive. But because the training that produced world-class expertise in managing *other people's* diaphragms produced zero expertise in sensing *their own*. The attentional spotlight was always pointed outward — toward the patient, the monitors, the surgical field. The body doing the attending was invisible.

This is diaphragmatic blindness. And it is not a metaphor.

## II. What You Cannot Feel

In 2009, A.D. Craig published a paper in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* that changed how scientists understand the relationship between the body and the self. Craig's model placed the anterior insular cortex — a small region of cortex folded deep within the lateral sulcus of the brain — at the center of interoceptive awareness. The insula, Craig argued, doesn't merely receive signals from the body. It constructs a *representation* of the body's state — a felt map of what is happening inside you, right now, updated moment by moment. This representation is what you experience as "how I feel."

The implications were significant. If feeling — not emotion, but the basic sense of the body's internal state — depends on the insula's representation, then interoceptive awareness is not a mystical capacity. It is a neural function. It has an anatomy. It has a mechanism. And it can be trained.

Catherine Kerr, working at Brown University, showed how. In 2013, Kerr and colleagues published a study demonstrating that mindfulness training — specifically, directed attention to specific body regions — modulated somatosensory alpha rhythms in the cortex. Alpha rhythms are the brain's gating mechanism: when alpha power increases over a brain region, that region's processing is suppressed; when alpha decreases, the region opens to incoming signals. Kerr's finding was that meditators could learn to voluntarily *tune* this gating mechanism — raising alpha over irrelevant regions and lowering it over the target of attention.

This is the neural mechanism behind diaphragmatic blindness. The CRNA has spent decades training alpha gating toward external clinical signals — the monitors, the surgical field, the patient's respiratory pattern. The alpha gate over their own diaphragmatic sensation has been closed for so long that the signal is effectively invisible. Not because the diaphragm isn't sending information — it sends information with every breath, fourteen to twenty times per minute, for the entirety of a career — but because the brain has learned to suppress it in favor of more professionally urgent signals.

The diaphragm is shouting. The cortex is not listening.

Hugo Critchley, working at the Brighton and Sussex Medical School, added another layer. In a 2017 paper on interoception and emotion, Critchley and Sarah Garfinkel distinguished three dimensions of interoceptive capacity: *accuracy* (can you actually detect body signals?), *sensibility* (do you believe you can detect them?), and *awareness* (does your confidence match your actual accuracy?). The distinction matters because clinicians often score high on sensibility — they *believe* they are attuned to their bodies — while scoring poorly on accuracy tasks like heartbeat detection.

The clinical significance is precise: a CRNA who believes she is managing her stress effectively (high sensibility) while her heart rate variability has been declining for six months (low accuracy) is experiencing a metacognitive gap that no amount of willpower can bridge. She doesn't know what she doesn't feel. Her confidence in her own regulation masks the deficit.

This is not a character flaw. It is a predictable consequence of a training system that developed one dimension of interoceptive capacity (outward clinical monitoring) while neglecting two others (inward body sensing and the calibration between them).

Wolf Mehling, at the University of California San Francisco, built the measurement tool that makes this gap visible. The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness — the MAIA, first published in 2012 and revised as MAIA-2 in 2018 — measures eight distinct subscales of body awareness: Noticing, Not-Distracting, Not-Worrying, Attention Regulation, Emotional Awareness, Self-Regulation, Body Listening, and Trusting.

Eight subscales. Eight ways the body talks to the mind. And when clinicians take the MAIA-2, a pattern emerges that surprises no one who has worked in an OR: high Attention Regulation (they are excellent at directing focus), high Noticing for external signals, and conspicuously low scores on Body Listening and Trusting — the two subscales that measure whether a person actively listens to their own body for insight and whether they experience their body as safe and trustworthy.

The diaphragm is one signal among thousands that the MAIA-2 illuminates. But it is the most consequential one for nervous system regulation, because the diaphragm is the only skeletal muscle in the body that is under both voluntary and involuntary control. It is the bridge between the nervous system you choose and the nervous system that chooses you.

## III. The Gateway

B.K.S. Iyengar understood this before the neuroscience existed to explain it.

In *Light on Pranayama*, published in 1981, Iyengar wrote what is arguably the most detailed manual for diaphragmatic awareness ever produced — not by a physiologist, but by a yoga practitioner who had spent decades refining his attention to the mechanics of breathing. Iyengar didn't use the language of alpha gating or insular cortex activation. He used the language of the body itself: the ribs, the intercostals, the floating ribs that allow lateral expansion, the pelvic floor that supports diaphragmatic descent, the thoracic cage as a trainable architecture.

His central insight was physiological, not spiritual: the diaphragm is the gateway between voluntary and involuntary nervous systems. When you consciously slow your breathing and engage the diaphragm, you are performing the only act that directly bridges the somatic nervous system (voluntary motor control) and the autonomic nervous system (involuntary regulation of heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, arousal). You are, in Iyengar's framing, pulling the one lever that connects the cockpit to the engine room.

Paul Lehrer, working at Rutgers University, proved the mechanism in 2000. Lehrer, along with Evgeny Vaschillo and Bronya Vaschillo, discovered that breathing at a specific rate — approximately 5.5 breaths per minute for most adults, though the precise rate varies individually between 4.5 and 6.5 — produces a phenomenon called cardiorespiratory resonance. At this resonant frequency, the oscillations of the respiratory cycle and the oscillations of the cardiovascular cycle lock into phase — the heart speeds up on inhale and slows on exhale in perfect synchrony, producing the largest possible amplitude of heart rate variability.

This is not relaxation. This is the cardiovascular system operating at peak efficiency. The baroreflex — the body's blood pressure regulation loop — gains maximum sensitivity. Vagal tone — the tonic activity of the vagus nerve that maintains cardiac deceleration — reaches its highest levels. The nervous system is not calming down. It is *tuning up*.

And it all depends on the diaphragm.

If you are breathing thoracically — from the chest, with the accessory muscles of the neck and shoulders — the respiratory oscillation is shallow and irregular. The resonance condition cannot be met. The baroreflex doesn't lock. The HRV amplitude stays flat. You can breathe at exactly 5.5 breaths per minute with perfect timing and still fail to produce resonance if the diaphragm is not the primary mover.

Diaphragmatic blindness, in this light, is not merely an interoceptive curiosity. It is the bottleneck. It is the reason that telling a stressed clinician to "take a deep breath" produces eye-rolling rather than relief. The instruction is technically correct and practically useless, because the clinician does not know whether they are breathing deeply with their diaphragm or shallowly with their chest — and under stress, the default is almost always chest.

## IV. The Antonym

Diaphragmatic blindness has a counterpart. If the blindness is the deficit — the inability to sense and control the diaphragm — then the trained capacity is *diaphragmatic literacy*: the ability to perceive diaphragmatic movement in real time, distinguish chest from belly breathing under varying cognitive loads, control breath depth and rate through deliberate engagement, and maintain diaphragmatic patterns under stress without conscious monitoring.

The distinction between blindness and literacy is not binary. It is a spectrum, and the spectrum is trainable.

In 2026, a team led by Little published a randomized controlled trial in paramedicine students — a population that shares the high-stakes, high-autonomic-load characteristics of CRNAs. The study tested a structured breathwork method and found measurable improvements in resilience and wellbeing markers. The finding was not that breathwork "works" in the abstract — that evidence base is decades deep. The finding was that structured breathwork training produces measurable changes in a population that had been given zero prior instruction in breathing as a regulation tool.

The paramedicine students were not resistant to breathwork. They were untrained. The intervention was not heroic. It was foundational.

This is the difference between a wellness recommendation and a literacy program. Telling a clinician to "practice breathing exercises" is like telling an illiterate person to "read more." The instruction presupposes a capacity that has not been developed. Diaphragmatic literacy — the actual ability to feel, control, and use the diaphragm as a regulation instrument — must be taught the way any clinical skill is taught: with progressive structure, immediate feedback, and measurable outcomes.

The progressive structure is the 21-day curriculum. The immediate feedback is heart rate data on the wrist, updated with every breath. The measurable outcome is the trend in HRV over weeks — not a feeling, not a self-report, but a number that goes up or down and tells you whether your nervous system is gaining flexibility or losing it.

None of it works until you can feel the diaphragm.

That is the first lesson. That is where everything begins.

Place your hand on your belly again. You did it at the start of this chapter. Do it now.

Breathe. Slowly. Feel the hand rise as the diaphragm descends, drawing air into the lower lobes of the lungs where gas exchange is most efficient. Feel the hand fall as the diaphragm ascends, pushing air out through a slightly narrowed glottis that extends the exhale and stimulates the vagal brake.

You are performing the single most powerful act available to you for modulating your own autonomic nervous system. You are pulling Iyengar's lever. You are entering the resonance window that Lehrer mapped. You are opening the alpha gate that Kerr discovered. You are activating the insular representation that Craig described.

You are also doing something your training never asked you to do: paying attention to your own body while you are alive and well, rather than only when something has gone wrong.

The diaphragm was never missing. The attention was.

*That can change today.*
