# The Body Is Evidence

Part II of this book is called "The Science." But before the studies and the mechanisms and the names of the researchers who mapped them, something more fundamental needs to be said about how this book treats knowledge itself.

Most knowledge systems ask a single question: *what is true?*

This book asks three different questions: *What can I trace? What have I felt? And what am I responsible for claiming?*

The distinction matters because this is a book that takes the body seriously as a source of knowledge — not metaphorically, not inspirationally, but epistemologically. The body is not an obstacle to knowing. It is not a distraction from the real work of the mind. The body is a way of knowing. And the science of the last three decades — from A.D. Craig's insular cortex mapping to Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis to Stephen Porges's neuroception — has established this not as philosophy but as neurobiology.

This chapter lays out the epistemological commitments that govern every claim in the remaining pages. Three commitments. Simple to state. Difficult to honor. Essential if a book that bridges clinical science, contemplative tradition, and embodied practice is going to hold together without collapsing into either overstatement or abstraction.

---

## I. Three Commitments

**The body is evidence.** Embodied pattern recognition — the gut feeling that something is wrong with a patient before the monitors confirm it, the intuition that a drug dose needs adjusting before the vital signs move, the felt sense of a room's tension that predicts a complication — has epistemic legitimacy alongside peer-reviewed literature. Not the same status. Not the same weight. But legitimate.

This commitment puts the book on specific scientific ground. A.D. Craig, working at the Barrow Neurological Institute, published a landmark paper in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* in 2009 titled "How do you feel — now?" Craig's model placed the anterior insular cortex at the center of subjective awareness itself. The anterior insula, Craig argued, does not merely receive signals from the body. It constructs a re-representation of the body's current physiological state — a felt map of what is happening inside you, updated moment by moment. This representation is what you experience as "how I feel."

Craig's claim was radical in its implications: interoception — the sense of the body's internal state — is not supplementary to consciousness. It is foundational to it. The "sentient self" arises from the anterior insula's integration of interoceptive signals. To feel your heartbeat, your breathing, your muscular tension, your temperature — this is not a secondary capacity bolted onto cognition. It is the ground floor of awareness itself.

If Craig is right — and the subsequent two decades of neuroimaging research have largely confirmed his model — then a clinician's felt sense of a patient's deterioration is not mystical intuition. It is interoceptive processing. The anterior insula is integrating hundreds of subtle signals — the patient's facial color, the pitch of the monitor tone, the room's emotional valence, the clinician's own autonomic state — into a unified "global emotional moment" that registers as knowing before the conscious mind can articulate what it knows.

**Provenance is the epistemology.** The origin of a claim determines how much weight it can bear. A randomized controlled trial published in *The Lancet* can support a stronger claim than a case report. A case report can support a stronger claim than an anecdote. An anecdote, honestly reported as such, can support a stronger claim than a fabrication dressed in the language of evidence.

This book traces every claim to its source. When the source is a PubMed-indexed study with a verified DOI, the claim carries the weight of the study's design and sample. When the source is Randy's twenty-eight years of clinical observation, the claim carries the weight of embodied expertise — substantial, but different. When the source is a yogic tradition spanning centuries, the claim carries the weight of accumulated practice wisdom — valuable, but requiring modern validation before it can anchor a clinical recommendation.

Provenance matters because the space between breathwork and neuroscience is littered with overstatement. "Studies prove" when studies suggest. "Clinically validated" when the study had twelve participants. "Ancient wisdom confirmed by modern science" when the mapping is approximate at best. This book refuses that slippage. Not because rigorous language is more impressive — it is less impressive, deliberately — but because clinical credibility is the currency that makes everything else possible.

**Knowledge is something you take care of.** The nursing process — Assess, Diagnose, Plan, Implement, Evaluate — is not merely a clinical workflow. It is an epistemological framework. You do not collect knowledge and set it on a shelf. You assess it (is this still accurate?), diagnose it (where are the gaps?), plan around it (what needs enrichment?), implement with it (put it into practice), and evaluate the results (did the intervention match the expectation?).

## II. The Somatic Marker

Antonio Damasio, working at the University of Iowa, published a paper in 1996 in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* that changed how neuroscience understands the relationship between body and decision.

The somatic marker hypothesis proposes that bodily states — gut feelings, changes in heart rate, muscular tension, the flush of the skin — serve as rapid decision-making signals. They mark options as favorable or unfavorable before conscious deliberation reaches a conclusion. The prefrontal cortex stores learned associations between situations and their somatic outcomes, and these associations fire automatically when a similar situation is encountered.

Damasio's evidence came from patients with ventromedial prefrontal cortex damage. These patients retained normal IQ scores. They could pass logic tests. They could analyze options dispassionately. But they could not make decisions. Without the body's input — without the somatic markers that tag options with feeling — the pure logic of the mind produced paralysis. Every option looked equally weighted. The body's vote had been silenced, and without it, the mind could not choose.

The implications for clinical practice are precise. What experienced clinicians call "clinical intuition" — the sense that something is wrong before you can articulate what — is a well-calibrated somatic marker system. The CRNA who walks into a room and feels unease before looking at the monitors is not being irrational. She is accessing decades of accumulated body-situation associations stored in her prefrontal cortex and expressed through her autonomic nervous system.

The feeling precedes the thought. The body reasons before the mind decides.

This is not a deficiency in the reasoning process. It is the reasoning process. Damasio demonstrated that without somatic markers, reasoning collapses. The body is not a noisy channel that distorts clear thinking. It is a signal channel that enables it.

## III. The Nerve That Listens

Stephen Porges published *The Polyvagal Theory* in 2011 — a book that reframed the entire architecture of the autonomic nervous system. The classical model taught two branches: sympathetic (fight or flight) and parasympathetic (rest and digest). Porges argued for three.

The oldest circuit, phylogenetically — the unmyelinated dorsal vagal complex — produces the freeze response. Immobilization. Shutdown. The reptilian defense. In extreme threat, the body does not flee or fight. It collapses.

The middle circuit — the sympathetic nervous system — produces mobilization. Heart rate up, blood pressure up, cortisol released, muscles ready. The mammalian defense.

The newest circuit — the myelinated ventral vagal complex — produces something different entirely. Social engagement. Calm presence. Precise motor control. The face, the voice, the middle ear, the larynx, the heart — all regulated by the ventral vagal branch of the vagus nerve, all coordinated for connection, communication, and the kind of focused-yet-open awareness that a clinician needs when everything around them is in crisis.

Porges introduced a term for the pre-conscious system that determines which circuit activates: *neuroception*. Before you consciously perceive a threat, the vagus nerve has already assessed the situation and initiated an autonomic response. Your heart rate changes before you know why. Your breathing shallows before you decide to be anxious. Your pupils dilate before you process the alarm.

Neuroception is interoception's darker twin. Where interoception (in Craig's formulation) is the conscious awareness of body states, neuroception is the unconscious detection system that generates those states. It is the nervous system's surveillance program — always running, never announced, shaping every physiological moment of every clinical shift.

The clinical significance is this: neuroception does not read the chart. It does not know that the patient in OR 7 is a straightforward cholecystectomy in a healthy 42-year-old. It only knows that the body has not yet recovered from the complex airway in OR 3, and the residual sympathetic tone from that case is still coloring the landscape. The ventral vagal brake — the circuit that supports the precise, calm engagement required for optimal clinical performance — may not have fully re-engaged.

This is why "take a deep breath" is both correct and insufficient. The instruction targets the right mechanism — slow breathing stimulates vagal afferents and promotes ventral vagal reengagement. But without structure, without measurement, without awareness of what the nervous system is actually doing, the instruction is a suggestion, not a training protocol.

## IV. Three Ways to Know Your Body

Hugo Critchley and Sarah Garfinkel, working at the Brighton and Sussex Medical School, added a distinction in 2017 that sharpens everything that came before.

In their paper "Interoception and Emotion," published in *Current Opinion in Psychology*, Critchley and Garfinkel articulated three dimensions of interoceptive capacity — three different ways a person can "know" their own body, each measurable, each trainable, and each capable of diverging from the others.

**Interoceptive accuracy** is objective performance. Can you actually detect your own heartbeat? Can you count your heartbeats in time with a clock? This is measured by tasks like the Schandry heartbeat detection test, where participants count their heartbeats without taking their pulse and the count is compared to the actual number.

**Interoceptive sensibility** is subjective belief. Do you believe you are attuned to your body? Do you rate yourself as someone who notices physical sensations readily? This is measured by self-report instruments — questionnaires that ask about your confidence in your own body awareness.

**Interoceptive awareness** is the correspondence between the two. Does your confidence match your accuracy? Is your belief about your body awareness calibrated to your actual body awareness?

The distinction matters because the three dimensions can dissociate. And in clinical populations — including clinicians themselves — they frequently do.

A CRNA who has spent decades in high-stakes environments has often developed high interoceptive sensibility. She believes she is attuned to her stress levels. She believes she knows when she needs a break. She has high confidence in her self-assessment.

But her interoceptive accuracy — measured objectively — may tell a different story. Under chronic stress, the body's calibration drifts. Heart rate variability declines over months and years, but the decline is gradual enough to escape notice. Resting heart rate creeps upward by two beats per minute per year, but the subjective experience of "normal" recalibrates around the new baseline. The clinician feels fine — high sensibility — while the data shows erosion — low accuracy.

The gap between sensibility and accuracy is the metacognitive blind spot. It is the reason that telling a burned-out clinician "you need to take care of yourself" produces resistance rather than recognition. From the inside, the clinician feels fine. The sensibility channel is clear. It is the accuracy channel that has gone silent — and no amount of willpower can restore a signal you do not know you have lost.

Wolf Mehling, at the University of California San Francisco, built the measurement tool that makes this gap visible. The Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness — the MAIA, first published in *PLoS ONE* in 2012 and revised as MAIA-2 in 2018 — measures eight distinct subscales of body awareness: Noticing, Not-Distracting, Not-Worrying, Attention Regulation, Emotional Awareness, Self-Regulation, Body Listening, and Trusting.

Eight dimensions. Eight different facets of how the body talks to the mind and how the mind listens. When clinicians take the MAIA-2, a pattern emerges: high Attention Regulation (they are excellent at directing focus), but conspicuously low Body Listening and Trusting — the two subscales that measure whether a person actively listens to their body for insight and whether they experience their body as safe and trustworthy.

The clinician's body has been instrumentalized. It is a tool for patient care — a pair of hands for intubation, a pair of eyes for monitoring, a pair of ears for auscultation. It has not been a source of personal information since the last time the clinician was a patient. The MAIA-2 makes this structural gap measurable.

## V. What We Know More Than We Can Tell

There is one more voice that belongs in this chapter, and it does not come from neuroscience.

Michael Polanyi — a physical chemist turned philosopher working at the University of Manchester — published *Personal Knowledge* in 1958 and *The Tacit Dimension* in 1966. His central claim was deceptively simple: "We know more than we can tell."

Polanyi was describing tacit knowledge — the kind of knowing that resists explicit articulation. A cyclist knows how to ride but cannot explain the physics of balance. A wine taster knows the difference between Burgundy and Bordeaux but cannot fully specify the sensory criteria. A surgeon knows the right tension on a suture but cannot describe it in units of force.

And a CRNA knows when something is wrong.

Polanyi distinguished between *focal awareness* — the explicit, articulable knowledge in the center of attention — and *subsidiary awareness* — the tacit, embodied knowledge that operates in the periphery. Clinical expertise, in Polanyi's framework, consists of making focal what was once subsidiary, and then incorporating the newly focal knowledge back into subsidiary awareness as it becomes automatic.

This is the structure of clinical mastery. The student nurse anesthetist focuses explicitly on intubation technique — hand position, blade angle, vocal cord visualization. The experienced CRNA has incorporated all of that into subsidiary awareness and now focuses on the patient's autonomic response to laryngoscopy — the heart rate change, the blood pressure spike, the depth of anesthesia. What was once focal (technique) becomes subsidiary (background competence), freeing attention for the next level of pattern recognition.

But Polanyi's framework also illuminates the blind spot. The subsidiary awareness that enables clinical expertise is directed outward — toward the patient, the monitors, the surgical field. The clinician's own body remains outside the frame. Tacit knowledge of one's own physiological state — the kind of knowledge that would allow a CRNA to notice her own heart rate variability declining month over month — has never been made focal. It was never part of the training. It was never part of the language.

Catherine Kerr, working at Brown University, demonstrated the neural mechanism. In 2013, Kerr and colleagues published a study showing that mindfulness training modulated somatosensory alpha rhythms in the cortex. Alpha rhythms are the brain's gating mechanism: when alpha power increases over a brain region, that region's processing is suppressed; when alpha decreases, the region opens to incoming signals. Meditators could learn to voluntarily tune this gate — opening awareness to body regions that had been suppressed.

The alpha gate is Polanyi's tacit dimension rendered in neural terms. The clinician has spent decades gating attention toward external signals and away from internal ones. The alpha power over their own somatosensory cortex is high — suppressed. The body's signals are present but invisible, not because the signals are absent but because the gate has been closed.

The gate can be opened. That is what training does. That is what the next four chapters are about.

## VI. The Epistemological Ground

This book stands on three legs, and every chapter that follows draws from all three.

The first leg is published, peer-reviewed science — the studies of Craig, Damasio, Porges, Critchley, Garfinkel, Mehling, Kerr, Lehrer, and the many others who mapped the body's ways of knowing. These studies carry the weight of controlled methodology, sample sizes, statistical analysis, and replication.

The second leg is clinical expertise — twenty-eight years of CRNA practice, thousands of cases, the accumulated tacit knowledge that Polanyi described. This expertise carries a different kind of weight: the authority of sustained, embodied practice under conditions that cannot be replicated in a laboratory. When a clinician describes what happens in the body during a crisis, that description is evidence — not peer-reviewed evidence, but experiential evidence that deserves serious consideration.

The third leg is contemplative tradition — Iyengar's pranayama, the Buddhist bardo, the Vedantic koshas, Gurdjieff's self-remembering. These traditions carry the weight of centuries of practice — thousands of practitioners across thousands of years who developed sophisticated maps of embodied awareness long before neuroscience had the tools to validate them. They are not science. They are the accumulated observations of contemplative communities, and the remarkable correspondence between their maps and the neuroscience that followed is not coincidence. It is convergent evidence from different epistemic traditions pointing at the same phenomena.

No single leg is sufficient. Published science without clinical context produces laboratory findings that never reach the bedside. Clinical expertise without scientific validation produces folk wisdom that cannot be distinguished from bias. Contemplative tradition without modern measurement produces beautiful maps with no way to verify the territory.

The three together — science, practice, tradition — form an epistemological architecture that is stronger than any single source. Every claim in this book identifies which leg it stands on. Every reader can assess for themselves how much weight to give it.

The body is evidence. The provenance tells you what kind.
