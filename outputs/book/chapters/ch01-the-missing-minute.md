## I. What Happens in Sixty Seconds

There is a moment every clinician knows but nobody teaches.

It arrives between the alarm and the action. Between the page and the intubation. Between the surgeon's voice saying "we've got a bleeder" and your hands reaching for the drug. It lasts about sixty seconds, though it doesn't feel like any particular length of time. It feels like falling and floating simultaneously — a gap where the nervous system is making decisions your conscious mind hasn't caught up to yet.

In anesthesia, we have a word for almost everything. We name the drugs, the dosages, the complications. We name the airway grades and the ASA classifications and the phases of emergence. We have protocols for malignant hyperthermia and anaphylaxis and cardiac arrest. We train for years to respond to every conceivable crisis with precision and speed.

But we have no word for what happens inside the clinician's body during that gap.

No protocol for the spike in heart rate that arrives before conscious thought. No training for the way the visual field narrows when cortisol floods the prefrontal cortex. No curriculum that addresses the tremor in the diaphragm — the same diaphragm we pharmacologically manage in our patients every single day — when it belongs to us and not to them.

This is the missing minute. Not the crisis itself. Not the response. The space between.

Stephen Porges, whose Polyvagal Theory mapped the autonomic hierarchy that governs these moments, calls the body's pre-conscious threat detection system *neuroception*. Before you decide whether a situation is dangerous, your vagus nerve has already decided for you. Your heart rate accelerates. Your breathing shallows. Your pupils dilate. The ventral vagal brake — the evolutionarily newest branch of the autonomic nervous system, the one that supports calm engagement and precise motor control — releases.

All of this happens in less than a second. And none of it is taught in any anesthesia program in the country.

The irony is precise: Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists are the world's foremost experts in managing other people's autonomic nervous systems. We titrate propofol to suppress consciousness. We dose epinephrine to restart hearts. We ventilate lungs, modulate blood pressure, and navigate the deepest states of physiological vulnerability a human body can enter — all while monitoring autonomic parameters on six screens simultaneously.

Then we walk to the break room, pour a coffee, and have no idea what our own heart rate variability looks like.

## II. The Numbers Nobody Wants

If the missing minute were only a philosophical observation, it would make an interesting journal article and nothing more. But the missing minute has a body count.

Sixty-two percent of CRNAs report burnout symptoms. Not dissatisfaction. Not "I had a bad week." Burnout — the clinical syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced sense of personal accomplishment. The number comes from the National Council of State Boards of Nursing, whose 2025 workforce study found a 28% increase in burnout since 2021.

The cost is $56,000 per nurse who leaves. Multiply by the turnover rate across 67,000 practicing CRNAs and more than 3,000 new students entering training every year, and the industry hemorrhages nine billion dollars annually — not on patient care, but on replacing the people who deliver it.

These numbers are familiar to anyone who reads nursing journals or attends AANA conferences. They appear in keynote slides and wellness committee reports and congressional testimony. The U.S. Surgeon General has declared clinician burnout a national priority. Congress has allocated more than $100 million to address it.

And yet.

When you ask clinicians what they actually need, the answer is not more money for wellness programs. The answer is time. Sixty-four and a half percent of clinicians cite lack of time as the primary barrier to any stress management practice. They know meditation might help. They've heard about breathwork. They've read the articles and attended the workshops and downloaded the apps.

They don't have twenty minutes. They don't have ten. Between induction and emergence, between one patient's extubation and the next patient's pre-op assessment, between the critical incident and the next case that requires full precision — they have perhaps thirty seconds. Perhaps sixty. Perhaps the length of time it takes to walk from OR 3 to OR 7.

That is the gap moment. And it is, as it turns out, enough.

Albert Wu coined the term "second victim" in 2000 to describe what happens to clinicians after adverse patient events. Susan Scott operationalized it in 2010, documenting the psychological cascade: shame, self-doubt, re-experiencing, sleep disruption, and — critically — autonomic dysregulation. The clinician's nervous system encodes the event not as a memory but as a state. The body carries it forward. Into the next case. Into the next shift. Into the drive home and the sleepless night and the morning when the alarm goes off and the body says *I cannot do this again* even though the mind says *I must*.

Between ten and forty-three percent of clinicians involved in adverse events experience significant second-victim symptoms. The rate is higher among anesthesia providers, for a reason that is physiologically precise: the severity and immediacy of potential complications in anesthesia mean the autonomic spike is larger, the cortisol load is heavier, and the window for processing is shorter than in almost any other clinical specialty.

Bessel van der Kolk wrote that the body keeps the score. He was describing trauma patients. But for clinicians, the formulation needs revision: the body keeps *working*. It keeps showing up. It keeps intubating and titrating and monitoring. It keeps performing at a level that satisfies every external metric of competence, even as the internal metrics — heart rate variability declining month over month, resting heart rate creeping upward year over year, blood pressure inching past the threshold that the clinician would flag immediately in a patient but ignores entirely in themselves — tell a different story.

The body keeps working until it doesn't.

## III. Thirty-Seven Windows

Here is what nobody told me in school.

A twelve-hour CRNA shift contains approximately thirty to forty natural transition moments. Between cases. During turnover. Walking from the OR to the supply room. Waiting for the surgeon to scrub. Standing at the patient's bedside during the three minutes between "we're ready" and "let's go." Sitting in the break room for the ninety seconds between pouring coffee and being paged.

These are gap moments.

They are invisible. They are unstructured. They are, in the current model of clinical workflow, dead time — intervals to be filled with charting, phone-checking, or the particular variety of dissociation that clinicians develop as a survival mechanism: the thousand-yard stare between cases that looks like rest but is actually the nervous system idling in a state that is neither recovery nor readiness.

What if those moments were something else?

The question sounds simple. It took twenty-eight years to ask it.

In Japanese aesthetic philosophy, there is a concept called *ma* — the meaningful pause, the interval where the space between notes creates the music. In Tibetan Buddhist psychology, the *bardo* is the liminal space between states of existence — and the tradition holds that these transitional windows are the moments of greatest potential for transformation. In yoga, *kumbhaka* — the retention of breath between inhale and exhale — is considered the gateway to deeper states of awareness. Viktor Frankl, writing from inside a Nazi concentration camp, described "the last of the human freedoms" as the space between stimulus and response.

The gap moment is not a new discovery. It is a very old recognition, arriving in clinical scrubs.

The difference — the thing that makes Gap Moment Training something more than a philosophical reframe — is measurement.

In 2019, a team led by Marianne Six Dijkstra, working with Rollin McCraty of HeartMath, published a study in *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback* that tested a one-minute paced breathing protocol on 877 Dutch workers. One minute. Six deep breaths. The heart rate range measured during that single minute correlated significantly with age, workability, BMI, waist circumference, cholesterol, and blood pressure. A sixty-second window was enough to capture a clinically meaningful signal about the state of a person's autonomic nervous system.

Two years later, Min You and Sylvain Laborde at the German Sport University Cologne asked a different question: does it matter how long you breathe? They tested slow-paced breathing at six cycles per minute for five, ten, fifteen, and twenty minutes, measuring cardiac vagal activity via RMSSD — the root mean square of successive differences between heartbeats, the gold standard for parasympathetic tone. The result surprised the field: five minutes produced the same vagal activation as twenty. The dose-response curve was flat. The nervous system's recovery mechanism engaged early and didn't improve meaningfully with more time.

Five minutes equals twenty. One minute captures the signal. Sixty seconds is a window, not a wall.

Somewhere inside these numbers is the outline of a different approach to clinician wellbeing. Not meditation retreats. Not resilience workshops. Not apps that ask you to find a quiet room and breathe for ten minutes when you haven't sat down in six hours. Something smaller. Something that fits inside the transitions that already exist in every clinical shift. Something measured, not just felt.

Something that takes one minute.

After twenty-eight years of managing other people's autonomic nervous systems, the most important clinical observation was the simplest: the tools I used every day on patients did not exist for the person delivering the care.

The diaphragm I paralyzed with succinylcholine and managed with mechanical ventilation — I had never learned to use my own as a regulation instrument. The heart rate variability I monitored on six screens simultaneously — I had never once looked at my own. The vagal tone I modulated with glycopyrrolate and atropine — I had never trained through breath.

The pharmacological vocabulary was enormous. The somatic vocabulary was empty.

This book is about filling that gap. Not with theory. Not with motivation. Not with the kind of wellness language that makes clinicians roll their eyes because it was clearly written by someone who has never stood in an OR at 3 AM with a crashing patient and a resident who is frozen and a surgeon who is shouting and a body that is doing exactly what twenty-eight years of unmanaged autonomic load have trained it to do.

This book is about one minute. Five breaths. What your nervous system does in the space between cases — and what it could do instead, if anyone had ever taught you to pay attention.
