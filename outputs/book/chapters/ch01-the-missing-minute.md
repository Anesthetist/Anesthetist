## The Sixty Seconds Nobody Uses

There is a moment between every case — after the patient is transferred, before the next one arrives — that every CRNA knows and nobody talks about. It lasts thirty to ninety seconds. Some days less. Some days, if the turnover team is slow, a luxurious two minutes.

In those seconds, the room resets. The table is wiped. The circuit is changed. The monitors are zeroed. Everything in the operating room returns to baseline.

Everything except you.

Your heart rate is still 97. Your RMSSD — the beat-to-beat variation that indexes vagal tone, the parasympathetic nervous system's grip on your heart — is suppressed. Your breathing is shallow, thoracic, driven by accessory muscles instead of your diaphragm. Your cortisol has nowhere to go because the next stimulus is already arriving.

This is the moment that defines this book. Not the crisis. The gap between the crisis and the next demand. The turnover. The handoff. The walk from OR 3 to OR 7.

It's the most valuable sixty seconds in your clinical day, and you are spending it on your phone, or talking to the circulator, or — most commonly — doing nothing at all. Not nothing as in meditation. Nothing as in dissociation. A micro-absence. The body waiting for the next stimulus because it has forgotten that it has any other mode.

Eighty-three percent of anesthesiology residents reported experiencing a stressful event within the previous thirty days. That finding, from Putnam and colleagues' 2025 snapshot of the field, surprised no one who has worked in an OR. What's remarkable isn't the stress. What's remarkable is that no standard protocol exists for what to do in the sixty seconds after the stress.

We have ACLS for cardiac emergencies. We have hand hygiene for infection prevention. We have surgical timeouts for wrong-site surgery. We have checklists for machine failures, drug errors, and malignant hyperthermia.

We have nothing — no protocol, no training, no shared language — for the thing that breaks us.

## What Nobody Taught You

Here is what you learned in your nurse anesthesia program: how to manage a patient's autonomic nervous system with pharmacological precision. Propofol titrated to bispectral index. Sevoflurane adjusted to end-tidal concentration. Vasopressors bolused by blood pressure response. Opioids dosed to blunt the sympathetic surge of surgical stimulation.

You learned to read the patient's nervous system through a monitor — heart rate, blood pressure, end-tidal CO₂, pulse oximetry, neuromuscular blockade — and to intervene with milligram accuracy.

You learned nothing about reading your own.

This is not a failure of education. It is a feature. The hidden curriculum of clinical training teaches a specific relationship to the body: *your* body is the vehicle for delivering care to *their* body. Your heart rate doesn't matter. Your breathing pattern is irrelevant. Your jaw tension, your shoulder set, your diaphragm — these are things you manage in patients, not in yourself.

Twenty-eight out of thirty anesthesia professionals, when asked to voluntarily engage their diaphragm — the single most powerful lever for autonomic regulation available without a prescription — could not do it. Four to five seconds of nasal inhalation using accessory muscles only. The very clinicians who assess, diagnose, and treat diaphragmatic dysfunction in patients every day cannot use their own diaphragm as a regulation tool.

This finding is the most concise statement of the problem this book addresses. The most powerful regulatory mechanism in the human body is available to every clinician, requires no equipment, takes less than a minute to deploy, and produces measurable autonomic shifts within seconds.

And we never learned to use it.

Because nobody taught us that our nervous system was part of the clinical equipment.

## The Drift You Can't Feel

Here is what happens over the course of a twelve-hour shift, and it happens so gradually that you never notice it.

Your heart rate variability — the subtle variation in the time between heartbeats that reflects your autonomic nervous system's flexibility — declines. Not dramatically. Not in a way that would alarm anyone watching a monitor. It declines the way a tide goes out: so slowly that you don't notice the sand is dry until you realize you're a long way from the water.

In a study of nurses working twelve-hour shifts, Li and colleagues measured this decline with continuous wearable ECG monitors. RMSSD — the time-domain HRV metric most sensitive to parasympathetic activity — dropped by approximately twenty-five percent over the course of a shift. The clinicians didn't report feeling different. Their self-assessed stress levels didn't track with the objective measurement. The nervous system was drifting, and the person inside it couldn't tell.

This is what we call *state drift.*

Most events aren't caused by ignorance. They're caused by state drift.

State drift is not a mental health problem. It is not burnout, though burnout is its cumulative expression. It is not stress, though stress is its fuel. State drift is the progressive, undetected degradation of autonomic regulation during sustained cognitive performance. It is what happens when the nervous system's recovery cycles are consistently incomplete — when each arousal spike resolves to a slightly higher baseline, and the baseline ratchets up across the hours like a thermostat that someone keeps nudging.

By hour ten of a twelve-hour shift, the clinical expert who could pattern-match a difficult airway at hour two is operating on a nervous system that has drifted far enough from baseline that pattern recognition itself is degraded. Not because they're tired — though they are. Not because they're distracted — though the attention network has narrowed. Because the autonomic substrate that supports expert-level decision-making has shifted underneath them, and nobody taught them to detect the shift.

Gary Klein, the cognitive scientist who developed the Recognition-Primed Decision Making model by studying how experts actually make decisions under pressure, showed that expert performance depends on pattern matching, not analysis. Experts don't weigh options. They recognize the situation, simulate the first satisfactory response, and act. This is what makes expert clinicians fast — and it's what makes state drift dangerous. Because pattern recognition runs on the autonomic nervous system, and when that system drifts, the pattern library becomes unreliable. Not wrong. *Unreliable.* Which is worse, because wrong is detectable and unreliable is not.

## The Number That Predicts Everything

In 1999, Christopher Cole and colleagues published a finding in the New England Journal of Medicine that should have changed healthcare training forever.

They measured a simple metric: how fast does your heart rate drop after you stop exercising? Specifically, after a standard exercise stress test, how many beats per minute does your heart rate decrease in the first sixty seconds of recovery?

If it dropped more than twelve beats per minute, you were normal. If it dropped fewer than twelve, your risk of dying from any cause — not just cardiac causes, *any* cause — was two and a half times higher than someone whose heart recovered normally. Independent of every other risk factor they measured. More predictive than peak heart rate, exercise capacity, or the ST-segment changes that cardiologists had spent decades perfecting.

The metric was called heart rate recovery. What it measured was not fitness. What it measured was vagal reactivation — the speed at which the parasympathetic nervous system could reassert control after the sympathetic system had been activated. It measured autonomic flexibility. The nervous system's ability to shift states.

That is what this book is about.

Not relaxation. Not wellness. Not self-care. Not mindfulness.

The speed at which your nervous system recovers after acute stress — and whether you can train that speed the way you train any other clinical skill.

The answer, supported by evidence across multiple meta-analyses, is yes. HRV biofeedback training improves heart rate recovery by eight to twelve beats per minute over eight to twelve weeks, with effect sizes that clinical researchers describe as large. Breathing training alone — structured, resonant-frequency pacing — improves it by six to ten beats per minute in four to six weeks.

Four to six weeks. Improvements detectable within two to four weeks of consistent practice.

And the measurement device? The Apple Watch on your wrist correlates with clinical ECG at r = 0.92 to 0.95 for one-minute heart rate recovery. The hardware you already own can track the metric that predicts your mortality.

## Inside Out

This book is called *Inside Out* because that is the direction of the training.

Most clinical education moves from outside in. We learn anatomy on cadavers, physiology from textbooks, pharmacology from drug references. We observe experts, then mimic, then internalize. The knowledge flows from the external world into the practitioner.

The training this book describes reverses the direction. It starts inside — with the felt sense of your own heartbeat, the quality of your breath, the tension you carry in muscles you've stopped noticing. It moves outward from there: from body awareness to emotional recognition to situational reading to team co-regulation.

## What This Book Will Ask of You

Sixty seconds, three times a day, for twenty-one days.

Not in a meditation room. Not before your shift. Not after. *During.* In the gaps between cases. In the moments you currently waste or dissociate through. In the sixty seconds that your room resets and your nervous system does not.

You will learn to find your diaphragm — and be surprised that you lost it.

You will learn to hear your heartbeat — and discover that your breathing can be anchored to it.

You will learn to detect the moment your autonomic state shifts — and realize that you've been missing it for your entire career.

You will see the number on your watch change. Heart rate dropping in real time. HRV rising. Not because you believe in the practice. Because the vagus nerve doesn't care what you believe. It responds to the stimulus.

And at the end of twenty-one days, you will have a trained baseline — a place in your nervous system that you can return to. Not calm. Something better than calm. *Functional parasympathetic dominance under load.* The state in which your pattern recognition is sharpest, your decision-making is fastest, your peripheral awareness is widest, and your recovery between stressors is most complete.

The state you were in at the beginning of every shift before the drift began.

The state you can now return to in sixty seconds.
