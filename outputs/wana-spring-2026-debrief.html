Audience Field Data

Show-of-hands polling of ~100 CRNAs, students, and educators. These are not survey results; they're real-time room reads, which arguably carry more weight: people raising their hand in front of peers reveals genuine baseline, not survey-inflated self-reports.

The breath training sources (trumpet, singing, swimming, yoga) are the detail that lands. Every single person who had formal breath practice got it from a non-clinical discipline. Clinical education produces zero respiratory self-regulation skill, in a profession that manages other people's airways all day.

Key Moments in the Room

The Heart Rate Question
"I wonder if my heart rate is higher or lower than the patient's right now."
First reaction: a nervous giggle. Then silence as the room considered both the importance and the novelty of the question. They had never been prompted to sense their own physiology in relation to a patient's. The nervous laughter revealed recognition; they know the question matters, they've just never been given permission to ask it.
First Audience Comment
"I think you're onto something here."
Pre-product-market-fit language. The audience is at the awareness stage; they recognize the gap the moment it's named, but haven't yet demanded a solution. This is exactly the right stage for a conference talk: plant the seed.
Post-Talk: Student with POTS
"I have POTS and I get overwhelming anxiety as I push my patient in the gurney to the OR."
A student CRNA approached after the talk. She has Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome, an autonomic condition, and experiences overwhelming anxiety during the specific transition of wheeling a patient to the operating room. Her husband is a baseball player who taught her performance anxiety and stress management skills from his sport. She's literally cobbling together tools from an entirely different domain because clinical education gave her nothing. This is the user story: a clinician with a diagnosable autonomic condition, doing clinical work that triggers it, forced to borrow tools from her spouse's athletic career because the CRNA profession offers zero standardized training for exactly this scenario.
Post-Talk: Second Student
Both students felt the topics were highly relevant and resonant.
A second student also approached, expressing appreciation. Neither student had heard of interoception before the talk. Both immediately recognized the gap in their training once it was named. This is the pattern: the need is invisible until someone articulates it, and then it's obvious.

Strategic Notes

Accreditation Constraint
Pausality™ was not mentioned by name during the Day 1 talk for accreditation/CE compliance reasons; the talk needed to remain educational, not promotional. The plan was to introduce Pausality more directly in Day 2 conversations. This is actually a strength: the content landed on its own scientific merit without any product pitch, which means the audience response data is uncontaminated by commercial framing.
Audience Composition
Mix of practicing CRNAs, SRNA students, and educators. This is the ideal propagation audience: students carry the concepts into their training programs, educators can integrate them into curricula, practitioners validate clinical relevance. One talk seeds three channels.

What I Learned Giving This Talk

1. The Interoceptive Blind Spot Is Deeper Than Expected

0.5 out of 100 advanced-practice clinicians recognizing "interoception" is not a knowledge gap; it's a cultural blind spot. The word doesn't appear in CRNA curricula. The concept isn't just untaught; it's invisible. This means the first job of any SRL content or talk is vocabulary creation: give people the word before you give them the practice.

2. The Body Opens the Door Faster Than the Brain

The heart rate question produced an immediate physiological shift in the room; the nervous giggle followed by silence was a collective interoceptive moment. The audience felt something before they understood something. That sequence (feel, think, reframe) is the pedagogical order that works. Don't start with theory; start with a body-level prompt.

3. Clinicians Are Already Cobbling; They Need Standardized Tools

The POTS student borrowing baseball performance psychology from her husband is the market in miniature. Clinicians who recognize the gap are already solving it with whatever they can find: yoga, sports psychology, music training, spouse's expertise. SRL's opportunity isn't to create demand; it's to standardize and professionalize what people are already doing in ad hoc, unvalidated ways.

4. Students Are the Most Receptive Audience

Both post-talk conversations were with students, not established practitioners. Students are in formation; their clinical identity is still being shaped. They're also the most likely to carry concepts forward into their programs and cohorts. Future conference strategy: always include content that speaks directly to students, not just credentialed practitioners.

5. Separating Science from Product Works

Not mentioning Pausality™ on Day 1 was a constraint that became an advantage. The audience responded to the science, the framework, and the self-awareness prompts; not a product pitch. This confirms the Communication Philosophy: earn the relationship through depth, not pitch. The accreditation constraint forced the purest possible test of the content, and it passed.

6. "Nervous Giggle Then Silence" Is the Signature Moment

The heart rate question response pattern (giggle, then genuine silence) is the tell that you've landed something real. The giggle is the defense mechanism (this is uncomfortable); the silence is the processing (wait, this matters). Future talks should be designed to produce exactly this sequence. It's the moment of awareness-before-technique, and it's the emotional core of the whole talk.

7. Show-of-Hands Beats Survey Data for This Audience

Using show-of-hands rather than formal polling was the right call. It made the data visible to the audience themselves; they could see that nobody else raised their hand either. That collective visibility is what turns a data point into a shared realization. The room saw its own blind spot in real time. Future talks should always include at least one show-of-hands moment for this reason.
