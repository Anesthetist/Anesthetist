## Definition

Contemplative neuroscience studies the neural, physiological, and psychological effects of meditation, mindfulness, and related contemplative practices using the tools of modern neuroscience. It investigates how sustained contemplative training changes brain structure (neuroplasticity), autonomic function, attentional capacity, emotional regulation, and interoceptive awareness. For SRL, this discipline provides the evidence base for why structured attention-to-body practices produce measurable physiological and cognitive change.

## Sub-Questions (Chapter Outline)

1. **The Davidson revolution** — Richard Davidson's founding of the Center for Healthy Minds. The first fMRI studies of long-term meditators. Altered traits vs. altered states. How contemplative neuroscience became a legitimate field.

2. **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction** — Kabat-Zinn's MBSR program and its evidence base. The Goyal et al. (2014) JAMA meta-analysis (47 RCTs). Effect sizes for anxiety, depression, pain. Why MBSR is the most-studied contemplative intervention.

3. **Neural correlates of meditation** — Default mode network (DMN) deactivation. Increased connectivity in salience network and central executive network. Insula thickening. Lazar et al. (2005): meditation and cortical thickness. Fox et al. (2014): meta-analysis of meditation neuroimaging.

4. **Meditation styles and their distinct effects** — Focused attention (shamatha), open monitoring (vipassana), loving-kindness (metta), nondual awareness. Lutz et al. (2008) taxonomy. Different practices, different brain signatures, different clinical applications.

5. **Contemplative practice and interoception** — Meditation enhances interoceptive accuracy and sensibility. Bornemann et al. (2015): ReSource Project showing interoception training through contemplation. Farb et al. (2015): interoceptive awareness in mindfulness. This is the primary bridge to SRL.

6. **HRV and meditation** — Does meditation increase vagal tone? Krygier et al. (2013): vipassana and HRV. Ahani et al. (2014): mindfulness and HRV. The mechanism question: is HRV change a mediator or just a correlate of contemplative benefit?

7. **Dose-response and minimum effective dose** — How much practice is enough? Brief vs. extended meditation effects. Creswell et al. (2014): 3-day mindfulness intervention. How SRL's "one minute" claim maps to the dose-response literature.

8. **Contemplative practice in clinical populations** — MBCT for depression relapse (Teasdale, Segal, Williams). MBSR for chronic pain. Loving-kindness for PTSD (Kearney). Where the evidence is strong vs. overhyped.

9. **Yoga and breath-based contemplative practices** — Yoga as contemplative practice (not just exercise). Pranayama as attentional regulation. Yoga nidra (non-sleep deep rest). Streeter et al. (2012): yoga and GABA. Bridge to D29 (Body Cultivation).

10. **The replication crisis and critiques** — Van Dam et al. (2018): problems with mindfulness research. Small samples, poor controls, expectancy effects. What survives critical scrutiny and what doesn't. How SRL should navigate this.

## SRL Bridge Points

- **SRL is contemplative training without the spiritual container.** The vault has the application layer (NeuroMinutes, contemplative progressive overload) but not the research canon that legitimizes it. Phase 3 closes this gap.
- **Dose-response data** directly informs the "one-minute" NeuroMinute claim. Creswell's work on brief interventions is the evidence anchor.
- **The interoception-contemplation bridge** (Farb, Bornemann) is arguably the most important single connection in the vault. It says: meditation works *because* it trains interoception. That's SRL's core thesis.
- **The replication crisis critique** (Van Dam 2018) must be in the vault to preempt skeptics. SRL's credibility depends on knowing what survives scrutiny and citing accordingly.
