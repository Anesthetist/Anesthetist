## The Resilience Training Landscape

Before I get to what we're proposing, it's worth surveying the full territory of how elite organizations train resilience — because every approach shares the same structural limitation, and that limitation is exactly what creates the research opportunity.

**U.S. Military — Comprehensive Soldier Fitness (CSF) and Master Resilience Training (MRT).** The Army's flagship program teaches cognitive reframing, emotional regulation, and growth mindset through the PERMA model (Seligman). Evidence shows modest improvements in self-reported resilience and reduced PTSD symptom reporting, but effect sizes are small and durability beyond training windows is inconsistent. The gap: CSF is entirely cognitive — it teaches soldiers to *think* differently about stress, not to *regulate autonomically* during it.

**U.S. Military — BUD/S and SERE.** Navy SEAL selection and Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape training use extreme stress exposure to build psychological hardness. The evidence for selection validity is strong — these programs identify people who can endure — but the evidence for trainability of resilience through pure exposure is mixed. Attrition rates suggest the approach selects for existing capacity rather than building it. The gap: no biofeedback, no interoceptive training, no dose optimization.

**NASA — Astronaut Psychological Training.** Long-duration mission preparation includes conflict resolution, team cohesion protocols, and behavioral health monitoring. NASA has invested heavily in crew compatibility screening and remote psychological support. The gap: astronaut resilience training addresses interpersonal and cognitive dimensions but does not include real-time autonomic regulation training or biofeedback-closed-loop interventions.

**FBI and Secret Service — Stress Inoculation Training (SIT).** Federal law enforcement uses graded exposure to simulated threat scenarios — active shooter simulations, hostage negotiations under time pressure — to build stress tolerance. The evidence base from Meichenbaum's SIT framework is well-established for anxiety disorders and shows transfer to operational contexts. The gap: SIT is session-based (requires dedicated simulation time), not workflow-integrated, and uses no physiological measurement to guide dosing or assess autonomic state during training.

**Aviation — Crew Resource Management (CRM) and Line-Oriented Flight Training (LOFT).** Commercial aviation's systematic approach to human factors training — communication protocols, decision-making frameworks, and full-mission simulation — has demonstrably reduced accident rates over four decades. CRM is the gold standard for team performance under pressure. The gap: CRM addresses cognitive and communicative dimensions of performance but does not monitor or train the autonomic states that underlie decision-making quality. A pilot in sympathetic overdrive will perform the CRM protocol with degraded cognition, and the protocol has no mechanism to detect this.

**Surgical and Medical — Crisis Resource Management and TeamSTEPPS.** Healthcare adapted CRM into Crisis Resource Management for OR teams, and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality developed TeamSTEPPS as a team training framework. Simulation-based training improves team communication and reduces certain error categories. The gap: like aviation CRM, these programs train the cognitive and behavioral layer without addressing the autonomic substrate. A surgeon or anesthetist in undetected state drift will execute the checklist while their decision-making capacity is physiologically degraded.

**Elite Athletics — USOC Mental Performance and F1 Conditioning.** The U.S. Olympic Committee employs sport psychologists who teach visualization, arousal regulation, and attentional focus. Formula 1 drivers undergo cardiovascular conditioning specifically to maintain cognitive performance under sustained G-forces and heat stress. The gap: elite sport comes closest to autonomic training (F1 conditioning explicitly targets cardiovascular fitness for cognitive performance), but the interventions are session-based, require dedicated training time, and are not designed for integration into the performance workflow itself.

**First Responders — Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM) and PsychArmor.** CISM provides structured post-incident debriefing for fire, EMS, and law enforcement. PsychArmor offers online resilience training modules. CISM has mixed evidence — some studies suggest it may not reduce PTSD incidence and could interfere with natural recovery. The gap: these are reactive (post-event) rather than proactive, and operate entirely in the cognitive-verbal domain. No physiological measurement, no biofeedback, no dose optimization.

**Healthcare-Specific Programs:**

- **RISE (Johns Hopkins)** — peer support for second victims of medical error. Evidence shows improved emotional processing and reduced isolation. Gap: reactive, not proactive; addresses the aftermath rather than the autonomic antecedents.
- **Stress First Aid (National Child Traumatic Stress Network)** — a continuum-of-care model for recognizing and responding to stress in self and others. Evidence supports the framework's face validity and adoption. Gap: cognitive assessment framework, no physiological measurement.
- **Mindful Practice (Rochester)** — Ronald Epstein's program teaches reflective practice and mindful clinical engagement. Some of the best evidence for sustained clinician wellbeing improvements. Gap: requires significant time commitment (8-week MBSR-style format), not workflow-integrated, not biofeedback-guided.
- **HeartMath (various hospital systems)** — the closest existing approach to what we're proposing. Uses HRV coherence biofeedback and brief breathing protocols. Evidence for HRV improvements and self-reported stress reduction. Gap: HeartMath's coherence metric is proprietary and debated in the psychophysiology literature; the program is not dose-optimized for clinical workflow integration; and it lacks the interoceptive assessment and training components that the MAIA-2 literature now makes possible.

## The Gap They All Share

Every program I've described shares four structural limitations:

1. **Cognitive-first.** They teach clinicians to *think* about stress differently — reframe, reappraise, debrief. But cognitive reappraisal is a prefrontal cortex function, and the PFC is precisely what degrades under acute sympathetic activation. The strategy most recommended for regulation is least available when most needed. The literature now suggests a temporal sequence: somatic regulation first restores PFC capacity, then cognitive strategies become available (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Zaccaro et al., 2018).

2. **Session-based.** They require dedicated time away from clinical work — workshops, simulations, retreats. For a CRNA managing a full OR schedule, the ask of "attend an 8-week mindfulness program" is often impractical. The 30-40 naturally occurring transition moments in a typical CRNA shift (between cases, walking to the OR, after critical events) represent an untapped training surface.

3. **Open-loop.** None use real-time physiological measurement to guide the intervention. A clinician performing a breathing exercise has no objective feedback on whether it's working — whether their HRV is actually recovering, whether their RMSSD is rebounding. The HRV biofeedback literature (Laborde et al., 2022, meta-analysis of 223 studies) demonstrates that closed-loop feedback produces superior autonomic outcomes.

4. **Not dose-optimized.** No program applies the pharmacological principle of minimum effective dose. What is the shortest intervention that produces a measurable autonomic shift? The literature jumps from 1-minute diagnostic tests to 5-minute therapeutic protocols. The range between 1 and 5 minutes is unmapped.

## The Interoceptive Suppression Hypothesis

Here's what I think is the most consequential — and most testable — idea in the program.

Medical and nursing education systematically trains clinicians to suppress their own body signals. The hidden curriculum — stoicism, emotional detachment, powering through fatigue, the "bladder of steel" as badge of honor — views the body as a machine to be operated, not a sensor to be tuned. I'm proposing that this suppression reduces interoceptive sensibility, produces autonomic inflexibility, and constitutes a primary biological mechanism underlying clinician burnout. Not a character flaw. A training artifact.

The causal chain: (1) the hidden curriculum suppresses interoception, (2) suppression attenuates the capacity to detect autonomic state changes, (3) without reliable interoceptive feedback the autonomic system loses fine-grained self-regulation capacity, and (4) chronic autonomic inflexibility manifests as what we call burnout.

I searched PubMed systematically for this intersection — interoception, interoceptive suppression, medical education, clinician burnout as a biological training artifact. Zero results for the full construct. The individual components are well-established: MAIA-2 is validated across 37 studies (Bravo et al., 2025 COSMIN review confirms it as the best body awareness measure available); alexithymia prevalence in healthcare workers runs around 14.8% (De Berardis et al., 2023) and correlates with burnout at r = 0.41 (Riethof et al., 2020); Mensinger et al. (2024) demonstrated that HRV biofeedback restores interoceptive awareness in healthcare workers. But nobody has put the full chain together and tested it as an integrated causal model.

This is wide open, and the instruments to test it already exist.

## Three Proposed Studies

I've designed three studies that build on each other. The first is the gateway — it enables everything that follows.

### Study 1: MAIA-2-CRNA Validation + ISH Cross-Sectional Test
**N = 240 (80 CRNAs, 80 pre-CRNA nursing students, 80 matched non-clinical controls)**

This study does two things simultaneously: validates a CRNA-specific adaptation of the MAIA-2 interoceptive awareness instrument, and tests the core ISH prediction — that clinical training attenuates interoceptive capacity in a dose-dependent manner.

Measures include MAIA-2 (with supplementary CRNA-context items), heartbeat detection task, TAS-20 for convergent validity, resting HRV, physiological stress response during a standardized stress task paired with subjective stress ratings (the ISH discrepancy measure — the gap between what the body is doing and what the clinician reports feeling), MBI, and years of clinical training exposure.

Primary hypotheses: CRNAs will show lower MAIA-2 scores than non-clinical controls (expected d = 0.4-0.6); CRNAs will show larger physiological-subjective discrepancy during stress tasks (expected d = 0.5-0.7); and years of clinical training will predict lower interoceptive scores, supporting a dose-response relationship.

Power analysis: n = 64 per group for d = 0.5, alpha = 0.05, power = 0.80. With 80 per group, we have room for multivariate models.

**Estimated cost: $40-60K. Timeline: 12-18 months from IRB approval.**

Publication targets: *Journal of Psychosomatic Research*, *Academic Medicine*, *Psychophysiology*.

### Study 2: State Drift Continuous Monitoring
**N = 60 CRNAs, within-subjects repeated measures, 3 consecutive shifts**

This study names and operationalizes what I've been calling "state drift" — the progressive, often undetected loss of autonomic regulation across a clinical shift. The few existing studies (Li et al., 2022; Zhan et al., 2024) show intra-shift HRV changes, but nobody has mapped the full trajectory with concurrent cognitive performance and subjective state data.

Sixty CRNAs wear continuous HRV monitors (Polar H10 or equivalent) across three consecutive shifts. Measures include continuous RMSSD and SDNN in 5-minute epochs, hourly subjective ratings, Psychomotor Vigilance Task at shift start/mid/end, shift characteristics (case complexity, critical events, break frequency), and post-shift nocturnal HRV via Oura Ring.

Primary hypotheses: RMSSD decreases across the shift (expected slope: -2 to -5 ms/hour); HRV drift magnitude correlates with end-of-shift cognitive performance decline; critical events accelerate drift; and nocturnal RMSSD on post-shift nights is lower than off-day nights.

**Estimated cost: $60-90K. Timeline: 12-18 months.**

Publication targets: *AANA Journal*, *Applied Ergonomics*, *Frontiers in Neuroergonomics*.

### Study 3: Gap Moment Training Pre/Post Pilot
**N = 60 CRNAs (30 intervention, 30 waitlist control)**

A 21-day pilot of Structured Interoception Training — five daily 60-second sessions delivered through naturally occurring workflow transitions. Pre/post measures: MAIA-2, RMSSD, MBI, professional engagement. This generates the effect size estimates needed to power the definitive RCT.

The hypothesis: brief structured interoception training (5 minutes total per day, delivered in 60-second increments within clinical workflow) produces measurable increases in both interoceptive awareness and autonomic flexibility within 21 days.

**Timeline: 12 months. Cost dependent on delivery platform.**

## The Nursing Theory

One more piece of context. Over the past several months I've formalized the theoretical framework underlying all of this as a middle-range nursing theory — *Somnistics: A Middle-Range Nursing Theory of Autonomic Self-Regulation in High-Acuity Practice*. It proposes that autonomic self-regulation is a trainable clinical competency, that its systematic suppression during medical education constitutes a primary mechanism of burnout, and that structured interoception training is the corrective intervention.

The theory generates 13 falsifiable hypotheses, extends Benner's Novice-to-Expert model with an autonomic dimension, specifies the regulatory mechanism implicit in Roy's Adaptation Model, and returns to Nightingale's original insight that the environment of healing includes the healer's own body.
