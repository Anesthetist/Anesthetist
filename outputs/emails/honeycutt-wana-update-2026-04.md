# Brief for Crystal Hunnicutt, VCU
## What I Learned at WANA and Where the Research Opportunity Is
### April 2026

---

Crystal,

I spoke at the WANA Conference on March 28 to a room of about 100 CRNAs, students, and educators. Nearly all doctorally prepared. The talk was called "Inside Out: Interoception and High-Performance for CRNAs." What happened in that room changed how I think about the research program we have been discussing.

## What the Room Told Me

I asked the audience two questions.

"Have you heard of interoception?" One hand went up. Halfway. Out of roughly 100 doctorally prepared clinicians.

"Has anyone had formal breath training?" Four hands. A trumpet player, a singer, a swimmer, and a yoga practitioner. Zero from clinical training. Not one person in the room had received structured breath training from nursing school, CRNA school, or any clinical education pathway.

Then I asked: "I wonder if my heart rate is higher or lower than the patient's right now?" The room laughed. Then they got quiet. They had never been prompted to sense their own physiology in relation to a patient. The first comment from the audience: "I think you're onto something here."

## Five Domains That Are Completely Untapped

What I walked away with is a conviction that there are five domains of clinical competency that are unacknowledged, untrained, and wide open for research.

### 1. Applied Breath Training for Clinical Practice

Not yoga breathing. Not meditation. Applied breath practice specifically designed for clinical environments where you have 60 seconds between cases and need measurable autonomic recovery in that window.

The military has invested heavily in this space. Tactical Combat Casualty Care teaches box breathing. Navy SEAL selection uses it. The Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program trains 1.1 million soldiers in resilience skills. NASA trains astronauts in psychological resilience for long-duration missions. The FBI uses stress inoculation training with graded exposure to simulated threats.

Every one of these programs shares a structural limitation: they are cognitive-first, session-based, open-loop, and not dose-optimized. They teach people to think about stress differently. They do not train the autonomic nervous system to regulate itself in real time. They require dedicated time away from the job. They use no physiological measurement to guide the intervention. And none of them have answered the basic question: what is the minimum effective dose of breath training that produces a measurable autonomic shift?

CRNAs manage other people's airways and ventilation all day long. They have more applied respiratory physiology knowledge than almost any other professional group. And yet they have received zero formal training in using their own breath as a regulation tool. That gap is not a knowledge deficit. It is a cultural suppression pattern embedded in clinical education. It is trainable and it is measurable.

### 2. Inner Dialogue Training for Clinical Practice

Every clinician has a running inner dialogue during cases. After a difficult airway, after a code, during a long complex case. That dialogue is untrained. Nobody teaches clinicians how to work with their own self-talk under pressure.

The sports psychology literature has decades of research on self-talk and performance. Elite athletes train their inner dialogue as deliberately as they train their bodies. Positive self-talk improves endurance performance. Instructional self-talk improves skill acquisition. Motivational self-talk improves confidence under pressure. The mechanisms are well documented.

In clinical practice this domain does not exist as a formal competency. There is no curriculum for it. There is no assessment for it. Clinicians develop their inner dialogue patterns through experience, often picking up maladaptive patterns like catastrophizing, rumination, or emotional suppression without ever being taught an alternative. When a CRNA walks out of a room after losing a patient, the inner dialogue that follows is the single strongest predictor of whether that experience becomes growth or trauma. And it is completely untrained.

This is addressable. It is measurable. And combined with breath training, it creates a two-channel regulation system: the body (breath) and the mind (inner dialogue) working together. That is what the military and NASA programs are missing. They train cognition without training physiology, or they train physiology without training cognition. Nobody trains both channels simultaneously in a format that fits inside clinical workflow.

### 3. Interoceptive Awareness Across the Eight MAIA-2 Dimensions, Interculturally

The MAIA-2 (Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness) measures eight dimensions of body awareness: Noticing, Not-Distracting, Not-Worrying, Attention Regulation, Emotional Awareness, Self-Regulation, Body Listening, and Trusting. It is validated across 37 studies and confirmed by a 2025 COSMIN review as the best body awareness measure available.

Nobody has studied how these eight dimensions present in CRNA populations. Nobody has studied how clinical training shapes each dimension over the course of a career. And nobody has studied the MAIA-2 interculturally across clinical populations in different healthcare systems and cultural contexts.

My hypothesis, which I laid out in the research proposal I sent you, is that clinical education systematically suppresses interoceptive capacity. The hidden curriculum of stoicism, emotional detachment, and powering through fatigue trains clinicians to ignore their own body signals. This suppression reduces the capacity to detect autonomic state changes. Without reliable interoceptive feedback the autonomic system loses fine-grained self-regulation. And that chronic autonomic inflexibility is what we call burnout. Not a character flaw. A training artifact.

The MAIA-2 gives us the instrument to test this across all eight dimensions, across career stages, and across cultures. The cross-cultural dimension is particularly important because the relationship between body awareness and clinical practice may differ significantly across healthcare systems that have different norms around emotional expression, somatic awareness, and professional stoicism.

### 4. Emotional Granularity and Amplitude Training

The fourth untapped domain is emotional precision. Ask a CRNA how they feel after a difficult case and the answer is almost always "fine" or "stressed." Two words for the entire spectrum of human emotional experience. The research on emotional granularity shows that people who can name what they feel with specificity regulate better than people who operate with a blunt vocabulary. Lisa Feldman Barrett's work demonstrates that emotional granularity is a skill, not a trait, and that it directly predicts emotional regulation capacity. Brene Brown cataloged 87 distinct human emotions and experiences in Atlas of the Heart. The MAIA-2 measures this as Emotional Awareness, dimension five of eight.

But naming is only half of it. Amplitude training is learning to detect the intensity of an emotional state at the physiological level. Not just "I am anxious" but sensing the difference between low-grade unease and acute dread in the body, before it reaches the threshold where it degrades clinical performance. This is interoceptive literacy applied to the emotional domain. A clinician who can distinguish between frustration and moral distress at the somatic level, before either one compounds, has a fundamentally different relationship to their own stress than a clinician who lumps everything under "tough day."

This is trainable. It is measurable with existing instruments. And in a profession where "I'm fine" is the default answer to every wellness check, it is radically underdeveloped.

### 5. Somatic Discharge and Peer Recovery Practices

The fifth domain is physical. Clinicians accumulate tension in the body across shifts. The psoas, the hip flexors, the jaw, the shoulders. This is not metaphorical. It is measurable in EMG studies of sustained postural loading. The tension patterns that build during a 12-hour shift of standing, leaning over airways, and absorbing the physiological stress of critical cases do not resolve on their own. They accumulate.

Structured somatic discharge practices, where clinicians engage in brief physical release exercises together, offer a peer-based recovery channel that is neither therapy nor meditation. It is closer to what athletes do after competition: a structured cool-down that helps the body process what it absorbed. The neurogenic tremoring literature, the somatic experiencing literature, and the body-based trauma processing literature all point in the same direction: the body needs a physical channel to discharge accumulated stress, not just a cognitive one.

The key distinction is that this is not treatment for trauma. It is proactive maintenance. Something a team can do together in five minutes after a difficult case. It builds co-regulation between colleagues. It normalizes physical recovery as part of clinical practice. And it creates a peer support channel that does not require a therapist, a referral, or a diagnosis. The question "can we take five minutes to shake it off together before the next case?" is fundamentally different from "do you need to talk to someone?" One builds a team culture of physical recovery. The other implies something is wrong with you.

## The Early Onset Burnout Model

The military and NASA both recognize that high-acuity professionals develop performance degradation patterns early, not just at the end of long careers. The Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program was designed proactively, before soldiers deployed, not reactively after they came home broken. NASA trains astronauts in resilience before the mission, not after.

Healthcare does the opposite. We wait for burnout to manifest and then offer EAPs, therapy referrals, and wellness webinars. By the time a CRNA is symptomatic, the autonomic damage has been accumulating for years.

What I am proposing is an early onset burnout prevention model modeled on how NASA and the Army approach the problem: proactive, physiologically measurable, embedded in training from day one, not bolted on after the damage is done. Start with student registered nurse anesthetists. Measure their MAIA-2 profiles at program entry. Train breath regulation, inner dialogue, interoceptive awareness, emotional granularity, and somatic discharge as formal clinical competencies alongside pharmacology and airway management. Measure again at graduation. Follow them into practice.

If we can show that clinicians who receive structured training in these five domains maintain higher interoceptive awareness, better emotional granularity, stronger autonomic flexibility, more effective self-talk patterns, and lower burnout scores across their careers, that changes how CRNA education works.

## What I Think This Means for Our Collaboration

These five domains form an integrated clinical competency model:

| Domain | What It Trains | Current State in CRNA Education |
|--------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| 1. Applied breath training | Autonomic regulation through structured respiratory practice | Zero formal training. 4/100 at WANA had any breath training, all from non-clinical sources. |
| 2. Inner dialogue training | Cognitive self-regulation under pressure through trained self-talk | No curriculum exists. Clinicians develop patterns through unguided experience. |
| 3. Interoceptive awareness (MAIA-2) | Detection and interpretation of internal body signals across eight dimensions | 1/100 at WANA had heard of interoception. The term does not appear in CRNA curricula. |
| 4. Emotional granularity and amplitude | Precision in identifying and sensing the intensity of emotional states | Default vocabulary is "fine" and "stressed." No assessment, no training pathway. |
| 5. Somatic discharge and peer recovery | Physical release of accumulated tension through structured team practices | Does not exist. No protocol, no culture, no language for it in clinical settings. |

None of these five domains appear in any CRNA curriculum I have found. None are assessed. None are trained. All are supported by existing evidence from adjacent fields: military resilience, sports psychology, interoception science, emotional granularity research, somatic processing literature. The gap is not that the science is missing. The gap is that nobody has assembled it into an integrated model and applied it to clinical anesthesia education.

I believe I am the person to do this. Twenty-eight years managing autonomic nervous systems in operating rooms. A structured knowledge graph with 472 evidence notes and 165 clinical concepts built to library science standards. A live product (Pausality, in the App Store) that delivers 60-second biometrically adaptive protocols. An IRB pilot protocol already drafted: 40 CRNAs, 6 weeks, HRV biofeedback plus interoceptive awareness training, estimated budget $30,000 to $37,000.

The three studies I proposed in my earlier correspondence map across all five domains:

| Study | Domains Tested | What It Answers |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Study 1: MAIA-2-CRNA Validation (N=240) | Interoceptive awareness, emotional granularity | Does clinical training suppress interoceptive and emotional awareness capacity? Dose-response across career stages. Cross-sectional with intercultural comparison. |
| Study 2: State Drift Monitoring (N=60) | Applied breath training, somatic discharge | Does autonomic regulation degrade across shifts? Can we measure it continuously? Does structured physical recovery between cases alter the trajectory? |
| Study 3: Gap Moment Training Pilot (N=60) | All five domains | Does 60-second structured training integrating breath, inner dialogue, interoceptive awareness, emotional precision, and somatic discharge produce measurable autonomic and psychological improvement in 21 days? |

These five domains are completely untapped in clinical anesthesia. Together they present a research program that is novel, fundable, and immediately relevant to every CRNA training program in the country. The instruments exist. The theoretical framework exists. The product exists. What we need is the academic partnership to run the studies.

I would welcome a conversation about next steps.
