Current Evidence
Mechanism
The Respiratory Component of the Vasovagal Cascade
Mennitto et al. · Psychosomatic Medicine · 2020 · RCT, n=160
This one caught my attention because of what you described. It turns out that even subtle hyperventilation — without any change in breathing rate — drives CO₂ depletion that contributes to the vasovagal cascade. Controlled breathing stabilizes end-tidal CO₂ and addresses the respiratory component of presyncope, which most standard workups don't look at. Changes in end-tidal CO₂ predicted vasovagal reactions (OR = 0.57, p = .001). The interesting part: it was the depth of breathing that mattered, not the speed. Which lines up with what you've been doing.
RCT · Published February 2026
Five Weeks of Breathing Training Improves Baroreflex Sensitivity
Bellocchi et al. · European Journal of Medical Research · 2026 · Sham-controlled RCT
This just came out last month, and it's probably the most directly relevant paper for your situation. Five weeks of inspiratory muscle training produced +4.7 ms/mmHg baroreflex sensitivity (p=0.01), +20.5 n.u. vagal HRV (p=0.02), along with improvements in expiratory time, anti-inflammatory markers (IL-10), and sleep quality. The baroreflex is essentially the feedback loop that catches blood pressure before it drops too far during a vagal surge — which is exactly what's happening in vasovagal episodes. What's encouraging about this study is the timeline. Five weeks of training, measurable improvement in the specific system that matters.
Framework Shift
From Relaxation to Regulation
Giorgi & Tedeschi · Acta Neurologica Belgica · 2025 · Scoping Review
This is the paper that shaped a lot of how I think about this. The field has been shifting away from "how do we calm people down" toward something more nuanced: autonomic flexibility — the ability of the nervous system to shift smoothly between sympathetic and parasympathetic states as the situation demands. For someone like you, with those wide HR swings during hockey, the interesting question isn't whether you can regulate — clearly you can. It's whether your switching speed is getting better or worse over time, and whether you can see that in the data. You already had that insight when you said lowering heart rate isn't the goal. This paper gives the framework for why that instinct is right.
Validation · RCT, n=547
Breathing Outperforms Physical Counterpressure
Mennitto et al. · Transfusion · 2018 · RCT
A nice large RCT that directly compared respiratory control versus applied muscle tension versus a combination. The breathing group was significantly less likely to require treatment for vasovagal symptoms, and the effect was actually stronger in people with lower baseline anxiety — which, given your profile, is worth noting. The way I think about it: counterpressure maneuvers are useful in the moment, during a prodrome. But breathing training changes the baseline over time, so episodes become less frequent in the first place. Both have a role, but they're solving different problems.
A Couple of Things Worth Thinking About

You raised some good questions in your conversation with Jason, and I wanted to share how I think about them — not as a pitch, just as context from someone who sits in this world every day.

On the Question of Beta Blockers
It Depends on What You're Trying to Solve
Beta blockers are great tools — I use them clinically. But they work by blunting the sympathetic response, and for someone whose episodes are driven by a parasympathetic surge (which is what vasovagal syncope is), the picture gets more complicated. Your resting heart rate of ~65 with vasovagal episodes suggests you probably have fairly high baseline vagal tone. That's actually a good thing from a cardiovascular standpoint — but it means when the vagal brake fires at the wrong moment, the drop can be dramatic. A beta blocker would lower your HR ceiling during hockey without really addressing the baroreflex — the feedback loop that detects a blood pressure drop and corrects it before you feel the prodrome. What's interesting about the Bellocchi study is that baroreflex sensitivity specifically is what improved with breathing training. So the question isn't really beta blockers versus breathing — it's whether you want to dampen the response or train the system to respond more accurately. Both have a place. They're just doing different things.
On the Size of the Opportunity
Your Experience Is More Common Than Most People Realize
You went through an extensive neuro and inner-ear workup and came out without a clear diagnosis. That experience is, unfortunately, incredibly common. Autonomic dysfunction tends to hide behind other labels — vertigo, anxiety, fatigue, exercise intolerance, unexplained syncope. The estimates in the literature are striking: roughly 70 million Americans have some form of dysautonomia, POTS alone affects 1-3 million, and vasovagal syncope is the single most common cause of fainting, touching up to 40% of people at some point in their lives. We chose to start with nurse anesthetists and anesthesia providers because they already understand this physiology deeply — they manage other people's autonomic nervous systems every day. They're a natural starting point for building clinical credibility. But the tools we're developing — for measuring and training autonomic flexibility — are relevant to a much wider group of people who are going through exactly what you went through: something is off, the standard workup doesn't quite explain it, and the answers end up being closer to physiology than pharmacology.
Specific Techniques to Explore
  • 1
    Resonance Frequency Breathing
    ~5.5–6 breaths per minute. This maximizes respiratory sinus arrhythmia and entrains the baroreflex. A 2025 meta-analysis found that on-screen biofeedback with shorter daily sessions (<20 min) produced the strongest effects. The key is finding your individual resonance frequency — it varies person to person, and it's the frequency where your heart rate variability is maximally amplified.
  • 2
    Inspiratory Muscle Training
    Resistance breathing using a threshold device. Directly strengthens the diaphragm, improves the mechanical pump affecting venous return, and — per the Bellocchi data — improves baroreflex sensitivity. This addresses both the Valsalva avoidance you're already practicing and the underlying autonomic responsiveness.
  • 3
    Salt & Hydration Optimization
    Current guidelines for autonomic dysfunction: 10–12g salt/day with 2–3L fluid to expand plasma volume. For my kid, this was foundational — it gave the nervous system enough hemodynamic buffer to stop overcorrecting. Simple. Boring. Effective.
  • 4
    Graduated Exercise Reconditioning
    The Modified Dallas Protocol starts with recumbent exercise and progressively loads the autonomic system. A 2025 systematic review found HR improvements across all studies, with 86% of adolescents reporting resolved or significantly improved symptoms at 5-year follow-up. The principle scales — structured cardiovascular load teaches the baroreflex to respond faster. You're already doing this with hockey. The question is whether a structured ramp alongside it accelerates the improvement.
