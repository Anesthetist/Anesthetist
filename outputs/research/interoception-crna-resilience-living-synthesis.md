## **Introduction**


Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists (CRNAs) operate in high-stakes, high-pressure clinical environments where split-second decisions can be life-saving. The intense responsibility, long hours, and emotional toll of anesthesia care contribute to **chronic stress and burnout** at alarming rates. Recent analyses indicate burnout prevalence among CRNAs ranges widely (12–72% depending on setting) and spiked during the COVID-19 pandemic . Nearly 39% of nurse anesthetists met clinical burnout criteria at the pandemic’s height, with many others reporting high disengagement and exhaustion . Beyond burnout, CRNAs may also become “**second victims**” in the aftermath of adverse events – suffering guilt, shame, self-doubt, or even post-traumatic stress when a patient is harmed despite the provider’s best efforts . Indeed, *almost 9 in 10* CRNAs believe that formal peer support and professional debriefing should be standard practice following such critical incidents . These data underscore the urgency of strengthening professional **resilience** – the capacity to withstand and grow from stress – among nurse anesthetists.


Amid these challenges, a promising insight emerging from neuroscience and mind-body medicine is the role of **interoceptive awareness** in building resilience. **Interoception** refers to the perception of internal bodily signals – the brain’s ability to sense heartbeats, breathing, visceral sensations, and the physiological states that accompany stress or calm. Far from being an esoteric concept, interoceptive processes underlie emotion regulation, stress reactivity, and the sense of well-being . Research over the past decade (2020–2025) has revealed that training individuals to better recognize and modulate their internal signals can improve mental health outcomes and coping capacity . In practical terms for CRNAs, honing interoceptive awareness might translate to earlier recognition of one’s stress responses in the operating room and deploying self-regulation techniques (like controlled breathing or grounding exercises) to maintain composure and cognitive clarity. Recognizing this, even nursing education leaders have called for a paradigm shift: in 2020 the American Association of Colleges of Nursing (AACN) urged nursing programs to move **“from crisis prevention to resilience training”**, integrating healthy self-care behaviors (e.g. mindfulness, emotional insight, stress management skills) into curricula . Enhancing interoceptive awareness may be a key piece of this resilience skill set.

We integrate foundational neuroscience of interoception (from Sherrington’s early definitions to cutting-edge neuroimaging by Khalsa, Critchley, Garfinkel, Schoeller, and others), discuss validated interoceptive **assessment protocols** (especially the Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness, Version 2 or MAIA-2), and review emerging **interventions** like biofeedback, virtual reality (VR), and haptic feedback for real-time stress modulation. By bridging neuroscience and anesthesia practice, this paper aims to equip CRNAs and healthcare leaders with actionable insights to foster resilience and well-being. ## **Background: Interoception and Resilience in the Healthcare Context**


**Interoception** – often called the “hidden sense” – is the body’s inner perception system. It encompasses the neural processes by which we detect, interpret, and integrate signals originating from within our body (heartbeat, breathing, blood pressure, gut motility, temperature, etc.) . The term was first coined by Sir Charles Sherrington in 1906, who defined interoceptive signals as “sensations from the interior of the body, especially the viscera” . Sherrington’s concept primarily contrasted **interoception** (internal sensing) with exteroception (sensing the outside world) . Over the past century, however, science has greatly expanded this definition. Contemporary neuroscience now conceives interoception as a **multidimensional construct**: it is not just about visceral reflexes, but about how the nervous system *senses*, *interprets*, and *regulates* internal states, often integrating these with external environmental information to maintain homeostasis and guide behavior . One useful modern definition describes interoception as *“the process of how the nervous system senses, interprets, and integrates signals originating from within the body”* .


Interoception plays a foundational role in **emotional experience and regulation**. We typically experience emotions along with bodily changes (for example, anxiety might come with a racing heart and tight chest; relief might come with slow breaths and relaxed muscles). The brain’s capacity to accurately perceive those internal changes and attribute meaning to them is critical for identifying and regulating emotions. Indeed, poor interoceptive awareness has been linked with difficulty in emotion awareness and emotion regulation . In one theoretical framework, interoception is viewed as the link between stressors and adaptive responses – it **ties the perturbation of internal state (e.g. a surge of adrenaline) to goal-directed action** (e.g. taking a few deep breaths or seeking help), thereby directly contributing to resilience . Put simply, if one can sense their stress responses early and clearly, one has a better chance to intervene and cope effectively, preventing runaway anxiety or shutdown. On the other hand, blunted or distorted interoception can leave individuals “flying blind” to their own stress, often resulting in maladaptive reactions.


Neuroscientists such as A.D. (Bud) Craig, Hugo Critchley, and Sarah Garfinkel have further refined our understanding by distinguishing different facets of interoceptive awareness. For example, Garfinkel et al. introduced a separation between **interoceptive accuracy** (objective performance in detecting internal signals, as in a heartbeat perception task) and **interoceptive awareness** (the conscious metacognitive insight into one’s interoceptive accuracy) . They also discuss **interoceptive sensibility**, referring to one’s self-reported perception of internal sensation (often measured by questionnaires). This nuance is important for clinicians: a nurse anesthetist might *think* they are very aware of their stress (high sensibility), but objective measures might show poor accuracy in detecting physiological shifts; training can target closing that gap.


Crucially, interoception is not just a passive sensing modality – it has an active regulatory component. Modern views, influenced by **predictive coding and active inference theories**, propose that the brain continuously generates predictions about internal bodily states and updates them with incoming interoceptive signals, aiming to minimize prediction errors. Through this lens, maintaining physiological stability (allostasis) involves an ongoing loop between brain and body: the brain issues commands (like altering heart rate or hormone levels) and monitors resulting internal feedback . Resilience may hinge on how flexibly this brain–body loop can adjust to stress. If interoceptive feedback is processed accurately and non-judgmentally, the individual can deploy appropriate coping (e.g. slowing breathing to quell heart rate, or recognizing anxiety and reframing thoughts). If not, the mismatch can manifest as anxiety disorders, somatic symptoms, or inadequate stress responses. In fact, atypical interoceptive processing has been implicated in conditions like panic disorder, PTSD, depression, and burnout, where individuals either overreact to benign internal sensations or underreact to important signals .


For healthcare professionals, and CRNAs in particular, these interoceptive dynamics are highly relevant. Anesthesia providers must maintain vigilance and clarity under pressure – their **stress responses** (rising heart rate, sweating, rapid breathing) are inevitable in critical moments, but if unchecked can impair cognitive function and decision-making. Becoming aware of those internal cues can enable the CRNA to activate techniques learned in training (e.g. a quick coherence breathing exercise to calm nerves, a mindful grounding in the present task). Over time, regularly tuning into bodily signals can also help providers notice cumulative stress or fatigue before it becomes overwhelming, prompting proactive self-care or help-seeking rather than crisis. In essence, interoceptive awareness acts as an early-warning system and a modulator for the **stress–resilience balance**.


It’s also important to situate interoception in the context of the **“second victim” phenomenon and trauma response**. After a medical error or adverse event, a provider may experience intense autonomic arousal (e.g. heart pounding, nausea) and emotional distress (guilt, fear) . These internal signals can condition a lasting trauma response if not processed. Strategies that incorporate interoceptive awareness – such as a guided debrief that includes noticing one’s bodily state and deliberately engaging calming techniques – may help mitigate the imprint of the trauma on the nervous system. The AORN Second Victim Toolkit explicitly includes content on understanding what affected individuals experience physiologically and psychologically, and recommends support interventions that likely tap into recalibrating those internal signals (through peer support, counseling, structured time-outs for recovery, etc.) . In summary, interoception forms a bridge between the **biological** and **psychological** aspects of resilience: it is the means by which the “mind” and “body” communicate about stress, and thus offers a leverage point for interventions at both individual and organizational levels.


## **Literature Review: The Neuroscience of Interoception (2020–2025)**


**Interoceptive neuroscience** has rapidly advanced in the past five years, painting an increasingly detailed picture of how body–brain communication underpins emotions, decision-making, and resilience. Cutting-edge research from 2020–2025 has focused on mapping the specific neural circuits of interoception, exploring interoception’s role in mental health, and developing technologies to modulate interoceptive processes. This section reviews key findings and theoretical developments, highlighting contributions from leading researchers (Khalsa, Critchley, Garfinkel, Schoeller, and others) that are particularly relevant to understanding and improving CRNA resilience.

**Neural Pathways and Hubs:** It is now recognized that interoceptive signals travel from body to brain through multiple channels – including the vagus nerve and other autonomic fibers, spinal thalamic pathways, and more – engaging a distributed set of brain regions. At the subcortical level, relay stations like the brainstem and thalamus distribute interoceptive input. A key cortical target is the **insula**, a region buried in the lateral sulcus, which acts as an integration hub for internal sensations. The anterior insula, in particular, is often highlighted as the seat of subjective feelings – the place where raw sensations are transformed into conscious “gut feelings.” Neural models suggest the insular cortex plays a central role in maintaining mental health and **resilience**, by integrating interoceptive information and coupling it to appropriate regulatory responses . For instance, individuals with stronger insula activation or structural connectivity in interoceptive tasks often show greater emotional stability and stress tolerance. Conversely, altered insula function is associated with anxiety, depression, and PTSD, linking aberrant interoception to vulnerability for these conditions .


In a landmark *Trends in Neurosciences* review (2021), Berntson and Khalsa outlined the **ascending and descending circuits** of interoception . They noted that while early conceptions (like Sherrington’s) emphasized simple reflex loops for internal regulation, recent evidence points to extensive **cortical and subcortical networks** that enable higher-order interpretation of bodily states . The anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex form part of a salience network that tags interoceptive changes as emotionally significant, while the prefrontal cortex can exert top-down modulation (for example, reassessing a sensation as “not dangerous” and thereby dampening the stress response). There is a *reciprocal loop*: not only do body-to-brain signals affect brain state, but the brain’s expectations and emotions (e.g. worrying thoughts) can alter bodily physiology through descending autonomic pathways . This two-way circuitry means that **interoception is inherently bi-directional** – it is both *sensing* and *regulating*. It also underscores why simply “powering through” stress without awareness can be detrimental: unacknowledged signals can amplify (heart rate climbs higher, cortisol surges) if the brain is not actively engaging its calming pathways. Resilience, therefore, may depend on robust connectivity and balance in these interoceptive circuits, allowing efficient detection of internal changes and prompt activation of corrective, soothing responses.


**Interoception and Emotion:** Hugo Critchley and colleagues have long studied how interoceptive processing influences emotional experience. One often-cited study found that individuals with higher interoceptive accuracy (measured by heartbeat detection tasks) show greater activation in the insula and report more intense emotional experiences – suggesting that feeling one’s heart pound more vividly might amplify feelings of anxiety or excitement. However, if harnessed, this sensitivity could also mean better emotion regulation: those who know *exactly* when they are getting worked up can apply strategies sooner. Critchley’s work, along with that of Sarah Garfinkel, has distinguished between merely sensing internal changes and being able to *contextualize* them appropriately. For example, feeling one’s heart race could be interpreted as a panic (“something is wrong with me!”) or as a normal response (“my body is getting ready to act”) depending on one’s interoceptive awareness and cognitive framing. This is highly pertinent to a CRNA managing an acute crisis – the goal is to sense the adrenaline but channel it as focus, not panic. Studies by Garfinkel et al. (2015) demonstrated that people can be trained to improve their interoceptive accuracy and that doing so might reduce anxiety symptoms . As we’ll explore later, biofeedback interventions that provide real-time heartbeat or breathing cues are direct descendants of these findings, aiming to strengthen the brain-body feedback loop for better emotional control.


**Interoception and Mental Health:** The period 2020–2025 saw an explosion of research linking interoceptive dysfunction to various mental health disorders – and importantly, suggesting interoception as a target for therapy. Khalsa and colleagues published a comprehensive “roadmap” for interoception in mental health (Biol. Psychiatry: CNNI, 2018), outlining how anxiety, depression, and even psychosis involve altered interoceptive signaling . For instance, anxiety disorders may involve a heightened attention to bodily sensations coupled with catastrophic interpretations, whereas depression might involve a blunted interoceptive responsiveness (diminished ability to derive pleasure or arousal). A 2021 review in *Biological Psychiatry* went further to propose that boosting interoceptive awareness could help *prevent* mental illness by providing a sense of control and embodied self-awareness that buffers stress. One empirical finding supporting this is the link between **heart rate variability (HRV)** – an objective metric of autonomic flexibility and often considered an interoceptive indicator – and resilience. Individuals with higher resting HRV (indicating a well-regulated autonomic nervous system with strong parasympathetic tone) tend to handle stress better and have lower rates of PTSD after trauma . HRV is thus seen as both a biomarker of interoceptive health and a potential training outcome (since practices like slow breathing can increase HRV). A recent systematic review noted that *“HRV is not only linked to interoception but is also a biomarker of stress and can be an indicator of resilience under stressful situations”* . This convergence of evidence has made HRV biofeedback a particularly attractive tool – it quantitatively connects body signals to perceived stress and can be used to train one’s interoceptive regulation (we discuss this more in the interventions section).


**Technological and Experimental Innovations:** Another exciting frontier (to which Felix Schoeller and colleagues have contributed) is the use of technology to modulate interoceptive experience. Researchers are devising **“interoceptive technologies”** – devices and software that can feed sensory signals to users to alter their interoceptive state . For example, Schoeller et al. (2019) described devices that provide **direct interoceptive modulation**, such as vibrating at the rhythm of one’s heartbeat or manipulating respiratory patterns, as well as devices that create **illusory feedback**, such as false heartbeat sounds, to trick the brain . The rationale is that by artificially boosting or perturbing interoceptive signals, one can engage the brain’s plasticity to eventually improve natural interoceptive awareness or correct maladaptive patterns. In one set of experiments, exposure to controlled *“aesthetic chills”* (a visceral shiver response induced by music or VR) was used to study and enhance emotional responses via interoceptive pathways . In another, as we will detail later, *haptic biofeedback* (vibrations synced to the heartbeat) was found to significantly improve interoceptive accuracy after just a single training session . Virtual Reality has also been combined with biofeedback to create immersive scenarios where one’s bodily signals are part of the virtual environment. For instance, a VR program might display the user’s heartbeat as a glowing orb that enlarges with stress – training the user to consciously shrink the orb through breathing techniques, thereby learning to control the actual heartbeat. These novel approaches, while in early stages, show the potential for **enhancing interoception through engaging, sensorily rich methods** beyond traditional meditation or breathing exercises.


**Summary of Relevance:** The neuroscientific advances from 2020–2025 reinforce that interoception is *fundamental* to how we experience ourselves and stress. They highlight key brain areas (like the insula) and metrics (like HRV) that can serve as targets or indicators for interventions. They also provide a mechanistic understanding: interoception links the brain’s predictions and the body’s responses in a continuous loop, and strengthening this link can yield better emotional stability and adaptability. For nurse anesthetists, these findings underscore why an *integrated mind-body approach* to resilience is warranted. Traditional cognitive training (e.g. cognitive-behavioral strategies for stress) can be powerfully complemented by interoceptive training (e.g. learning to sense and modulate one’s internal arousal). ## **Validated Assessment Protocols for Interoceptive Awareness**


To integrate interoceptive training into professional development, we first need robust ways to **assess interoceptive awareness**. Accurate assessment allows identification of baseline strengths or deficits, and provides outcome measures to track improvement. Over the past decade, researchers have developed and refined several tools – both self-report questionnaires and objective tasks – to capture the multifaceted nature of interoception. The most widely used self-report instrument is the **Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA)**, recently updated to **MAIA-2**, which provides a comprehensive profile of how an individual experiences and relates to their internal signals. This section will focus primarily on MAIA-2 and its eight domains, and briefly mention other relevant measures, highlighting their validation and utility in both research and clinical settings.


**Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA-2):** Originally published in 2012 by Mehling et al., the MAIA filled a void for a patient-reported outcome measure of interoception. The latest Version 2 (MAIA-2) is a 37-item questionnaire with 8 distinct subscales, each tapping into a different dimension of interoceptive experience . Importantly, MAIA-2 was carefully validated and refined (adding five items to the original MAIA) to improve its psychometric robustness . The eight MAIA-2 scales and the core aspect they assess are :

- **Noticing** – Awareness of uncomfortable, comfortable, and neutral bodily sensations (e.g. *“I notice where in my body I feel comfortable”*). This measures basic perception of sensations .
- **Not-Distracting** – Tendency not to ignore or distract oneself from pain or discomfort. High scores mean one stays present with unpleasant sensations rather than looking away .
- **Not-Worrying** – Tendency not to worry or experience emotional distress with uncomfortable sensations. High scorers remain emotionally calm in the face of body discomfort .
- **Attention Regulation** – Ability to sustain and control attention to bodily sensations (the capacity to focus on internal signals at will)  .
- **Emotional Awareness** – Awareness of the connection between bodily sensations and emotional states (e.g. noticing that a tight stomach accompanies anxiety)  .
- **Self-Regulation** – Ability to use attention to body sensations to regulate distress (effectively, calming oneself by tuning into the body)  .
- **Body Listening** – Active listening to the body for insight, akin to “trusting your gut” or using bodily signals to inform decision-making  .
- **Trusting** – Experiencing one’s body as safe and trustworthy. High scores indicate a positive, secure feeling toward one’s bodily sensations  .


MAIA-2 does **not yield a single total score**; rather, each subscale is considered separately, since one might excel in some areas and struggle in others . For example, a CRNA might score high in Noticing (very aware of all internal signals) but low in Not-Worrying (they become anxious about those signals). That profile would suggest the need to work on decoupling sensation from anxiety – perhaps via cognitive reframing or exposure exercises – rather than on basic awareness per se.


The MAIA-2 has demonstrated solid reliability and validity across diverse populations, including college students, clinical groups, and international samples . The updated version improved internal consistency for most scales (though Noticing and Not-Worrying still have somewhat lower alphas, ~0.65, indicating these constructs can be variable) . Importantly for intervention studies, MAIA scales are **sensitive to change**, meaning they can detect improvements in interoceptive awareness after training . For instance, if nurse anesthetists undergo a mindfulness or biofeedback program aimed at boosting interoceptive skills, we would expect to see pre-post increases in relevant MAIA scales (perhaps Attention Regulation and Self-Regulation in particular). Indeed, research by Price and colleagues using Mindful Awareness in Body-oriented Therapy (MABT) – an intervention to train interoceptive awareness – has shown significant increases in multiple MAIA subscales after training, correlating with improved clinical outcomes .


In practice, a CRNA or SRNA (student) could take the MAIA-2 as a reflective assessment. It takes about 5–10 minutes to complete and asks them to rate statements (Never to Always) about their bodily awareness in daily life . The results could help identify areas to focus on. For example, a low *Not-Distracting* score might reveal a habit of tuning out bodily signs of stress (like pushing through fatigue or hunger on long shifts) – a behavior that, while sometimes necessary, could lead to burnout if not balanced with self-care. A low *Trusting* score might indicate the provider doesn’t feel secure in their body (perhaps after a personal health scare or simply from chronic stress), suggesting that interventions to rebuild a sense of safety in one’s own physiology (such as yoga or progressive muscle relaxation) might be beneficial. Thus, the MAIA-2 can be a conversation starter and a personalized roadmap in resilience coaching for healthcare professionals.


Beyond MAIA, other **assessment protocols** include objective tests like the **heartbeat detection task** (counting or tracking one’s heartbeats without feeling the pulse) which measures interoceptive accuracy, and the **breathing detection task** (noticing changes in breathing resistance). These tasks have been used in lab research to correlate with brain imaging or psychological outcomes . While not as practical for routine clinical use, they provide important validation – for instance, someone who says they are aware of their heartbeat (high MAIA Noticing) should, if accurate, perform reasonably well on a heartbeat counting task; if not, it might reveal more of an anxious hypervigilance rather than true accuracy.


Another instrument worth noting is the **Body Awareness Questionnaire (BAQ)**, an older scale that assesses attentiveness to normal bodily processes. However, the BAQ is less nuanced than MAIA and doesn’t separate adaptive vs. maladaptive awareness. The **Interceptive Confusion Questionnaire** (ICQ) is sometimes used in contexts of alexithymia (difficulty identifying feelings), which overlaps with interoceptive awareness deficits. For trauma-exposed populations, the **Somatic Dissociation Questionnaire** can gauge the extent of feeling disconnected from one’s body – a relevant issue for second victims who may feel “numb” or in a daze after an event.


In summary, **MAIA-2 stands out as the most comprehensive and clinically useful tool** for measuring interoceptive awareness across multiple dimensions. Its eight subscales can guide tailored interventions – for example, a resilience workshop for CRNAs might use MAIA-2 results to group participants into those who need to work on simply noticing body signals vs. those who notice well but need to worry less about them, etc. Using such validated assessments ensures that we approach interoceptive training scientifically, with baseline metrics and outcomes that can be measured. As we turn to interventions, we will see how these assessments tie in: improvements in MAIA scales or objective heartbeat detection can serve as evidence that an approach is truly enhancing the interoceptive aspect of resilience, and not just producing general self-reported benefits.


## **Biofeedback and Real-Time Interventions: From Heart Rate Variability to Virtual Reality**


Developing interoceptive awareness and resilience is not a passive process – it benefits greatly from **active training and real-time feedback**. In recent years, several intervention modalities have been explored and validated to help individuals tune into and modulate their internal states. These include **biofeedback techniques** (where physiological signals are fed back to the user in real time), technology-enhanced training (such as VR simulations that integrate bodily signals), and even tactile or haptic devices that gently prompt bodily awareness. For CRNAs and other healthcare professionals, such interventions hold promise as efficient, skills-based methods to improve stress regulation on the job. This section reviews key approaches: heart rate variability biofeedback (HRV-BF), breathing regulation exercises, VR-based stress inoculation training, and haptic feedback devices – highlighting evidence of their effectiveness (especially from 2020–2025 studies) and how they can be deployed in practice.


**Heart Rate Variability Biofeedback (HRV-BF):** One of the most evidence-based interoceptive interventions is HRV biofeedback, which has users practice controlling their heart rate variability through paced breathing and emotional regulation techniques, often using a sensor and app for feedback. **Heart rate variability (HRV)** refers to the variation in time between consecutive heartbeats; higher variability at rest is generally a sign of a healthy, well-balanced autonomic nervous system (with robust parasympathetic activity), whereas low HRV is linked to stress and poor cardiovascular outcomes. In biofeedback training, individuals typically wear a heart monitor (like a chest strap or finger/ear sensor) and watch a real-time display – for example, a rising and falling graph or a changing color – representing their HRV or a related metric (such as heart coherence). They are then coached to breathe at a slow, regular pace (often around 5–6 breaths per minute) and to **cultivate a positive or calm mental state**, which together can increase HRV. The immediate visual or auditory feedback shows when the desired state is achieved (often termed **“coherence”** when heart rhythms become smooth and synchronized with breathing) .


Studies have found that **HRV biofeedback effectively reduces stress, anxiety, and depression, and improves resilience** across various populations . For instance, a meta-review noted significant anxiety reductions in those who practiced HRV-BF regularly. In a pilot with nurses and nursing students, even brief biofeedback interventions led to decreased self-reported worry and increased resilience scores . Ongoing research during the COVID-19 pandemic targeted frontline healthcare workers, suggesting that HRV-BF could mitigate psychological burden in that high-stress period . The mechanism is straightforward: by **visualizing the normally invisible** (your heart’s beat-to-beat changes) and learning to control it with breathing and emotional cues, you enhance your interoceptive awareness and also strengthen your parasympathetic (vagal) tone. Over time, this translates to an improved ability to **self-regulate under pressure** – your body can more quickly shift into a calm state after a stressor, and you become more aware of early signs of stress. One 2022 nursing education study noted that maintaining parasympathetic dominance (which correlates with high HRV) during rest is crucial for building stress resilience . HRV-BF specifically trains that dominance by teaching techniques to maximize vagal input (slow breathing, positive emotion).


For CRNAs, HRV biofeedback could be incorporated into both training and daily routine. For example, a nurse anesthesia program might introduce HRV-BF in a wellness seminar, having students practice with a smartphone app (several commercially available ones, like Inner Balance by HeartMath or EliteHRV, use simple ear clip sensors). Students could practice achieving coherence in increasingly stressful simulations – e.g., maintaining steady breathing and HRV while performing a difficult airway scenario in a sim lab – thereby linking the skill to real clinical stressors. On the job, a CRNA could use a 5-minute HRV-BF session during a break or before a challenging case to center themselves. There are even wearable devices (like certain smartwatches or dedicated biofeedback devices) that can give discreet feedback via vibration or on-screen prompts if your physiology indicates rising stress, cueing you to take a moment to breathe.


The beauty of HRV-BF is that it quantifies progress. A provider can see their coherence percentage or HRV score improve over weeks of practice, which can be motivating and reassuring (e.g., “Last month I could hardly get my coherence above 50%, but now I can reliably hit 80% – my body is learning to relax faster”). It externalizes the interoceptive process in a game-like way. However, one must also **wean off external feedback** eventually – the goal is to internalize the skill so that in the operating room, one doesn’t need a phone app to know to slow down breathing when stressed. Many protocols therefore use HRV-BF daily for a period (say 4–6 weeks) and then encourage users to practice the breathing and emotion techniques without always relying on the device.


**Breath Training and Respiratory Biofeedback:** Closely related to HRV-BF are breathing-based interoceptive trainings. Breathing is one of the few visceral processes we can control willfully, and it has powerful leverage on the autonomic state (fast, shallow breathing can provoke anxiety; slow, deep breathing induces calm). Simple breathing exercises – like the **4-7-8 breathing** or box breathing (4 seconds in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold) – are often taught in stress management. These can be enhanced with biofeedback by using capnography (feedback of CO2 levels) or a device measuring respiratory sinus arrhythmia (the HRV changes with breathing). One emerging concept is **respiratory interoceptive exposure**: deliberately focusing on one’s breath even during slight air hunger or stress, to increase tolerance of internal sensations. This might help providers who experience panic symptoms (e.g. feeling unable to breathe when anxious) to remain grounded.


For CRNAs, who are of course experts in managing ventilation for patients, learning to harness their own respiratory patterns can be empowering. One could imagine, for instance, a quick exercise before a high-anxiety case: the CRNA steps aside for one minute to do 6 slow diaphragm breaths, perhaps using a phone app that visually guides the breath timing. This can **reset** their physiological arousal. There are also breathing/meditation apps (like Calm or Headspace) that include body-scan meditations, which systematically build interoceptive awareness by guiding attention through various internal sensations. Even a few minutes a day of such practice can increase scores on interoceptive scales and reduce perceived stress, according to some mindfulness research.


**Haptic Biofeedback and Wearable Tech:** A fascinating development in 2024 was the demonstration that **haptic (touch) feedback of one’s heartbeat can enhance interoceptive awareness more effectively than visual feedback** . In a controlled study (Dobrushina et al., 2024), participants received real-time feedback of their heartbeats either through a gentle vibration on the chest (haptic condition) or as an animated heart on a screen (visual condition). After a single session, the group that felt the vibrations showed a significant improvement in their ability to accurately perceive their heartbeats (measured by a heartbeat discrimination task), as well as increased confidence in their interoceptive sense . The visual feedback group did not show such improvements. Participants also found the haptic feedback more pleasant and reported that it drew their attention inward to their bodily sensations more than the visual feedback did . This suggests that aligning feedback with the **natural sensory modality** of the heartbeat (we normally *feel* heartbeats as a thump in the chest, not see them) makes the training more intuitive and potent.


From a practical standpoint, this result hints at a new generation of **wearable devices for clinicians**. Imagine a comfortable patch or band that a CRNA could wear which vibrates subtly with each heartbeat or perhaps lights up a small LED – providing a private cue of their state. When one is calm and coherent, heart rate and its variability might fall into an optimal pattern (perhaps indicated by a steady gentle vibration). If one becomes stressed, the heart might race or the vibration pattern might change noticeably, cueing the anesthetist to acknowledge the spike and take steps to control it (like taking a brief mental pause, activating a breathing exercise). There are already early versions of such technology: for example, some smartwatches give stress alerts based on heart rate patterns, and devices like the Apollo Neuro (a wearable band) provide vibrations meant to promote calm via somatosensory stimulation. The research by Amedi’s team in Israel (the 2024 study) provides scientific backing for these approaches, showing that *directly feeling* one’s bio-signals can strengthen the brain-body connection.


Additionally, haptic feedback could be integrated into **simulation training**. Picture a scenario in a simulation center where, as the sim grows more complex or as the participant’s stress level rises, their vest or chair delivers corresponding haptic signals (maybe even simulating tremors of one’s heartbeat). The trainee must learn to remain composed and work through those internal sensations. While this might sound futuristic, it aligns with the concept of stress inoculation – exposing individuals to manageable levels of physiological stress in training so they are more prepared in real life. Haptic feedback could thereby become a resilience training tool, teaching CRNAs to function with a heightened, but controlled, interoceptive state.


**Virtual Reality (VR) and Immersive Training:** VR offers a unique platform to combine multiple elements – visual, auditory, and interoceptive feedback – in a controlled environment. There has been experimental work using VR for anxiety treatment where patients face stressful scenarios (like virtual heights or public speaking) while practicing calming techniques. For healthcare, one could use VR to recreate operating room stressors (monitors beeping, high-pressure decisions) and simultaneously provide the trainee with biofeedback within the VR display. For example, a VR could show a **“resilience meter”** that goes up when the user’s physiological readings (heart rate, skin conductance, etc.) indicate calm, and down when stress rises. The user’s challenge is to keep the resilience meter up while managing the clinical scenario – reinforcing the interplay of maintaining internal control while executing tasks.


Researchers like Riva et al. (2021) have even discussed **Regenerative Virtual Therapy (RVT)**, where VR is combined with interoceptive modulation and mindfulness to “rewrite” faulty bodily self-representations . In simpler terms, VR can create powerful illusions (like seeing an avatar of oneself) and introduce interoceptive discrepancies (like an out-of-sync heartbeat sound) to promote flexibility in how the brain processes body signals. While much of that line of work is aimed at conditions like eating disorders or chronic pain (where body representation is disturbed), the principles can be applied to resilience training: VR could help clinicians practice shifting from a threat response (“oh no, everything is going wrong”) to a challenge response (“I feel my heart pounding, that means I’m ready to act”) in a safe simulation, reinforcing a resilient mindset.


A concrete example might be a VR module for second victims: The user (a provider who had a recent adverse event) goes through a guided VR experience that might simulate some aspects of the event or related stress, but in a therapeutic narrative – perhaps prompting them to notice their bodily sensations of anxiety, then guiding them through slowing their breathing, maybe presenting calming imagery or sounds, and showing their biofeedback (like heart rate dropping) as a visual flower blooming. Such a module could help process the trauma by pairing recollections of the event with learned regulation responses, potentially reducing persistent hyperarousal triggers in the real OR.


Though VR for CRNA resilience is still a nascent idea, it is highly scalable (especially as VR headsets become more common and affordable) and can be engaging for users who might not otherwise stick with a traditional meditation program. **Gamifying interoception** – whether through VR or smartphone apps – taps into motivation and can demystify the mind-body connection by making it visible and interactive.


**Traditional Biofeedback and Neurofeedback:** It’s worth noting that apart from HRV and respiration, other biofeedback modalities (like skin temperature, muscle tension via EMG, or even EEG neurofeedback) have their place in stress management. Progressive muscle relaxation with EMG feedback can teach recognition of tension (interoceptive in the sense of muscle sense, or proprioception). EEG neurofeedback, which rewards producing certain brainwave patterns, has been used for focus and calm (though its relationship to interoception is more indirect, via attention training). In general, any biofeedback that increases someone’s awareness of their internal state and ability to control it can be considered an interoceptive training tool.


**Evidence of Effectiveness:** The collective evidence from 2020–2025 strongly supports these interventions. To highlight a few findings: A 2023 systematic review of biofeedback interventions in workers found that **mobile app-directed programs were particularly promising for improving resilience and aiding recovery after stress** . A 2022 pilot study in nursing students found even *three sessions* of HRV biofeedback led to statistically significant reductions in worry and improvements in Brief Resilience Scale scores . An ongoing clinical trial (as per ClinicalTrials.gov) is comparing HRV biofeedback, interoceptive exposure training, and mindfulness to see which best reduces stress and improves mental health in high-stress occupations – the results of such research will further refine best practices, but all arms acknowledge interoception’s central role. The Dobrushina et al. study in *Psychophysiology (2024)* provided a rigorous test of haptic vs. visual feedback and confirmed that aligning with the body’s natural sensory channels (touch for heartbeat) yields better awareness gains . Meanwhile, Schoeller et al. (2019) in *Physics of Life Reviews* articulated the vision of **interoceptive augmentation** – they reviewed devices and experiments showing that by feeding controlled interoceptive-like stimuli to people, you can enhance emotional experiences and potentially empathy . They described prototypes like *“the Frisson device”* which induces shivers to boost positive emotions, hinting at eventual tools to induce calming sensations on demand.


For CRNAs, what these studies translate to is a suite of **actionable, tech-enabled strategies** to bolster their resilience:

- **Regular HRV Check-ins:** Using a personal biofeedback device or app at home or during breaks, to practice getting into a coherent state. This could be as routine as doing a “heart-centering” exercise each morning for 5 minutes.
- **On-the-Spot Breathing:** Training with feedback so that in the OR, when an alarm causes a jolt of adrenaline, the CRNA automatically takes two slow breaths to prevent panic. Essentially creating an ingrained response.
- **Wearable Alerts:** Possibly employing devices that nudge the wearer when physiologic stress markers go above threshold – like a smart ring vibrating when your pulse stays elevated, reminding you to employ a technique.
- **VR or Simulation Training:** Participating in periodic simulation drills that include stress-management objectives, not just clinical performance. After a simulated crisis, debrief both the clinical decisions *and* how the person managed their physiology – e.g., “I noticed my hands were shaking when the virtual patient coded; next time I’ll try to remember to use a grounding technique.”
- **Peer Biofeedback Sessions:** Interestingly, groups can do biofeedback too – e.g. a debrief session where everyone synchronizes breathing, visible on a shared screen, building team coherence after a critical event (this has been experimented with in some high-stress team trainings, fostering collective calm).


Incorporating these interventions doesn’t require extensive extra time; rather, it’s about embedding them into existing workflows and training contexts. Over time, the goal is for CRNAs to naturally exhibit high interoceptive awareness and strong self-regulation – to have *“anesthesia provider resilience reflexes”* where their default under duress is to stay aware of their body, use it as an ally (slowing it down or harnessing its alertness), rather than disconnect or be overwhelmed by it.


## **Organizational Strategy: Fostering Resilience and Second Victim Support at the System Level**


While individual training in interoceptive awareness and self-regulation is crucial, a comprehensive approach to professional resilience must also engage the **organizational and cultural level**. Healthcare institutions play a key role in either mitigating or exacerbating clinician stress. Supportive policies, programs, and cultures can make the difference between a single stressful event being growth-inducing versus career-ending. In this section, we discuss organizational strategies aligned with the insights of interoception and resilience science – including the implementation of second victim support toolkits (like the AORN Second Victim Toolkit), fostering a “just culture” that encourages open communication and peer support, integrating resilience and wellness into professional standards, and providing technological and educational resources (like wellness apps or peer support networks) at scale. The alignment of these strategies with interoceptive principles is emphasized; for example, just as an individual must *acknowledge and respond* to internal stress signals, an organization must acknowledge and respond to the collective stress signals of its providers.


**AORN Second Victim Toolkit – Building a Support Program:** The **Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN)** has taken a leading step by developing an evidence-based **Second Victim Toolkit** for hospitals and departments to implement . The purpose of this toolkit is to provide **education and awareness about second victims** and to guide the development of local support programs . Key components include understanding what the second victim experience entails (the psychological and physical symptoms providers may have after an adverse event), how to provide immediate and long-term support, how to measure the impact (via surveys or the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool, SVEST), and how to establish a peer support team . By normalizing the physiological and emotional reactions (e.g. acknowledging that insomnia, replaying the event, increased heart rate at work are common), the toolkit helps remove stigma. It encourages a culture where seeking support is seen as strength, not weakness.


Actionables from the toolkit that align with interoceptive resilience concepts include: creating “**safe zones**” or spaces where clinicians can go to de-escalate (quiet rooms with low lighting, maybe biofeedback devices or aromatherapy on hand); establishing a **critical incident stress debrief protocol** (within 24-72 hours of an event, a trained peer supporter meets with the involved provider to talk through it, including how it felt in their body and mind); providing contact info for counseling or employee assistance programs that are trauma-informed; and educating leaders to recognize signs of second victim distress (like a drop in a provider’s performance, avoidance of certain cases, or hyper-arousal) so they can gently intervene and offer help . The AORN toolkit essentially operationalizes an organizational **interoceptive awareness** – the organization learns to sense when its “body” of staff is in distress and responds supportively.


**Just Culture and Open Communication:** A crucial foundation for any support strategy is a **Just Culture**, which emphasizes that errors are usually system issues or human mistakes rather than willful negligence, and that staff should feel safe reporting and discussing errors. A Just Culture reduces the additional stress of **shame and hiding**, which can otherwise amplify the internal turmoil of second victims (imagine the internal state of a CRNA who is holding in their anxiety and guilt vs. one who has been able to talk about it and was met with understanding). An AORN Journal commentary in 2024 by Croke described strategies for organizations to facilitate second victim recovery **“building on a just culture including counseling services and peer support.”** . This means leadership must explicitly encourage employees to speak up after difficult cases, provide reassurance that they will be supported, and treat the event as a learning opportunity rather than a trigger for automatic punishment.


In practice, a department might implement a **“Second Victim Rapid Response Team”** analogous to a code team – a group of trained peers (e.g., other CRNAs, surgeons, nurses) who are on-call to respond when a colleague experiences a traumatic event. This team can offer immediate on-site support (cover the rest of the provider’s shift if needed, give them a moment to breathe, maybe do a quick grounding exercise or just listen empathically). Peer support has been highlighted as highly desired by CRNAs and indeed many anesthesia departments have started peer support programs. For example, one initiative described in the AANA Journal (Oct 2023) evaluated the use of the SVEST tool two years after implementing a peer support program and found improvements in second victim outcomes and perceived support . This indicates that formalizing such support (not leaving it to chance or ad hoc sympathy) makes a measurable difference – likely by intervening early to prevent acute stress from becoming chronic PTSD or burnout.


**Training and Education in Resilience:** Organizations should also **embed resilience and wellness training into professional development** pathways. The AACN’s new Essentials for nursing education (2021) explicitly include a domain of demonstrating healthy self-care and resilience behaviors . In the CRNA world, the National Board of Certification and Recertification for Nurse Anesthetists (NBCRNA) could consider including wellness/resilience modules as part of continued certification (some of this is already happening informally via conference offerings on provider wellness). Hospitals could offer workshops on mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) or yoga specifically geared for clinicians. Importantly, framing these not just as “stress management” but as **performance optimization** can increase buy-in; for example, “This breathing and grounding training will make you a sharper clinician under pressure.” Given CRNAs are high achievers who value excellence, showing that resilience training improves patient safety (through better focus and reduced errors) can align it with organizational goals.


**Leveraging Technology at Scale:** Healthcare organizations can also leverage technology to support their staff’s resilience on a broad scale. Some large hospital systems have begun offering subscriptions to wellness apps (like Calm, Headspace, or Even Health’s “Cabana” which is group support in VR) to all employees as a benefit. Others have installed relaxation rooms with biofeedback kiosks where staff can take a 15-minute “reset.” The Betsy Lehman Center’s **Peer Support Toolkit** (referenced by AANA Wellness resources) is another valuable compendium – though not interoception-specific, it provides templates for setting up peer support, which is complementary.


We can envision an anesthesia department partnering with developers to create a custom mobile app for their staff that might include: a quick log to track daily stress and recovery (helping providers become more self-monitoring), guided breathing or interoceptive meditations, one-touch access to peer supporters (“press this button if you had a tough case and want to talk to someone within 2 hours”), and educational tidbits on resilience science. Given the familiarity of CRNAs with monitoring and equipment, an app or device framing might actually resonate (turning wellness into another parameter to optimize).


**Policy and Leadership:** On a policy level, professional organizations like the AANA have a role. The AANA has published **Critical Incident Stress Management Guidelines** for CRNAs , which outline a structured approach: post-incident debriefing, peer support tiers, counseling referrals, etc. They also have position statements on issues like substance use and mental health, promoting the view that seeking help is a professional responsibility and not a personal failure. Leadership at anesthesia departments should ensure compliance with work-hour limits, provide adequate staffing to avoid fatigue, and encourage taking time off after especially draining periods (some institutions, for example, mandate a day off after a sentinel event for the involved provider to recuperate). These systemic supports acknowledge that humans have physiological and psychological limits – essentially respecting the “interoceptive data” at the organizational level (e.g., if many providers are calling out sick or showing signs of burnout, that’s akin to a patient’s vital signs indicating distress – the leadership should respond with interventions like it would treat an elevated heart rate).


In sum, an **organizational strategy for resilience** weaves together a culture of safety and openness, concrete support tools like the AORN Second Victim Toolkit, educational initiatives, and resources that empower individuals. The organization’s attitude should be proactive: assume that clinicians *will* face internal turmoil and plan for it, rather than react after someone has already hit a breaking point. By doing so, not only is the well-being of staff protected, but patient care quality improves – a resilient provider is less likely to make errors and more likely to create a healing environment for patients. As one AORN resource put it, supporting second victims ultimately *“promotes recovery and ensures they never have to face these experiences alone.”* This holistic, compassionate approach at the organizational level reinforces and amplifies the interoceptive and resilience skills being developed at the individual level.


## **Use Cases and Implementation: Integrating Interoceptive Training into CRNA Practice and Education**


To illustrate how the concepts and tools discussed can be applied in the real world of nurse anesthesia, this section presents several **use cases and scenarios**. These examples show how interoceptive awareness and resilience training can be woven into various phases of a CRNA’s professional journey – from student training to daily clinical practice to post-incident recovery. The goal is to translate theory into actionable steps and to inspire stakeholders (educators, department chiefs, and CRNAs themselves) to pilot these approaches. Each use case will highlight the challenge, the interoceptive/resilience strategy applied, and the expected or observed outcomes.


**Use Case 1: Simulation-Based Resilience Training for SRNAs**


**Scenario:** A cohort of Student Registered Nurse Anesthetists (SRNAs) is in the simulation lab practicing a high-fidelity crisis scenario (e.g., an anaphylactic reaction during surgery). Historically, faculty noticed that some students become so overwhelmed by stress in these sims that they freeze or make rushed decisions, reflecting poor coping that could carry into real OR events.


**Intervention:** The anesthesia program integrates an interoceptive training component into simulations. Before the scenario, each student is equipped with a wearable that monitors heart rate and possibly displays it on a tablet only they can see. They are briefed on a simple technique: if they notice their heart rate spiking above a certain threshold or feel symptoms of panic, they are to **pause for 10 seconds, take two deep slow breaths, and reconcentrate**. Faculty explicitly state that learning to maintain physiological control is a objective *just as important* as managing the medical problem.


During the simulation, a faculty observer also keeps an eye on the student’s level of agitation. If the student appears locked up or extremely flustered, the faculty member may trigger a “time-out” (simulating perhaps a consult call) to prompt the student to take a moment, check in with their body, and use the breathing technique. After the scenario, the debrief addresses not only clinical decisions but also asks the student: *“What were you feeling in your body when the crisis hit? Did you notice your heart pounding or breath holding? How did you bring yourself back?”* The students share strategies and normalize that everyone felt a surge of adrenaline.


**Outcome:** Over several sessions, students report increasing confidence in handling stressful simulations. One student might say, “I was about to blank out when the vitals dropped, but I remembered to breathe and surprisingly it cleared my head.” Their performance improves not just from repetition of scenario content, but from improved self-regulation – they can think more clearly under pressure. MAIA-2 surveys administered at semester start and end show significant improvements in **Attention Regulation** and **Self-Regulation** scales for the cohort, indicating they gained skill in focusing on internal states and using them for regulation. This use case highlights that **resilience skills can be taught experientially**, and that simulation labs – already a staple for technical skills – can double as safe spaces to practice psychological skills.


**Use Case 2: Pre-Shift Centering and Continuous Wearables in Clinical Practice**


**Scenario:** A CRNA working in a busy urban hospital often starts the day with multiple high-acuity cases. Mornings are hectic – between preop assessments, machine checks, and addressing surgeons’ questions, the CRNA frequently enters the first case already tense, with a racing heartbeat, which sometimes doesn’t subside until midday. This leaves them drained by afternoon. They wish they could “start off calmer” but feel there’s no time.


**Intervention:** The department leadership introduces a 5-minute **Pre-Shift Centering** routine for all anesthesia staff. One of the ORs is set aside as a “warm-up room” from 6:30–6:35am. A simple guided exercise is overhead-paged or played on speakers – for example: *“Close your eyes, stand or sit comfortably. Take a deep inhale… exhale… Now scan your body from head to toe, releasing any tension…”* including a brief breathing synchronization (sometimes using box breathing or a coherent breathing count). Many CRNAs attend; it’s made optional but culturally encouraged. Additionally, the hospital provides interested CRNAs with a wearable smart device (like a ring or fitness band) that tracks stress indicators (heart rate, HRV). These devices are set to **vibrate gently** when the wearer’s heart rate stays above a personalized threshold for over 2 minutes (indicating sustained stress) or when HRV falls into a low zone.


During the day, as the CRNA manages cases, they receive these subtle biofeedback nudges. For instance, while preparing for a particularly challenging case, the CRNA’s ring vibrates – they realize they’ve been rushing and breathing shallowly. Having been trained what the alert means, they take 30 seconds to do their personal centering technique (maybe a quick breathing exercise or recalling a calming mantra like “I’ve got this”). They notice their heart rate number on the monitor (if visible) drops from 110 to 90. They proceed with setup but with a more measured demeanor. The CRNA also makes use of brief moments between cases: instead of immediately diving into the next task, they might step into a quiet corner and practice the **4-7-8 breathing** cycle twice, effectively resetting their interoceptive baseline.


**Outcome:** Over several weeks, the CRNA finds they no longer feel utterly exhausted by noon. Colleagues comment that they seem more “steady” even on hectic days. If a critical incident occurs, the CRNA is more apt to remember to call for help early (not getting tunnel vision) and to keep their voice and hands steady during the management – they credit the internal awareness for this ability. Data collected from the wearables across the department show an interesting trend: average HRV of providers during cases improved after implementing the program, and self-reported stress scores on end-of-day surveys decreased. This suggests that a combination of structural routine (the pre-shift centering) and continuous feedback can inculcate a **culture of physiological mindfulness** throughout the workday.


**Use Case 3: Post-Adverse Event Recovery – The Second Victim Support Loop**


**Scenario:** A nurse anesthetist experiences a serious adverse event – an unexpected difficult airway leads to a patient’s anoxic injury. Although a root cause analysis finds no negligence (it was a “can’t intubate, can’t ventilate” scenario that the team handled as best as possible), the CRNA is traumatized. In the immediate days after, they suffer insomnia, elevated heart rate, loss of appetite, and replay the scenario mentally on a loop . They are dreading returning to work and especially to any case that might trigger memories.


**Intervention:** The hospital’s **Second Victim Support Program** swings into action. Within hours of the event, the CRNA’s manager and a trained peer supporter (another senior CRNA) reach out. The manager assures the CRNA that the focus is on their well-being and offers them the next day off without penalty. The peer supporter meets the CRNA that evening for a decompression session. Using principles from the AORN toolkit, the peer creates a safe space for the CRNA to recount what happened, including *how it felt*. The CRNA shakily describes the moment they realized the patient wasn’t ventilating and how their “heart was pounding out of my chest” and they felt on the verge of panic. The peer normalizes these responses (“Anyone would feel their adrenaline surge in that situation – that’s your body trying to help you act.”) and perhaps even guides a short breathing exercise together during the retelling when the CRNA becomes overwhelmed. The peer also shares some of their own past second victim experience, emphasizing that *time and proper support helped them recover and return to confident practice*.


The CRNA is given **resources**: written info from the toolkit about common second victim symptoms (so they know their racing heart and hypervigilance are not signs of personal weakness but a known phenomenon ), suggestions for self-care (like engaging in moderate exercise to burn off excess stress hormones, practicing the same breathing exercises to aid sleep), and contacts for professional counseling if needed. Over the next few weeks, the peer supporter checks in regularly, including doing a **graded exposure** plan: first visit the OR together when no case is going on, just to sit and let any anxiety symptoms arise and resolve; later, have the CRNA assist in a routine case with the peer present as backup; and eventually, the CRNA feels comfortable solo again. During this time, the CRNA also uses an app for brief daily relaxation exercises (provided by the hospital’s wellness program).


**Outcome:** Initially, the CRNA had considered leaving the profession (so intense was their distress). But with structured support, they not only continue working, but report about a month later that they feel “more in touch” with their stress signals and more proactive in managing them. They learned through counseling to recognize when their body is entering a fight-or-flight state and to use grounding techniques (like feeling their feet on the floor, or using a quick HRV biofeedback on their phone) to stay present. The hospital’s program records show that this CRNA did not take extended leave or suffer long-term disability from the event, as sometimes happens in unmitigated second victim cases. Furthermore, when a colleague later has a different adverse event, this CRNA volunteers to become a peer supporter, indicating post-traumatic growth and a desire to “pay forward” the help they received. This case exemplifies that **organized, interoceptively-informed support** (addressing the mind-body aspects of trauma) can substantially shorten recovery and even strengthen resilience in the aftermath of a medical crisis.


**Use Case 4: Continuous Professional Development – Resilience Workshops and Apps**


**Scenario:** A group of experienced CRNAs at a national conference attends a workshop titled “Embodied Resilience for Anesthesia Professionals.” Many attendees are mid-career and have felt the strain of COVID surges, increased workload, or personal health issues. They’re curious but some are skeptical – they’ve sat through generic burnout talks before.


**Intervention:** The workshop leaders (including a CRNA and a neuroscientist) take an interactive approach grounded in the science we’ve reviewed. They begin by presenting data: the burnout rates in CRNAs, the link between high stress and errors, but also the emerging science of interoception and resilience – showing, for instance, a graph of HRV improvements with biofeedback and a scan of the brain highlighting the insula’s role in anxiety . They keep slides minimal and focus on *experiential learning*. Attendees are guided through a **body scan meditation** to actually feel, perhaps for the first time that day, where they hold tension (one CRNA might notice “my neck and shoulders are like rock”). Then they do a fun exercise: using provided fingertip pulse oximeters, everyone practices a quick breathing exercise and watches their heart rate on the device slow down – a simple biofeedback demo that invariably gets a few raised eyebrows and “wow” moments.


The leaders introduce the MAIA-2 questionnaire via a quick self-assessment: they choose a few items from each scale and have participants rate themselves by show of hands or using a phone poll. This sparks discussion – e.g., many rate low on *Not-Distracting*, acknowledging they often push through discomfort without pause (a badge of honor in nursing historically). The facilitators point out how that can lead to not recognizing burnout until too late, and suggest small habit changes like doing a 1-minute check-in between cases (What am I feeling? What do I need?). They also discuss *Not-Worrying* – how ruminating on physical discomfort or stress (“I’ll never get over this pit in my stomach”) can worsen outcomes, and how reframing and support can help.


Participants then rotate through stations: one for HRV biofeedback practice (with a device and coach), one for movement-based interoception (some light stretching or yoga focusing on breath with movement), and one as a peer discussion circle sharing stories of near misses or stress and how they coped. Through these, attendees pick up practical techniques and realize they are not alone in their struggles. The workshop ends with each person making a “resilience action plan” – choosing one technique to implement in their routine (like *“I will do the 4-7-8 breathing every day at lunch”* or *“I will use the insight timer app to do a 5-minute body scan before bed”*).


Additionally, the organizers unveil a pilot **mobile app** specifically for anesthesia providers, developed with AANA support. The app includes a MAIA-2 short form quiz, a breathing pacer, guided relaxations of 5, 10, 15 minutes tailored to pre-op, intra-op breaks, post-op wind-down, and a community forum (possibly anonymous) where CRNAs can share tips or seek support (moderated by wellness experts). Attendees are invited to use the app and give feedback.


**Outcome:** The immediate outcome is enthusiastic feedback – many participants say this was one of the most *“real”* and useful wellness sessions they’ve attended, because it gave them concrete tools and made the science relatable. The longer-term hoped-for outcome is behavior change: follow-up surveys three months later find that a good proportion of attendees did integrate at least one new habit (like regular breathing exercises or using the app’s relaxation tracks). Some have even become champions at their workplaces, leading a short version of the body scan in staff meetings or organizing a peer support check-in after tough weeks. The app data shows steady engagement, and the AANA considers rolling it out to all members. This use case demonstrates the value of **ongoing professional development** focusing on resilience – treating it not as a one-off, but as a skill set to be refreshed and built upon, just like clinical skills.


These scenarios collectively show that integrating interoceptive awareness and resilience practices is feasible and beneficial at multiple levels. From training new providers to daily self-care to institutional crisis management, the applications are versatile. Key themes include the importance of **leadership buy-in** (to allow these practices time and space), the need to **normalize and destigmatize** discussions of stress (making it as routine as discussing drug errors or case efficiencies), and leveraging **existing processes** (like simulations, rounds, debriefs) as opportunities to insert resilience exercises rather than always needing standalone events. Moreover, technology – when thoughtfully used – can serve as a bridge to make the invisible visible, which is exactly what interoception work is about.


In implementing these, some challenges might arise, such as skeptics who view these practices as “soft” or not relevant to patient care. It is crucial to keep framing them in terms of patient safety and clinician performance (e.g., fewer errors, faster thinking under stress, better teamwork, reduced turnover) because these are outcomes administrators and providers alike value. Encouragingly, early adopters and success stories (like those above) can create a positive ripple effect, gradually shifting the culture to one that values the wellness of providers as much as the outcomes of patients – understanding that the two are deeply interconnected.


## **Future Directions and Conclusion**


As the understanding of interoception and resilience continues to evolve, so too will the strategies to support nurse anesthetists in their professional and personal well-being. This **living scientific synthesis** is intended to be updated as new evidence emerges. Looking ahead, several key areas merit further research and development:

- **Longitudinal Outcomes:** Future studies should examine whether improvements in interoceptive awareness (as measured by tools like MAIA-2 or HRV metrics) in CRNAs correlate with long-term outcomes such as reduced burnout rates, fewer medical errors, better patient satisfaction, or longer career longevity. Establishing these links would provide powerful justification for institutional investment in resilience training.
- **Tailored Interventions:** Research could identify if certain interoceptive interventions work better for certain individuals. For instance, perhaps high-anxiety providers benefit most from HRV biofeedback, while those with more emotional exhaustion benefit from mindfulness-based practices. A personalized approach – possibly guided by initial assessments (e.g., a CRNA with low Trusting and high Noticing on MAIA-2 might get a different program than one with the opposite profile) – could optimize efficacy.
- **Technology Integration:** The next generation of wearable tech and smart ORs offers exciting possibilities. Imagine an anesthesia machine or OR monitoring system that not only displays patient vitals but also has an indicator for the clinician’s stress level (connected via a wearable) – this could enable the team to support each other dynamically (e.g., a circulator noticing the CRNA’s stress indicator is high might proactively offer assistance or a break). Ethnographic research in OR settings could explore how to implement such feedback loops without causing distraction or stigma. Data security and privacy (ensuring such biometric data isn’t misused punitively) would be an important consideration here.
- **Organizational Policy Research:** It would be valuable to formally evaluate programs like the AORN Second Victim Toolkit implementation across many hospitals to see which components have the greatest impact on staff well-being and what the barriers are in those that struggle to implement. Additionally, studying the cost-benefit (e.g., reduced turnover, fewer sick days) can help build the business case for wellness initiatives.
- **Neuroscience and Biofeedback Innovations:** Ongoing neuroscience research might yield novel biofeedback parameters – for example, *brain-based* interoceptive feedback (if lightweight EEG headbands become feasible, could we train people to modulate their insula activation?). Also, exploring pharmacologic or dietary adjuncts: there is emerging work on whether certain supplements or beta-blockers might enhance interoceptive training by reducing physiological noise. Such adjuncts could potentially help jump-start the learning process for those who struggle initially (though behavioral techniques remain the cornerstone).
- **Resilience Curriculum Accreditation:** Professional bodies (like COA for anesthesia programs or even hospital accreditors like The Joint Commission) could integrate wellness and resilience criteria. Future guidelines may require that anesthesia departments have peer support programs, or that educational programs teach interoceptive awareness. Research and pilot programs undertaken now will shape what those standards should look like.


In conclusion, fostering **professional resilience in nurse anesthetists** is a multi-dimensional endeavor – one that must engage the mind, body, and environment of the provider. The neuroscience of interoception provides a compelling framework to understand how stress and resilience function at a fundamental level, and it points to actionable levers for improvement. By training CRNAs to become more attuned to their internal signals and equipping them with tools to regulate those signals (from a simple breath to a high-tech biofeedback device), we empower them to navigate the challenges of their critical role with greater clarity, composure, and fulfillment. Coupling these individual skills with organizational support systems – such as the Second Victim Toolkit and a culture that prioritizes wellness – creates a reinforcing loop where healthy providers deliver high-quality care, and positive outcomes feed back into further resilience.


The period of 2020–2025 has demonstrated that these approaches are not just theoretical. They are being applied and tested in real-world clinical settings with promising results. Nurse anesthetists who have embraced mindfulness or biofeedback report feeling more present and less reactive at work. Departments that have implemented peer support and resilience workshops see improved morale and cohesion. These are seeds of a larger transformation in healthcare, one that recognizes that caring for the *caregiver’s* inner world is integral to the overall healing mission.


As we continue to update this living synthesis, the hope is to keep CRNAs and other professionals informed of the latest evidence and best practices. In rapidly changing and often stressful healthcare landscapes, one constant should be the commitment to **self-awareness, growth, and mutual support**. By listening to the wisdom of our own bodies – the racing heart, the tight chest, the deep sigh of relief – and by responding with compassion and skill, nurse anesthetists can not only withstand the pressures of the job but also find deeper meaning and satisfaction in their vital work. The long-standing ethos of anesthesia providers has been vigilance for the patient; the emerging ethos adds vigilance for oneself and one’s colleagues, creating a resilient circuit that ultimately benefits all.


In sum, interoceptive awareness and professional resilience are two sides of the same coin: by looking inward, CRNAs fortify themselves to face outward challenges. This integrated approach will help ensure that those at the head of the operating table remain as sharp, steady, and healthy as possible, now and throughout the careers to come.

# ***No interoception training*********


The field of anesthesiology has put considerable focus on provider well-being and burnout prevention, but explicit, structured application of interoception science to provider self-care and resilience is very rare, if present at all. Here’s what the literature and best practices show:

- **Provider Well-Being Initiatives:

** Most well-being curricula, wellness programs, and interventions for anesthesiologists focus on resilience, stress management, peer support, mindfulness, social activities, mentorship, and cultivating awareness of stress/burnout warning signs. Programs encourage self-awareness, self-care, and mental health support, but these are rarely constructed in terms of formal interoceptive training.apsf+4​
- **Burnout & Emotional Regulation:

** There is notable recognition that anesthesiologists require self-care tools for working under pressure, with the emphasis on systemic, organizational, and social support structures. Individual interventions are usually targeted at emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and maintaining a sense of personal accomplishment.openanesthesia+2​
- **Interoception in Practice:

** While interoceptive awareness (the ability to sense and understand internal bodily states) is a theme in wellness (e.g., “situational conscience and mindfulness”), there’s no evidence of widely adopted, explicit interoceptive skills training in anesthesiology residency or staff well-being programs. Some interventions (breathwork, mindfulness, body scan) are described, but the field primarily uses general terminology of “awareness” or “mindfulness,” with interoception not operationalized as a distinct, skill-based concept.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih+3​
- **Conclusions:

**
- While anesthesiology acknowledges the high-stress nature of the work and offers several well-being programs, the direct, scientific application of interoceptive training to provider wellness is almost nonexistent.
- The field would benefit from the integration of structured interoceptive skill building—formal training in noticing, interpreting, and actively regulating bodily signals—to help mitigate the physiological and emotional demands unique to anesthesia providers.actaanaesthesiologica+1​

# ***Current training*********


Coping skill interventions in anesthesiology residency programs are increasingly common and multifaceted, aiming to address the high risk of stress and burnout among residents. Here’s a synthesis of the most prominent approaches:


**1. Structured Wellness Curricula:

** Residency wellness programs often include modular or longitudinal curricula addressing:

- Physician wellness, resilience, professionalism, social/team support, and occupational and emotional well-being.
- Mindfulness, situational awareness, and stress management techniques such as deep breathing and muscle relaxation.
- Encouragement for self-care activities: physical activity, balanced eating, rest, and social engagement.iaapartners+4​


**2. Peer Support and Mentorship:

** Programs incorporate peer mentorship, resident-led wellness committees, and organized group activities to foster community, decrease isolation, and encourage shared learning of coping strategies.unmc+2​


**3. Mental Health and Professional Support:

** Encouragement to seek professional mental health support, routine access to counseling, and development of decompression spaces within hospitals are common approaches.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih+2​


**4. Problem-Focused Coping Promotion:

** Residents are taught to proactively plan, reframe stress, accept circumstances, and seek both emotional and informational support. These “approach coping” strategies are linked to lower burnout compared to avoidant behaviors (denial, disengagement, substance use).sysrevpharm+1​


**5. Teamwork and Organizational Change:

** Efforts to improve work schedules (e.g., shift work), reduce workload, enhance supervision, and strengthen teamwork are considered essential for resilience.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih+1​


**6. Workshops and Simulation:

** Some programs use scenario-based simulation workshops to introduce coping skills in real clinical contexts, improving emotional preparedness and skill application under pressure.asmepublications.onlinelibrary.wiley​


**7. Individualized and Group Interventions:

** Activities range from guided discussions and online classes to social events and stress-reduction workshops. Some programs encourage both individualized (personal mental health, physical care) and group (peer, team, and institutional) interventions.actaanaesthesiologica+2​


**Key Gaps:**

- While “situational awareness” and mindfulness are sometimes mentioned, explicit training in interoceptive awareness, breath regulation, or somatic self-regulation is not routinely found in mainstream programs.pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih​
- Many interventions still rely on individual effort rather than systemic, culture-level shifts, and some studies note potential resistance if the burden is placed solely on individuals without institutional support.wpchange​


In summary, current coping interventions address psychological, social, and organizational dimensions but have yet to widely integrate body-based or interoceptive skill training as a formal component.

Skill domains


Top-down and bottom-up skills are essential for professionals who need to stay calm, recall large bodies of knowledge, and respond effectively to emergencies that arise unexpectedly. These abilities rely on both cognitive strategies (top-down) and embodied regulation (bottom-up), and their integration can powerfully support effectiveness under pressure.youtube​thebehaviorhub+1​


## **Top-Down Skills**


Top-down skills involve using conscious thought processes to manage stress and recall knowledge. Examples include:

- Cognitive reappraisal—acknowledging that discomfort does not necessarily indicate danger and reframing stressors using logic.leftbrainbuddha​youtube​
- Mental rehearsal and scenario-based practice to aid recall of critical knowledge in emergencies.sentinelresilience+1​
- Utilizing spaced repetition and regular review of key information to improve rapid recall during crises.whentoreview+1​
- Simplifying information retrieval by using checklists, prompts, and step-by-step guides, especially when under stress.sentinelresilience​


## **Bottom-Up Skills**


Bottom-up skills help regulate physiological and emotional states, grounding the individual for optimal cognitive performance. Key methods include:

- Breathwork: Slow, deliberate breathing activates parasympathetic nervous system responses, calming fight-or-flight reactivity.northshoreprofessionaltherapy​youtube​
- Movement and posture training: Yoga, physical exercises, and progressive muscle relaxation enhance body awareness and resilience.thebehaviorhub+1​
- Sensory-motor exercises and protocols designed to release stored stress energy and restore nervous system balance.theembodylab+1​


## **Integration for High-Recall Under Stress**


Professionals require a combination of these approaches:

- Realistic simulations and scenario drills bridge the gap between theoretical training and practical emergency response, conditioning both mind and body for optimal recall and regulation under pressure.samatters+1​
- Mindfulness practices improve both emotional control and somatic awareness, making it easier to recall knowledge and act effectively in stressful moments.linda-thai+2​


## **Essential Training Components**

- Cognitive training: Stress inoculation, mental rehearsal, scenario drills, checklist use.
- Somatic training: Breathwork, movement, body scan, progressive muscle relaxation, sensory refinement.
- Practice in realistic, high-pressure environments to foster recall and calm through embodied experience.samatters​


Neglecting these training modalities leaves professionals vulnerable to overwhelm, poor recall, and impaired judgment during emergencies, underscoring the need for integrated top-down and bottom-up skill development in every high-stakes field.youtube​leftbrainbuddha+1​
