# Why a Nurse Anesthetist Built a Breathing App

*The meditation apps kept telling me to "let go." I couldn't. My patient's blood pressure was crashing.*

---

I've spent fifteen years managing autonomic nervous systems. Not my own — other people's. 

As a CRNA (Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetist), I'm the one who keeps you alive while surgeons do their work. I watch monitors that track your heart rate, your breathing, your blood pressure. I notice the moment your body starts drifting in a direction it shouldn't. And I intervene before it becomes a crisis.

It's high-stakes work. And after years of doing it, I realized something uncomfortable: I was better at regulating my patients' nervous systems than my own.

## The Meditation App Problem

Like a lot of healthcare workers, I tried the obvious solutions. Downloaded Calm. Gave Headspace a shot. Set aside time for "self-care" the way HR told us to.

Here's what I learned: most meditation apps are built for people who have 20 minutes to spare and a quiet room to sit in.

I don't have that. Neither do most of the nurses, doctors, paramedics, and healthcare workers I know.

Between cases, I might have 90 seconds. Maybe three minutes if I'm lucky. I'm not going to achieve enlightenment in that window. But I might be able to shift my nervous system from "fight or flight" back to something functional.

The apps didn't help with that. They wanted me to close my eyes and visualize a forest. I was standing in a hallway that smelled like antiseptic, with my pager about to go off.

So I started doing what I do in the OR: I worked with what I could actually measure.

## What I Know About Nervous Systems

Here's the thing about being a nurse anesthetist — you develop a very practical understanding of how the autonomic nervous system works. Not theoretical. Practical.

I know that breathing patterns directly influence heart rate. I know that slow, controlled exhales activate the parasympathetic response. I know that you don't need 20 minutes of meditation to shift your physiological state — sometimes you need 60 seconds of the right breathing pattern.

This isn't mystical. It's not "wellness culture." It's basic physiology that I've watched play out on monitors thousands of times.

The problem was, none of the apps I found were built around this understanding. They were built around content. Hours of guided meditations, sleep stories, music libraries. The assumption was that what you needed was more things to listen to.

What I needed was a tool that would actually measure what was happening in my body and help me shift it.

## Building for People Like Me

I built Pausality because I wanted the app I couldn't find.

It runs on the Apple Watch — the device already on my wrist between cases. It tracks your heart rate during breathing exercises, so you can actually see the impact. No content library. No ten-day courses. Just 21 breathing protocols designed for people who have limited time and high stress.

I didn't set out to compete with Calm or Headspace. I'm not trying to replace meditation. I'm trying to solve a different problem: How do you help someone who can't step away from their high-stakes environment actually regulate their nervous system during the workday?

For me, that meant stripping away everything that wasn't essential. No guided audio that requires headphones. No video content. No gamification designed to keep you in the app longer. Just breathing patterns, real-time heart rate feedback, and practices you can complete in 60 seconds to 5 minutes.

## The Healthcare Burnout Reality

I need to be honest about something: I built this app during a period when I was burning out.

Healthcare burnout isn't new, but the pandemic pushed it to a breaking point that we haven't recovered from. The numbers are brutal — studies show that over 60% of healthcare workers report burnout symptoms. Nurses are leaving the profession in record numbers. And the people who stay are often running on fumes.

The solutions we get offered tend to fall into two categories: "practice self-care" (as if we haven't tried) or "see a therapist" (good luck finding one who takes your insurance and has availability).

What's missing is the middle ground — practical tools that acknowledge the reality of the job. You can't meditate during a code. You can't step away for a yoga class when you're covering a unit. But you might be able to do a 90-second breathing reset between patients.

That's the gap I'm trying to fill. Not a replacement for therapy or genuine rest. Just a tool for the moments when you need to function and your nervous system isn't cooperating.

## What I Got Wrong (And Changed)

I want to be transparent about something: the first version of Pausality was built around HRV — heart rate variability. The theory was sound. HRV is a legitimate marker of autonomic function, and there's real research behind using it for biofeedback.

But in practice, the Apple Watch's HRV readings weren't consistent enough for real-time feedback. Too much variability between readings, too much noise. Users would do the same exercise and get wildly different results depending on factors outside their control.

So I deprecated it. Stripped out HRV and focused on heart rate instead — a simpler metric, but one that the Watch measures accurately and consistently.

It was humbling to build a feature, ship it, and then admit it wasn't working. But I'd rather have an app that does one thing well than one that promises more than it can deliver.

## For Healthcare Workers (And Everyone Else in High-Stakes Work)

I started building Pausality for nurses and doctors because that's my world. But I've learned that the problem is broader than healthcare.

Anyone in a high-pressure role — first responders, teachers, lawyers, executives, parents — knows what it feels like when your nervous system is stuck in overdrive. The racing heart when you're trying to fall asleep. The irritability that leaks into your relationships. The sense that you're always one stressor away from snapping.

These apps aren't built for people like us. They're built for people with time.

So I built one for people without it.

Pausality is $49.99/year and requires an Apple Watch. It's not for everyone. But if you've tried the meditation apps and they didn't stick — if you need something that works in the reality of your day, not in some idealized version of it — this might be what you're looking for.

You can find it at [pausality.health](https://pausality.health) or in the App Store.

And if you're a healthcare worker who's burning out — I see you. This job asks too much of us. But maybe we can figure out how to survive it together.
