What you describe—the struggle to translate an inner world of images, colors, and complex thoughts into the linear stream of speech—is exactly what many with **childhood apraxia of speech (CAS)** or late speech experience. Let me unpack a few dimensions of this:  

---

## 1. Mechanistic View (Neuro/Clinical)
- **Speech apraxia** is not about weakness of the tongue or lips—it’s a motor planning disorder. The brain has the thought, but the neural pathway that sequences the muscle commands to produce sounds is unreliable.  
- Areas involved:  
  - **Broca’s area** (left inferior frontal gyrus) → motor planning.  
  - **Supplementary motor area** → sequencing.  
  - **Insula & basal ganglia** → fine-tuned coordination.  
- The “translation gap” between thought and speech is real. Many kids (and adults) with apraxia report knowing exactly what they want to say, but being unable to get it out smoothly.  

---

## 2. The Stress of Social Speech
- With one person, your brain can keep up: you can pause, gesture, and be understood.  
- With more than one, the demands multiply—keeping track of conversational flow, timing your entry, shaping the words. This often triggers **performance anxiety** layered on top of the motor challenge.  
- The intensity you describe is common: speech feels less like communication and more like trying to align multiple “gears” at once under pressure.  

---

## 3. The Strategy You Chose (Studying Prosody)
- Prosody (rhythm, melody, pauses, stress) is one of the most *forgiving* aspects of speech—humans are tuned to pick up on it even when articulation is imperfect.  
- By studying others’ intonation, you built a scaffold: almost like learning the music of speech first, then laying words into that rhythm.  
- Many with apraxia or dysfluency naturally gravitate to this—imitating cadence, tone, and body language—to mask or support their expressive challenges.  

---

## 4. Cognitive Cost
- You were essentially **running two simultaneous translations**:  
  1. Rich imagistic thought → compressed into words.  
  2. Words → rehearsed rhythm/intonation so they’d “land.”  
- That’s why it felt exhausting and stressful—it was double encoding.  
- Over time, this likely gave you unusual sensitivity to **pattern, timing, and flow of human speech**—a gift, but one born of necessity.

## Origins and Conceptual Foundations of Somnistics  
Somnistics originated through the personal and professional journey of its founder, whose life experiences spanned speech disorder, cross-cultural learning, and medical training. As a child, the founder struggled with **apraxia** – a motor speech disorder that impaired the brain’s ability to plan and execute speech movements. Apraxia often leaves individuals with monotonic or “robotic” speech due to disrupted prosody (intonation and rhythm)【47†L115-L123】. Grappling with this, the founder discovered that focusing on **prosody-based learning** – emphasizing the melody and rhythm of speech – dramatically improved their articulation and fluency. This echoed clinical observations that techniques like Melodic Intonation Therapy (which uses musical intonation and rhythm) can engage alternate neural pathways to compensate for speech motor deficits. By practicing speech at slowed tempos and with exaggerated intonation, they effectively retrained their brain’s speech circuits. This insight later became encapsulated as the **“Half-Speed Principle”** – the idea that performing an activity at half speed can embed precision and awareness, laying a stronger foundation for full-speed performance. In motor learning research, it is well known that slowing down practice can improve accuracy and neural encoding, which in turn leads to better performance when speed is reintroduced【34†L23-L30】【34†L25-L27】. The founder’s early experience thus revealed that **slowing down time** (subjectively) could paradoxically speed up learning – a theme that would reappear in Somnistics training.
