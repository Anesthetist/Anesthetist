## 1) Model the whole thing as a feedback control loop
- **Plant (the system to control):** clinician’s autonomic state (ANS), cortical arousal, attentional field.
- **Setpoint:** “operational baseline” (calm vigilance, wide peripheral vision, steady hands).
- **Sensors:** Apple Watch PPG/HR/HRV; (optionally) pupillometry via front camera; subjective RPE/clarity.
- **Controller:** NeuroMinute™ protocol (breath cadence, holds, prosody, haptic pacing, exteroceptive cues).
- **Actuators:** respiratory mechanics, ocular/peripheral vision cues, posture prompts, brief self-talk.
- **Disturbances:** case complexity, noise, time pressure, interpersonal friction, sleep debt, caffeine, alarms.
- **Outputs:** time-to-baseline (TtB), HRV delta, pupil size variability, tremor reduction, subjective clarity.

The question isn’t “does a protocol help?” It’s “is our **closed loop** stable, fast, and robust to disturbance?”

## 2) Design implications that usually get missed

**A. Minimize loop latency (speed beats everything)**
- Goal: **stimulus→sensor→decision→actuation→physio change** in < 10 s perceived, < 60 s full.
- Product moves: pre-cache audio, one-tap start, immediate haptic metronome, defer heavy analytics until after the minute.

**B. Gain scheduling (match protocol to arousal regime)**
- Low arousal: slightly **up-regulating** cadence (e.g., 4-in/4-out + extero cues).
- High arousal: **down-regulating** (e.g., 4-in/6-out or 4-7-8), minimal cognitive load, darker screen.
- Extremely high: **feedforward** pattern (two sighs + 6 slow cycles) before feedback catches up.
- Encode this as a **state machine**; don’t let users pick—let the controller pick.
