### Propofol “Arm-to-Brain” Lag Time — 10 Ground-Level Tips for CRNAs  

1. **Keep a 30-second mental stopwatch.**  
   Start counting as you push propofol. Loss of verbal response or eyelash reflex in ≤15 s = *fast* circulation (young athlete, anxious tachy-cardic). 20-30 s = *typical*. >30 s flags low flow (hypovolemia, elderly, cardiac failure).  One glance gives you an instant cardiac-output clue before the first NIBP cycle finishes. citeturn0search0  

2. **Match dosage to lag category.**  
   Fast arm-brain time? Expect blood levels to peak higher—consider 10-20 % dose trim or slower titration. Slow time? You may need the full weight-based dose *plus* a watchful eye for hypotension once it finally hits.  

3. **Cross-check with your own pulse.**  
   While counting seconds, note your HR. A big gap (your HR > patient HR + 15 bpm) often means you’re sympathetically ramped and might over-estimate how “slow” the patient looks. One nasal inhale + long exhale steadies both you *and* your clinical judgment.  

4. **Log the seconds in the chart (it fits under “induction notes”).**  
   Writing “propofol LOC = 18 s” takes no extra clicks but builds a running database of each patient’s circulation status—gold for QI projects and teaching moments with residents.  

5. **Use repeat injections as trending data.**  
   If a supplemental bolus 20 min later takes longer to work, think rising vasodilation or blood loss; if it hits faster, suspect catecholamine surge (pain, light anesthesia).  

6. **Pediatrics: expect “blink-and-gone.”**  
   Healthy kids (<10 y) often lose reflexes in 8–12 s—tiny vessels, high CO. Have the mask or tube ready *before* you inject to avoid chasing apnea. citeturn0search4  

7. **Athletes vs. fragile hearts.**  
   Marathoner with resting HR 50? Lag ≈10–12 s but blood pressure may dive once SVR drops—be ready with phenylephrine. EF 30 % heart-failure patient? Lag can stretch past 30 s; give fractional doses (20 mg every 10 s) and preload fluids/pressors. citeturn0search2  

8. **Teach residents the “silent vital sign.”**  
   Have them call out lag time aloud. It trains vigilance and links pharmacology to real hemodynamics better than any slide deck.  

9. **Pair with waveform capnography.**  
   During hand-ventilation, watch for rising EtCO₂ while noting lag seconds. Slow EtCO₂ rise *and* slow lag screams low flow—act before the first hypotensive alarm.
