Here are a few **practical frameworks** that organizations, labs, and high-cognition teams use to catalogue *compounding insights* without turning it into bureaucracy:

### **1. Insight Log (a.k.a. “Learning Ledger”)**
- Treat each “aha” as a micro-asset.  
- One line per entry:  
  **Date — Trigger — Reframe — Consequence.**  
  Example: *2025-03-14 | Jason Fields prototype | Awareness is trainable in 60s windows | Led to NeuroMinute UX rev.*  
- Review monthly; tag by domain (clinical, business, personal).  
- Over time, patterns of repetition or acceleration become visible — your personal *metacognition telemetry.*

---

### **2. The Double-Loop Journal**
Chris Argyris’ organizational-learning model:  
- **Loop 1:** What did I do and what happened?  
- **Loop 2:** What assumption shifted?  
Write both after each project or meeting. It captures not just learning but *the learning about learning*—the compounding layer.

---

### **3. Zettelkasten / Digital Garden**
Use tools like Notion, Obsidian, or Roam.  
- Each insight becomes an atomic “card.”  
- Link it to other notes by *why* it matters.  
- Over time, you see the *graph of thought*—clusters where insights reinforce one another (your cognitive network map).

---

### **4. Aha Density Chart**
What we just built: a **temporal map** showing the *rate* of insight over time.  
- X-axis = time  
- Y-axis = number of distinct “aha” moments  
- Optional color codes for domain (strategy, physiology, narrative).  
- You can compare phases—e.g., “Mid-2024 insight rate 3× baseline.”  
This helps you see which environments or collaborations amplify insight production.

---

### **5. Learning Sprints**
Borrowed from agile design:
- Each sprint ends with a *Learning Review*.  
- Team lists “Top 3 new truths” and how they’ll change behavior.  
- Insights become backlog items for the next iteration.

---

### **6. Periodic Meta-Narrative**
Every quarter, write a one-page “What Changed in My Thinking.”  
This narrative view captures the *trajectory* rather than the data points — it’s how thinkers like John Vervaeke and Iain McGilchrist would map cognition over time.
