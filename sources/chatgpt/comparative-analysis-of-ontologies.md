Part I: The Cognitive Atlas: An Ontology for Cognitive    Neuroscience
The field of cognitive neuroscience is animated by a grand ambition: to map the complex functions of the mind onto the intricate structures of the brain. However, this endeavor rests on a foundational challenge—the lack of a shared, systematic framework for defining the very "mental processes" being mapped. Without a common vocabulary, cumulative progress is hindered, as researchers may use different terms for the same concept or the same term for different concepts, making the integration of knowledge from an exploding number of studies exceptionally difficult. It is this challenge that prompted the development of the Cognitive Atlas project.
1.1 The Genesis and Ambition of the Cognitive Atlas
Led by Professor Russell Poldrack at Stanford University, the Cognitive Atlas is an open, collaborative knowledge-building project designed to characterize the current state of thought in cognitive science. Its primary goal is to provide a knowledge base, or ontology, that offers a more systematic representation of the mental entities—the "parts" of the mind—that are the targets of neuroscientific investigation. By creating a shared resource to support the annotation of data and improve discourse within the community, the project aims to build a solid foundation for mapping mental processes to brain function.
The project's philosophy is rooted in the recognition that before one can answer the question of where in the brain a process occurs, one must first agree on what that process is. The Atlas is therefore not merely a repository of facts but an active tool designed to formalize the relationships between mental concepts and the experimental tasks used to manipulate and measure them.
1.2 Deconstructing the Ontology: Core Components
The Cognitive Atlas is structured around several key components that, together, form a dynamic and interconnected knowledge base.
Mental Concepts
At the heart of the Atlas are Mental Concepts, which represent the fundamental psychological constructs under investigation. The database includes over 900 concepts, ranging from broad, high-level categories such as "attention," "emotion," and "memory" to highly specific cognitive processes like "abductive reasoning" or "acoustic phonetic processing".
