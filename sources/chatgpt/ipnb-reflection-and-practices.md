The statement captures a real psychological dynamic, but calling it “one of the most intoxicating drugs” is poetic exaggeration.

When we first sample a higher standard of living—a nicer home, business-class travel, gourmet dining—it triggers a burst of novelty-driven dopamine much like other rewarding experiences. Neuroimaging studies show that status cues and luxury signals activate the brain’s reward circuitry (ventral striatum, medial prefrontal cortex) in ways comparable to food and some psychoactive substances. 

Yet the effect is usually transient. Hedonic adaptation sets in quickly: we grow accustomed to the new baseline, the dopamine response fades, and the “high” becomes expectation. At the same time, social-comparison mechanisms keep raising the reference point, so the gap between what we have and what we now crave can widen—fueling further consumption rather than lasting satisfaction. This is why economists speak of positional or “keeping-up-with-the-Joneses” goods, and psychologists warn of the “hedonic treadmill.”

There’s also a regulatory cost. Research on materialism shows that frequent upward comparison correlates with lower life satisfaction and higher anxiety. In Interpersonal Neurobiology terms, chasing external status can crowd out integration: attention narrows to acquisitive goals, limbic reward loops dominate, and resonance with self, others, and the present moment diminishes.

So yes, the taste can feel intoxicating—especially if it temporarily soothes insecurity or signals social belonging—but unlike many drugs it rarely delivers a durable sense of well-being. Lasting fulfillment tends to come when novelty is integrated with meaning, relationships, and purpose rather than pursued for its own sake.
