You’re using “R²” in a psychologically honest way, but it’s worth tightening the concept.

R² is a statement about predictability, not deservingness. A low-ish R² can coexist with a large, real causal effect of your actions—especially in high-variance domains where noise dominates any single realized outcome. The cleanest reconciliation is: you can take credit for the policy (your repeatable decision and effort algorithm) without claiming ownership of the specific draw of the dice.

Deep dive.

1) What R² would even mean for “a life”
In regression terms, the closest analogue is: “Across many comparable episodes, how much of the variance in outcomes is explained by my controllable inputs?”

There’s a useful identity here (law of total variance):

Var(Y) = Var(E[Y | A]) + E[Var(Y | A)]

Y = results/outcomes  
A = your action-policy (effort, learning cadence, risk posture, who you ask for help, how you respond under pressure)

The first term, Var(E[Y | A]), is the part of outcome variance that is systematically driven by your policy (your “agency component”). The second term is irreducible noise (everything that still varies even when you hold your policy constant: timing, other people, random shocks, hidden constraints, base-rate volatility).

In a noisy environment, the second term stays big. That pushes any estimated R² down even if your policy is excellent.

2) Why your “0.3” intuition is not crazy, but also not the main point
In medicine, entrepreneurship, and complex human systems generally, a large fraction of variance comes from factors you don’t control. So an “agency R²” that feels like 0.1–0.4 is often psychologically and operationally plausible.

But the key is this: R² is not the same as “percent credit.” It’s “percent variance explained.” Those are different moral and causal categories.

A vivid example: if outcomes have fat tails (rare, consequential wins/losses), a policy that shifts your probability of landing in the right tail can be profoundly important while still explaining only a modest slice of total variance. Your actions change the distribution more than they “determine the point outcome.”

3) The specific trap in “I can’t give myself credit for the results”
This sentence often smuggles in an all-or-nothing ethic: either you caused it, or you didn’t. Real causality is almost never like that.

Most meaningful achievements are “necessary-but-not-sufficient” structures:

Your actions: often necessary (without them, probability collapses)  
Context/luck/others: often necessary (without them, probability collapses)

When multiple causes are jointly necessary, it becomes incoherent to give yourself zero credit while also claiming your consistent effort mattered. If your work and curiosity reliably raised the odds, then part of the result is legitimately attributable to you—just not all of it.
