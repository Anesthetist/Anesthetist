# Answering 5 Key Equity Questions for Early Startup Employees

As a founder, it’s important to address the critical questions early employees may have about their equity and the company’s financial health. Below are five key questions and explanations to help you provide clarity and transparency to your team, backed by best practices and examples.

## 1. What is the total **liquidation preference** on the company?

**Liquidation preference** is the amount that investors (preferred shareholders) must be paid back before common stock (employees’ shares or options) receive any proceeds in an exit (acquisition or IPO). In simple terms, **investors get paid first**【22†L64-L72】【22†L99-L103】. The total liquidation preference is typically equal to all the money the company has raised to date (assuming a standard 1× non-participating preference for each round), though it could be higher if any investors negotiated a higher multiple or other special terms【12†L49-L57】. 

- **Why it matters:** If your startup has raised a large amount of capital, the company must *at least* return that amount in a sale before common stockholders (like employees) get anything. For example, if investors put in a total of $200 million, and the company sells for $200 million, all those proceeds would go to satisfy the investors’ preferences – meaning employees would likely get **$0** from the sale【22†L99-L103】. Even a headline-making “$200M exit” can leave employees empty-handed if the liquidation preference “overhang” equals or exceeds the sale price【22†L99-L103】. In other words, **the company must clear the preference stack for your equity to have value**.

- **How to find out:** As CEO, you should know (and be willing to share) the total *liquidation preference* outstanding. This is usually the sum of all dollars invested so far (for 1× prefs) or more if there are compounded or multiple preferences. If your company has raised $X million in total, that’s a baseline for the liquidation preference. Early employees asking this question want to understand the minimum exit threshold needed before their shares have value. If a company isn’t willing to share this information, it’s considered a **red flag** (it might imply the equity could end up worthless)【12†L49-L57】. Transparency here helps set realistic expectations.

- **Be transparent and clarify terms:** Ensure your team also understands whether your investors’ preferences are *non-participating* (most common, meaning investors either take their money back or convert to common) or *participating* (investors take their money back *and* also share in the remaining upside). Non-participating preferences mean that if the exit is high enough, investors will just convert to common and everyone shares pro-rata. But if the exit valuation is low relative to money raised, the liquidation preference ensures investors get their money back first, potentially leaving little or nothing for employees【22†L99-L103】【22†L149-L158】.
