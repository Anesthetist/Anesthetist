This disclosure relates to a closed-loop neurotechnology system for stress regulation. The system continuously monitors a user’s physiological and behavioral signals, detects acute stress-related changes, and delivers tailored interventions in real-time. It consists of two interconnected components: (1) a **biometric-triggered intervention algorithm** that automatically initiates calming micro-practices based on sensor data, and (2) a **neuro-timing calibration engine** that dynamically adjusts the timing, intensity, and modality of these interventions to the individual’s neurophysiological profile. Together, these components form a feedback loop comprising sensors, signal processing, an influencing algorithm for feedback decision, and actuators that deliver the intervention【27†L58-L62】. The result is an intelligent system that senses the user’s state, decides when and how to intervene, and learns from each interaction to optimize stress relief. This technology can be implemented as a method, system, or software, enabling broad patent claim coverage (e.g., method steps for monitoring and intervening, a wearable device system with processing modules, or a computer program product performing the intervention logic).

## Biometric-Triggered Intervention Algorithm  
**Physiological Signal Monitoring:** The system employs multiple sensors to capture real-time biomarkers of stress. These may include heart activity (for heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV)), respiratory patterns, galvanic skin response (electrodermal activity), and even facial micro-expressions or muscle tension cues. For example, a photoplethysmography or ECG sensor provides beat-to-beat heart data to compute HRV, a chest or abdominal sensor measures breathing rate and depth, a skin conductivity sensor measures perspiration, and a camera or wearable EMG can capture subtle facial cues (e.g., frowning or jaw tension). Collecting **multimodal data** provides a robust picture of the user’s autonomic state【38†L1-L4】. Notably, HRV is a validated indicator of stress: under stress, parasympathetic (vagal) activity tends to drop, reflected as a decrease in high-frequency HRV power【39†L192-L200】. This physiological fact underpins the algorithm’s use of HRV features as a trigger – a reduction in HRV or related metrics can reliably signal elevated stress【39†L200-L204】. Similarly, acute stress or anxiety often causes rapid, shallow breathing and increased electrodermal activity (sweating), which are captured by the respiration and GSR sensors.
