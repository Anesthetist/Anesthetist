## Background: Medicaid Financing and the Paragon Institute Report  
The Paragon Health Institute’s paper *“Addressing Medicaid Money Laundering: The Lack of Integrity with Medicaid Financing and the Need for Reform”* shines a spotlight on how states finance their share of Medicaid and alleges widespread use of questionable funding tactics【21†L149-L157】. In Medicaid’s joint federal-state funding model, states are supposed to contribute a portion of costs, but many have developed strategies to minimize their own spending by leveraging funds from healthcare providers or local entities. Paragon’s report characterizes these practices as *“Medicaid money laundering,”* suggesting that states “scheme so that the provider gets an enormous flow of federal dollars with no state cost exposure”【21†L149-L157】. In essence, states impose taxes or fees on hospitals and other providers and then route those funds back to the healthcare system as inflated Medicaid payments, which draw additional federal matching dollars【21†L203-L211】. This cycle allows states to shift a significant portion of Medicaid’s cost to federal taxpayers without proportionate state funding – a trend Paragon argues reflects a lack of financing integrity. The report calls for reforms to curb these practices, positing that tightening Medicaid funding rules would restore fiscal responsibility. This has sparked debate at the national level, as these financing mechanisms have become integral to how Medicaid is run in almost every state.

## Federal Medicaid Financing Practices and Integrity Concerns  
Over the past decades, nearly all states have come to rely on **provider-funded revenue schemes** to bolster Medicaid funding. These include provider taxes (also known as assessments or fees on hospitals, nursing facilities, managed care organizations, etc.), intergovernmental transfers (IGTs) from county or public hospitals, and other creative arrangements. Such mechanisms are legal and have been used since the 1980s, but critics argue they are a loophole allowing states to avoid putting up genuine state funds【21†L165-L173】【21†L199-L207】. The basic pattern is that a state levies a tax or fee on healthcare providers, which is **“broad-based”** (applies to many providers) and not explicitly linked to Medicaid patient volume to satisfy federal rules. The revenue from this tax is then used as the state’s Medicaid contribution, and it gets **federally matched** (often at $1 to $1 or more, depending on the state’s match rate). The state can then **return much of that money to those providers through higher Medicaid reimbursement rates or supplemental payments**, effectively *laundering* the funds: providers get their tax dollars back (plus extra federal funds), and the state avoids using as much general budget money【21†L203-L211】【21†L169-L177】.
