## 1. Does anesthesia mostly “take out the left hemisphere”?

Short version:  
**No single anesthetic just turns off “the left hemisphere,”** but a lot of what *we* care about clinically and subjectively **looks like left-coded functions going offline first**:

- Inner speech and narrative
- Sequential reasoning and task focus
- Voluntary motor planning
- Precise time-tracking and working memory
- Propositional language

All of those are strongly left-lateralized in McGilchrist’s frame.

Mechanistically, though, what modern data actually shows is:

- **Frontoparietal feedback connectivity collapses under general anesthesia** (both GABAergic and volatile), especially the top-down frontal → parietal direction. citeturn1search1turn1search25  
- **Transmodal / associative networks (frontoparietal + DMN) lose their special status first**, while unimodal sensory networks (vision, somatomotor) are relatively preserved until deeper levels. citeturn1search0turn1search4turn1search28  
- **Thalamocortical loops are disrupted**, especially between medial prefrontal cortex and thalamus, which is tightly tied to the loss and recovery of consciousness. citeturn1search16turn1search24turn1search13  

Those networks are *bilateral*, but they’re exactly the ones McGilchrist would say are crucial for the **“world as living field”** (right style) and the **“graspable problem space”** (left style). Anesthetics are hitting the *bridgework* and the *control loops* more than a single side.

So your intuition—*“it feels like mostly the left”*—isn’t wrong experientially, but it’s more like:

> **Stage 1:** Left-hemisphere *style* (narrative, sequential, verbal, goal-maintaining) fails in a very salient way.  
> **Stage 2:** The big bridging networks (thalamus ↔ cortex, fronto-parietal ↔ DMN, interhemispheric coherence) disintegrate.  
> **Stage 3:** What’s left is local “islands” of activity (sensory, limbic, brainstem) without a unified field.
