# Trauma-Informed Design

Everything SRL builds is trauma-informed. Not as an add-on. Not as a sensitivity layer applied after the fact. As a foundational design principle that shapes every script, every instruction, every interaction from the ground up.

## Why This Is Non-Negotiable

These are deeply embodying techniques. Breathing practices bring people INTO their bodies — and for people carrying trauma, the body is the last place they want to be. The body holds what the mind has tried to forget ([[van-der-kolk-2014-body-keeps-score]]). Directing attention inward can:

- Trigger flashbacks or body memories
- Activate the dorsal vagal shutdown (freeze/dissociation) instead of the ventral vagal calm we're targeting
- Produce panic when the practitioner encounters unfamiliar body sensations
- Reactivate somatic trauma patterns (breath holding, chest tightening, throat constriction)

**People can't even close their eyes and feel comfortable.** Eye closure removes visual safety cues, activates neuroception of threat ([[polyvagal-theory]]), and eliminates the social engagement system's primary input (seeing other faces). For someone with trauma, closing their eyes in a room full of people — or alone — can feel dangerous.

CRNAs specifically carry:
- Cumulative exposure to patient suffering, death, and near-misses
- The "second victim" phenomenon from adverse events
- Moral injury from systemic pressures
- The interoceptive suppression damage from medical training itself ([[interoceptive-suppression-burnout-root]])
- Personal trauma (grief, loss, relationship dissolution) that doesn't pause for shift work

## The Seven Principles of Trauma-Informed Somnistics Design

### 1. Invitation, Never Command

Every instruction is an invitation. The practitioner always has permission to do less, do something different, or do nothing.

| Never Say | Always Say |
|-----------|-----------|
| "Close your eyes" | "If comfortable, allow your eyes to soften — or keep a soft gaze downward" |
| "Take a deep breath" | "Notice your next breath, however it comes" |
| "Relax your body" | "If there are areas willing to soften, allow that" |
| "Feel your heartbeat" | "See if you can notice any rhythm in your body" |
| "Let go of tension" | "Notice where you're holding — that's information, not a problem" |
| "You should feel calm now" | "Notice whatever you notice. There's no wrong experience" |

### 2. Choice and Agency in Every Session

The practitioner controls the experience. They choose:
- **Eyes:** Open, soft gaze, half-closed, or closed — their choice, every session
- **Anchor:** Breath, feet on floor, hands on thighs, ambient sound — multiple options always offered ([[polyanchora]])
- **Depth:** The 8-layer VagalBeats slider means they can stay at Layer 1 forever if that's what's safe
- **Duration:** "You can pause or stop at any time" appears in every session script
- **Posture:** Standing, sitting, walking — never prescribed

### 3. Name Sensation, Not Emotion

Physiological labeling is safer than emotional labeling for trauma survivors:
- "I notice tightness in my chest" (observable, contained, external to identity)
- NOT "I feel anxious" (identity-level, potentially overwhelming, may trigger shame)

This is also culturally adaptive (sensation words translate cleanly) and more interoceptively accurate (body precedes label). See [[script-design-invitational-language]] for the full design system.

### 4. Normalize Difficulty

"If your attention drifts, that IS the practice — noticing is the skill."
"If this feels uncomfortable, that's useful information."
"There is no wrong experience in this practice."

Trauma survivors expect to fail at body-based practices because their body has been a source of distress. Normalizing difficulty prevents the shame spiral that causes abandonment.

### 5. Titrate Intensity

Never go from zero to deep interoceptive work. The [[anterocept]] progression exists specifically for trauma safety:

- **Domain 1:** External, simple, grounding — feet on floor, air temperature, hand position. Safe because it's peripheral, not visceral.
- **Domain 2:** Slowly layering in deeper signals — breath rhythm, heartbeat. Only after Domain 1 is stable.
- **Domain 3:** Integrated internal-external awareness. Only after months of safe practice.

The [[titration-to-effect]] principle: never more intensity than the nervous system can integrate. The "spicy" sensation in the Anesthetist's Breath is the body's own dose-limiting signal.

### 6. Provide Exit Ramps

Every session has explicit permission to stop:
- "You can pause or open your eyes at any time"
- "If anything feels too intense, simply return to noticing your feet on the floor"
- "The ground is always available as an anchor"

Grounding techniques (feet on floor, hands on solid surface, ambient sound awareness) are taught FIRST — before any interoceptive work — so the practitioner always has a safe exit ramp.

### 7. Understand That the Body Remembers

Breathing practices can surface stored somatic experiences. This is not a side effect — it's the mechanism ([[van-der-kolk-2014-body-keeps-score]]). But in a self-guided CE course without a therapist present, the design must ensure this surfacing stays within the window of tolerance:

- Keep practices short (60 seconds = natural containment)
- Keep practices structured (the protocol provides scaffolding)
- Keep practices choice-based (the practitioner controls depth)
- Recommend professional support for anyone who experiences persistent distress
- Never promise that the practice will resolve trauma — it supports regulation, not therapy

## Key Literature and Books

### Foundational Texts

**David Treleaven — *Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness* (2018, W.W. Norton)**
The definitive guide for adapting mindfulness practices for trauma survivors. Treleaven provides specific modifications for every standard mindfulness instruction. Key contribution: the "window of tolerance" framework (Siegel/Ogden) applied to meditation — keeping the practitioner in the zone between hyperarousal and hypoarousal.

**David Emerson & Elizabeth Hopper — *Overcoming Trauma through Yoga* (2011, North Atlantic Books)**
Developed with Bessel van der Kolk at the Trauma Center. The first systematic adaptation of yoga for PTSD. Key principle: invitational language replaces authoritative instruction. "You might try..." instead of "Now do..." Provides the linguistic template that SRL's script design system draws from.

**Bessel van der Kolk — *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014)**
Already in the vault ([[van-der-kolk-2014-body-keeps-score]]). Establishes that trauma is stored somatically and that body-based interventions are necessary (not just helpful) for trauma recovery. The theoretical foundation for why breathing practices both help AND carry risk.

**Peter Levine — *Waking the Tiger* (1997) / *In an Unspoken Voice* (2010)**
Somatic Experiencing (SE) framework. Trauma as incomplete autonomic response cycle (fight/flight that never completed). SE uses gentle interoceptive awareness to help the body complete the interrupted cycle. Directly informs the somnistics approach of slow, titrated body awareness.

**Pat Ogden — *Sensorimotor Psychotherapy* (2006/2015)**
The "window of tolerance" concept (with Dan Siegel). Body-based interventions must keep the client within the zone between sympathetic hyperarousal (panic, flashback) and dorsal vagal hypoarousal (shutdown, dissociation). The NeuroMinute 60-second format IS a natural window-of-tolerance container.

### Peer-Reviewed Research (from PubMed)

**Interoception + Trauma + Dissociation:**
- PMIDs 41485717, 41510299, 37144411, 36746344, 36788573, 34076566, 32721494 — emerging literature connecting interoceptive deficits to trauma-related dissociation. Key finding: trauma disrupts interoceptive accuracy, and restoring it requires careful, graduated exposure to body signals.

**Meditation-Related Adverse Experiences:**
- PMIDs 40133019, 34074221 — Willoughby Britton's (Brown University) research documenting that meditation can cause harm: increased anxiety, depersonalization, re-traumatization. Critical evidence that mindfulness is not inherently safe — design matters.

**Trauma-Informed Mindfulness:**
- PMID 24176784 — early framework paper linking trauma-informed care principles to mindfulness instruction.

**Trauma-Informed Embodied Practice:**
- PMIDs 41605633, 41489260, 41154278 — emerging literature on somatic/embodied approaches adapted for trauma populations.

**Trauma-Sensitive Yoga + PTSD:**
- PMID 28121466 — van der Kolk's trauma-sensitive yoga research for PTSD.

## How This Applies Across SRL

### CE Course
- Every session script follows the 7 principles
- Session 1 teaches grounding (exit ramp) before any breath technique
- Eye closure is NEVER required in any session
- Multiple anchor options in every session (not breath-only)
- Post-session reflection prompts are optional and self-paced

### Somnistics Certification
- Knowledge exam includes trauma-informed design principles
- Capability test does NOT require eye closure
- SCP-Instructors must demonstrate trauma-aware facilitation

### Pausality App
- No notification says "close your eyes"
- No notification uses directive language
- Every session has a visible "I'm done" exit button
- Biometric feedback pauses if HRV drops below safety threshold (dorsal vagal detection)

### Script Design System
- [[script-design-invitational-language]] IS the trauma-informed script system
- Sensory stacking adds one channel at a time (never overwhelm)
- Cultural neutrality prevents triggering religious/spiritual trauma
- "Thank you for this moment of settling" — affirms agency, doesn't evaluate performance

## Randy's Framing

These are very embodying techniques. They bring people into their bodies. For CRNAs who've been suppressing body signals their entire career, and carrying cumulative clinical trauma on top of that, the first encounter with their own nervous system can be intense. Trauma-informed design isn't a nice-to-have. It's the only responsible way to teach this.
